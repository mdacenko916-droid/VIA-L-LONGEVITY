Below is a comprehensive evidence-based reference module for the male hormonal panel, integrating guideline-based reference ranges, nutritional interventions, marker interactions, and red flags.

## TOTAL TESTOSTERONE (TT)

**Reference Ranges by Age and Why Standard Ranges Are Too Broad**

The Endocrine Society defines the lower limit of normal based on healthy, non-obese men aged 19-39 years: **total testosterone ≥264 ng/dL (9.2 nmol/L)** using CDC-certified assays.[1] However, a landmark individual participant data meta-analysis of 21,074 men found that the age-related decline in total testosterone is **negligible between ages 17-70** (change per SD increase in age: -0.27 nmol/L), with meaningful decline occurring primarily **after age 70** (-1.55 nmol/L per SD increase).[2] This challenges the common assumption of steady linear decline from the 30s onward.

The critical clinical point: **BMI has a larger effect on testosterone than age** — each SD increase in BMI reduces testosterone by 2.42 nmol/L (~70 ng/dL), compared to the negligible age effect before 70.[2] The ACP defines age-related low testosterone as a decline averaging **1.6% per year** beginning in the mid-30s, with syndromic low testosterone (≥3 sexual symptoms + TT [6]

**Low TT — Mechanistic Significance:**

Low testosterone impairs mitochondrial function, reduces erythropoiesis, increases visceral fat (which further converts testosterone to estradiol via aromatase), suppresses IGF-1, and feeds a vicious cycle: low T → visceral fat → more aromatase → lower free T → more fat.[7][8] Testosterone must be measured on **two separate mornings, fasting, before 10 AM**, in the absence of acute illness.[1][9]

**High TT:**

Endogenous elevation is rare — consider androgen-secreting tumors (adrenal or testicular), congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or exogenous androgen use. Exogenous testosterone suppresses LH/FSH to undetectable levels — this pattern is diagnostic of exogenous use.[8]

**Nutritional and Nutraceutical Interventions:**

- **Weight loss** is the single most impactful intervention — each kg/m² reduction in BMI increases testosterone proportionally[2][10]
- **Mediterranean diet** enhances testosterone synthesis via synergistic bioactive nutrients: oleocanthal inhibits COX-1/2, resveratrol reduces ROS, olive oil enhances testicular antioxidant capacity and restores the SREBP2 cholesterol pathway[11]
- **Ashwagandha (300-600 mg/day standardized root extract):** A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (1,706 patients) found ashwagandha significantly **increased testosterone in men (MD +57.43 ng/dL)** but not in women, with a significant gender difference. It also significantly reduced cortisol (SMD -1.18). A 12-month safety study confirmed sustained testosterone increases with no hepatotoxicity.[12][13][14]
- **Fenugreek seed extract (500 mg/day):** Systematic review identified it alongside ashwagandha as having the most consistent positive effects on testosterone in RCTs[13]
- **Zinc (15-30 mg/day):** Zinc supplementation in marginally deficient elderly men increased testosterone from 8.3 to 16.0 nmol/L (p=0.02) over 6 months. However, in zinc-sufficient men, ZMA supplementation showed **no significant effect**.[15][16][17]
- **DHA-enriched fish oil (860 mg DHA + 120 mg EPA/day):** RCT in overweight/obese men showed significantly increased total testosterone after 12 weeks, with changes positively associated with erythrocyte omega-3 content[18]
- **Vitamin D:** Despite observational associations, a well-designed RCT (n=100, 20,000 IU/week for 12 weeks) in men with sufficient baseline testosterone found **no effect on TT**[19]
- **Magnesium:** Positively associated with total testosterone in observational studies (InCHIANTI, n=399 older men); mechanistically, magnesium competes with testosterone for SHBG binding, potentially increasing bioavailable testosterone[20]

## FREE TESTOSTERONE (FT)

**Reference Ranges and Clinical Significance**

Free testosterone represents the **0.5-2% of total testosterone** that is unbound and biologically active.[8] A 2025 study — the largest to date (n=1,202 men) using equilibrium dialysis followed by LC-MS/MS — established the reference range for healthy non-obese men aged 18-39 as **184-749 pmol/L (5.3-21.6 ng/dL)**. Free testosterone declines at **-4.5 pmol/L per year**, and a BMI of 30 and 35 kg/m² reduces free testosterone by **14.4% and 22.2%**, respectively, compared to BMI 22.[21]

**When to Measure Free Testosterone:**

The Endocrine Society recommends measuring free testosterone when total testosterone is near the lower limit of normal or when conditions altering SHBG are present (obesity, diabetes, aging, thyroid disease, liver disease).[1] Free testosterone should be measured by **equilibrium dialysis or calculated using the Vermeulen equation** (total testosterone + SHBG + albumin) — direct analog-based free testosterone immunoassays are inaccurate and should not be used.[1][9]

**Nutritional Relevance:**

Interventions that lower SHBG (weight loss in obese men, reducing insulin resistance) increase free testosterone even without changing total testosterone. Conversely, interventions that raise SHBG (alcohol abstinence, healthy diet) may increase total testosterone while having a more modest effect on free testosterone.[3]

## SHBG (Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin)

**Reference Ranges and Determinants in Men**

Typical range: **10-70 nmol/L** in men (varies by assay and age). SHBG increases with age and is the primary reason free testosterone declines faster than total testosterone.[2][22]

**Nutritional Interventions to Modulate SHBG:**

Dietary factors regulate SHBG production at the hepatic level. A comprehensive review found that **monosaccharides (fructose, glucose) downregulate SHBG** via hepatic lipogenesis → increased liver fat → reduced SHBG transcription, while **dietary fiber, olive oil polyphenols, and coffee** upregulate SHBG production.[24][23] The clinical implication: reducing refined carbohydrates and fructose is the most direct nutritional lever for raising low SHBG in insulin-resistant men.

**Clinical Pitfall:** Low SHBG in obese/insulin-resistant men can mask true testosterone deficiency — total testosterone may appear "normal" while free testosterone is actually adequate, or conversely, total testosterone may appear low while free testosterone is normal. Always interpret total testosterone in the context of SHBG.[1][8]

## ESTRADIOL (E2) IN MEN

**Reference Ranges and Clinical Significance**

More than **80% of circulating estradiol in men** is derived from aromatization of testosterone.[25] Using LC-MS/MS (the gold standard), reference ranges for men are:

- **12-136 pmol/L (3.3-37 pg/mL)** in healthy men aged 18-50[26][27]
- **Normal reference: 10-50 pg/mL** per NHANES LC-MS/MS protocols[28]
- **2.5th percentile in healthy older men (age ~76): 28 pmol/L (~7.6 pg/mL)**[5]

Immunoassays systematically **overestimate** estradiol in men compared to LC-MS/MS — this is a critical interpretive consideration.[29][30]

**The Testosterone-to-Estradiol (T/E2) Ratio — Clinical Significance:**

A low T/E2 ratio reflects increased aromatase activity (typically from visceral adiposity) and has emerged as a clinically meaningful marker. In a study of 611 men with severe atherosclerosis, **low T/E2 ratio was independently associated with increased systemic inflammation** (CRP 2.81 vs. 1.22 µg/mL, p[31]

The landmark Finkelstein study (NEJM 2013) demonstrated that estradiol deficiency — not just androgen deficiency — is responsible for fat accumulation in hypogonadal men, while both androgens and estrogens regulate sexual function and lean mass.[25]

**Low E2 in Men:**

Estradiol is essential for male bone health, body composition, glucose metabolism, and vasomotor stability. Men with aromatase deficiency or estrogen receptor mutations develop osteoporosis, unfused epiphyses, and metabolic syndrome.[32][7]

**High E2 in Men:**

Elevated estradiol with gynecomastia requires workup for estrogen-secreting tumors (testicular Leydig/Sertoli cell tumors, adrenal tumors) and hCG-secreting tumors. The AUA recommends measuring estradiol in testosterone-deficient men with breast symptoms prior to testosterone therapy, and referring men with elevated baseline estradiol to endocrinology.[33]

**Nutritional Interventions to Optimize T/E2 Ratio:**

- **Weight loss** is the most effective intervention — visceral fat is the primary site of aromatase activity[31][35]
- **Cruciferous vegetables (DIM/I3C):** Shift estrogen metabolism toward protective 2-hydroxyestrogen metabolites; DIM is a competitive androgen receptor antagonist
- **Zinc (15-30 mg/day):** Inhibits aromatase activity in vitro
- **Grape seed extract / resveratrol:** Aromatase inhibition demonstrated in preclinical studies, but clinical evidence in men is limited

## DHEA-S (Dehydroepiandrosterone Sulfate)

**Age-Specific Reference Ranges in Men**

DHEA-S peaks at age 20-24 (~3,470 ng/mL in men) and then declines steadily — approximately **3% per year** — reaching ~670 ng/mL by age 70+.[36] By age 70-80, concentrations are approximately **20% of peak values**.[37] DHEA-S does not vary with diurnal rhythm or menstrual cycle, making it a stable adrenal marker.[36][38]

**Nutritional Interventions:**

No food directly raises DHEA-S. The Endocrine Society notes that while DHEA supplementation (50 mg/day) increases DHEA-S, free/total testosterone, and estradiol, it has **little reproducible beneficial effect** on sexual function, bone density, lipids, or glucose.[37] Interventions focus on adrenal support: adequate sleep, stress reduction, vitamin C (500-1,000 mg/day), B5 (500 mg/day), and ashwagandha (which reduced cortisol but also reduced DHEA-S in one RCT — suggesting HPA axis modulation rather than simple adrenal support).[12][41]

## LH (Luteinizing Hormone)

**Reference Ranges and Diagnostic Patterns**

Normal range in men: **1.5-9.3 mIU/mL** (varies by assay). LH is the critical marker for distinguishing **primary from secondary hypogonadism**.[1][9][33]

**Diagnostic Interpretation:**

- **Low testosterone + elevated LH/FSH:** **Primary hypogonadism** (testicular failure). Most common cause in younger men: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) — karyotyping indicated.[8]
- **Low testosterone + low/normal LH/FSH:** **Secondary (central) hypogonadism** — hypothalamic or pituitary cause. Measure prolactin; consider pituitary MRI.[8][9][42]
- **Rising LH with stable testosterone:** Compensated hypogonadism — the testes are working harder to maintain testosterone output. This pattern increases after age 70 and may predict future overt hypogonadism.[2]
- **Suppressed LH ([8]

The individual participant data meta-analysis found that LH was directly associated with age **only in men older than 70 years**, suggesting that reduced testosterone and increased LH concentrations may indicate impaired testicular function specifically after this age.[2]

**Nutritional Relevance:**

LH pulsatility is suppressed by energy deficit, excessive exercise, opioids, and chronic stress. Ashwagandha supplementation significantly increased LH in middle-aged men (p=0.002 vs. placebo) in one RCT, suggesting HPA-HPG axis modulation.[43]

## FSH (Follicle-Stimulating Hormone)

**Reference Ranges and Clinical Significance**

Normal range in men: **1.5-12.4 mIU/mL**. FSH in men primarily reflects **Sertoli cell function and spermatogenesis** rather than testosterone production.[1][9]

**Diagnostic Patterns:**

- **Elevated FSH (>12 mIU/mL) with normal testosterone:** Isolated spermatogenic failure — Sertoli cell damage with preserved Leydig cell function. Common after chemotherapy, radiation, or varicocele.
- **Elevated FSH + elevated LH + low testosterone:** Primary hypogonadism (both Sertoli and Leydig cell failure).[8][9]
- **Low FSH + low LH + low testosterone:** Central hypogonadism — pituitary or hypothalamic pathology.[42]
- **Very elevated FSH (>25-30 mIU/mL):** Severe testicular damage; in younger men, karyotype for Klinefelter syndrome.[8]

**Nutritional Relevance:**

FSH is less responsive to nutritional interventions than LH. Its primary clinical value in the male panel is as a marker of spermatogenic capacity and for distinguishing primary from secondary hypogonadism.

## KEY MARKER INTERACTIONS

- **Low TT + low/normal LH + elevated prolactin:** The prolactin-HPG axis cascade — prolactin suppresses GnRH → low LH → low testosterone. Treating the prolactin elevation (dopamine agonist or removing causative medication) restores the entire axis.[45][8][33]
- **Low TT + low SHBG + elevated E2 + high BMI:** The obesity-aromatase cycle — visceral fat converts testosterone to estradiol via aromatase, while low SHBG increases free estradiol availability. The T/E2 ratio is the most informative marker of this imbalance. Weight loss breaks the cycle.[31][7][35]
- **Normal TT + high SHBG = low free testosterone:** Symptoms of hypogonadism despite "normal" total testosterone. Common with aging, hyperthyroidism, liver disease, and anticonvulsant use. Calculate free testosterone using the Vermeulen equation.[1][8]
- **Low TT + low SHBG + insulin resistance:** SHBG is a marker of hepatic insulin sensitivity. Low SHBG with low testosterone suggests metabolic syndrome as the primary driver — insulin sensitization (weight loss, dietary modification) may restore both SHBG and testosterone.[24][23]
- **Rising LH + declining testosterone (after age 70):** Compensated → decompensated primary hypogonadism. The testes are failing despite maximal gonadotropin stimulation.[2][5]
- **Low DHEA-S + high cortisol + low testosterone:** Triple-axis depletion — chronic HPA activation suppresses both adrenal DHEA-S production and HPG axis function simultaneously.[12][41][37]

## RED FLAGS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE PHYSICIAN REFERRAL

The following findings are **outside nutritional territory** and require urgent medical evaluation:

- **Total testosterone [9][33]
- **Prolactin >100 µg/L:** Pituitary MRI mandatory; >250 µg/L is virtually diagnostic of prolactinoma[44][45]
- **Elevated hCG in a man:** Testicular germ cell tumor until proven otherwise — urgent testicular ultrasound[29][47][48]
- **Elevated estradiol + gynecomastia:** Workup for estrogen-secreting tumors (testicular Leydig/Sertoli cell, adrenal) — testicular ultrasound first, then adrenal imaging if normal[33][29][48]
- **Rapidly progressive gynecomastia, especially unilateral:** Exclude male breast cancer (mammography ± biopsy)[47][48]
- **Very elevated FSH/LH + low testosterone in men [8]
- **Suppressed LH/FSH to undetectable + high testosterone:** Exogenous androgen use — counsel on fertility implications (spermatogenesis suppression)[8]
- **Low testosterone + visual field defects, headaches, or multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies:** Sellar mass — urgent neurosurgical evaluation[8][9]
- **Polycythemia (hematocrit >54%) in a man on testosterone therapy:** Dose reduction or discontinuation required; risk of thromboembolic events[9][49]
