## 1. Bidirectional Relationship Between OSA and Testosterone — Can Nutrition Support Both Simultaneously?

**OSA and testosterone deficiency are bidirectionally linked, but the evidence strongly supports treating OSA first (or simultaneously) rather than relying on nutrition alone to restore testosterone.** In this patient (total testosterone 8.2 nmol/L, STOP-BANG 5, neck circumference 43 cm, witnessed apneas), the low testosterone is likely substantially driven by OSA-mediated mechanisms: each apneic event triggers sympathetic activation → cortisol spike → LH suppression → Leydig cell testosterone suppression, while sleep fragmentation disrupts the slow-wave sleep during which ~65% of daily testosterone is secreted.[1][2]

The critical question — whether CPAP restores testosterone — has **conflicting evidence**. A 2014 meta-analysis (232 men) and a 2019 meta-analysis (388 men) both found no significant change in total testosterone after CPAP.[3][4] However, a 2026 study of severely obese men with decompensated OSA (AHI >30) showed CPAP for 3 months **significantly improved total testosterone independently of BMI changes** (p = 0.009).[5] A key review notes that the conclusion that CPAP has no effect on testosterone is "premature because available studies are underpowered and better-quality studies suggest otherwise." CPAP also significantly improved erectile function and elevated FSH, LH, and testosterone in men with severe OSA and erectile dysfunction.[2][6]

**Clinical implication for this patient:** Nutrition and OSA treatment should proceed **simultaneously**, not sequentially. Nutritional interventions (Mediterranean diet, weight loss, anti-inflammatory foods) address the metabolic substrate (visceral fat → aromatase → estradiol, insulin resistance, inflammation) while CPAP addresses the proximate cause of sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxia. Waiting to "fix nutrition first" delays correction of the nightly hypoxic insults that are directly suppressing testosterone and activating the HPA axis.

## 3. Dietary Patterns — Mediterranean Diet, Macronutrient Timing, and OSA Severity

**The Mediterranean diet has the strongest evidence for reducing OSA severity, with benefits independent of weight loss and CPAP use.**

The **MIMOSA RCT** (180 patients with moderate-to-severe OSA) is the landmark trial: the Mediterranean diet group achieved an AHI reduction of **-24.7 events/h** and the Mediterranean lifestyle group **-27.3 events/h**, compared to -4.2 for standard care alone. Critically, these differences remained significant **after adjusting for body weight change** (p = 0.004 and 0.008), indicating benefits beyond weight loss alone.[21] The Mediterranean lifestyle intervention also significantly reduced hs-CRP and oxidative stress markers (urinary 8-iso-PGF2α), and these cardiometabolic benefits were sustained at 12-month follow-up (6 months post-intervention).[22][23]

A 2025 narrative review of 191 articles confirmed that greater Mediterranean diet adherence is associated with longer sleep duration, shorter sleep latency, higher sleep efficiency, and lower risk of insomnia and OSA, mediated through the tryptophan-serotonin-melatonin pathway, reduced systemic inflammation, enhanced gut microbiota diversity, and improved circadian rhythm regulation.[24]

**Macronutrient timing and sleep quality:**

A 2025 scoping review comparing Mediterranean diet and chrononutrition found that while 17/24 Mediterranean diet studies showed improved sleep quality, only 2/9 chrononutrition studies demonstrated benefit — suggesting **dietary quality matters more than meal timing** for sleep outcomes.[25] However, practical recommendations include:

- **Avoid large meals within 3 hours of bedtime** — gastroesophageal reflux worsens OSA and disrupts sleep architecture
- **Distribute protein across meals** (1.2-1.6 g/kg/day) — tryptophan from protein sources supports serotonin/melatonin synthesis
- **Evening carbohydrate quality matters** — complex carbohydrates (not refined) may facilitate tryptophan transport across the blood-brain barrier, supporting melatonin synthesis[26]
- **Low-carbohydrate diets** have shown benefits for OSA beyond weight reduction alone, potentially through reduced pharyngeal fat deposition and improved insulin sensitivity[27]

**For this patient (BMI 29, fasting glucose 5.9 mmol/L):** A Mediterranean diet with moderate carbohydrate restriction (targeting ~40% of calories from complex carbohydrates) addresses both the OSA and the impaired fasting glucose simultaneously, while providing the anti-inflammatory polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, and fiber that independently improve sleep quality.

## 5. Nutritional Role in HPA Axis Normalization — Cortisol Rhythm Restoration

**CPAP is the most potent intervention for HPA axis normalization in OSA.** A sham-controlled study showed OSA was associated with significantly higher 24-h cortisol levels, and 2 months of CPAP lowered cortisol to near-control levels.[32] A meta-analysis of 637 individuals confirmed CPAP significantly reduced plasma cortisol (SMD -0.28 in prospective studies, -0.39 in RCTs) along with SBP (-5.4 mmHg) and DBP (-3.3 mmHg).[33] Detailed deconvolution analysis showed untreated OSA caused markedly disordered ACTH and cortisol pulsatile secretion with prolonged tissue exposure to elevated hormone levels — all reversed by 3 months of CPAP.[34] CPAP also restored the flattened cortisol awakening response (CAR) within 3-6 months.[35]

**Nutritional strategies that support (but do not replace) HPA axis normalization:**

**Anti-inflammatory Mediterranean diet** reduces the inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α, hs-CRP) that independently activate the HPA axis. The MIMOSA trial demonstrated significant hs-CRP reduction with Mediterranean diet intervention in OSA patients, with sustained benefits at 12 months.[22][23]

**Specific nutrients supporting cortisol regulation:**

- **Magnesium (300-400 mg/day):** Modulates HPA axis reactivity; magnesium deficiency increases cortisol response to stress; enhances GABAergic inhibitory tone[7][26]
- **Omega-3 fatty acids (2-3 g EPA+DHA/day):** Reduce cortisol response to mental stress in RCTs; anti-inflammatory effects reduce NF-κB-driven HPA activation[19]
- **Vitamin C (500-1,000 mg/day):** The adrenal glands contain the highest vitamin C concentration in the body; supplementation attenuates cortisol response to acute stress
- **B vitamins (B5, B6, B12, folate):** Cofactors in neurotransmitter synthesis (serotonin, GABA) and adrenal steroidogenesis; deficiency impairs HPA axis regulation[26]

**What to avoid — interventions that worsen HPA dysregulation:**

- **High-dose caffeine** (>200 mg/day): Directly stimulates cortisol secretion via adenosine receptor antagonism
- **Alcohol:** Acutely elevates cortisol; chronically disrupts cortisol diurnal rhythm; relaxes upper airway muscles worsening OSA
- **Prolonged fasting or severe caloric restriction:** Activates the HPA axis as a starvation response; moderate caloric deficit (500 kcal/day) is preferred over aggressive restriction
- **High-glycemic-index foods:** Rapid glucose spikes and crashes trigger cortisol release as a counter-regulatory response
- **Excessive high-intensity exercise:** While moderate exercise reduces cortisol, prolonged high-intensity training (>60 min) acutely elevates cortisol; for this patient, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (150-300 min/week) is preferred initially
