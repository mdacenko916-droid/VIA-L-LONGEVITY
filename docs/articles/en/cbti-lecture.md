# Lecture: insomnia in perimenopause and CBT-I

*7 min read*

## The main causes of sleep problems

- **Hormonal fluctuations, night sweats and hot flashes, anxiety, stress, emotional strain.** The result: trouble falling asleep, frequent awakenings or waking too early.
- **Restless legs syndrome** — an unpleasant feeling in the legs that keeps you from falling asleep. Most often it develops against a background of iron deficiency.
- **Obstructive sleep apnea.** How it develops is often underestimated: falling estrogen levels, lower muscle tone in the airways, weaker throat tissues. How apnea shows up: light snoring, pauses in breathing, micro-awakenings at night, the feeling "I slept badly and I don't know why". The consequences are daytime fatigue and poorer cognitive function. Apnea plus insomnia is a comorbid condition: each makes the other worse.
- **A shift in circadian rhythms in perimenopause.** Circadian rhythms move. Sleep comes earlier, and so does waking. And a typical mistake follows: a woman wakes at 4–5 a.m. and thinks "I have insomnia". But in fact the body has changed its sleep timing — this isn't a disorder, it's the biological clock resetting.

## What the assessment should look like

What is sleep quality like, are there awakenings, early waking, is there snoring, are there pauses in breathing at night, is there an urge to move the legs. Perimenopause is a time when sleep problems have many causes, and one cause often hides another disorder and calls for a thorough assessment.

Studies have shown that treating anxiety and depression without addressing sleep is less effective, while better sleep automatically improves other symptoms too. It's important to look for apnea, iron deficiency, circadian shifts and combined disorders.

CBT for insomnia in perimenopause is the gold standard of insomnia treatment. According to the guidelines of international societies, the main CBT-I techniques are as follows.

## 1. Restricting time in bed

The aim of this technique is to match time in bed to the body's real need for sleep, strengthen the natural sleep pressure and make sleep more solid and less broken.

For example, if a person spends 8 hours in bed but actually sleeps only 5.5–6, the therapist may adjust time in bed to the actual sleep duration. A steady wake-up time is also set, for example 6:00, regardless of how well the person slept.

As sleep efficiency improves, time in bed is gradually increased — often in 15-minute steps. Decisions to increase or reduce the time are based on the sleep diary and the patient's individual response.

**Important:** this doesn't mean depriving yourself of sleep on your own. Standard CBT-I protocols usually don't set time in bed below 5 hours. The method needs individual tailoring and professional supervision. For some patients a gentler version of the technique is used — sleep compression.

## 2. The sleep diary

One of the most important CBT-I tools is the sleep diary. Every day the patient records:

- the time they went to bed;
- roughly how long it took to fall asleep;
- the number and length of night awakenings;
- the time of final waking and getting out of bed;
- total sleep time;
- daytime naps;
- caffeine and alcohol.

These notes help to see the real picture of sleep, spot patterns and judge how well therapy is working.

## 3. Why not rely entirely on sleep trackers?

Smartwatches and other consumer devices can be useful for following general sleep trends, but they have limits. They don't always accurately detect the moment of falling asleep, night awakenings and especially individual sleep stages. So their readings can't be treated as a full replacement for a clinical assessment or a sleep diary.

There's another important point: constant tracking can increase anxiety and create an excessive focus on sleep quality. There's even a special term for it — **orthosomnia**. It's an excessive fixation on achieving perfect sleep numbers, when a person starts judging their rest mainly by the figures on a screen. Paradoxically, this behaviour can increase anxiety and make insomnia worse.

## 4. Working with thoughts and beliefs

People with insomnia often have anxious, catastrophic thoughts:

- "If I don't fall asleep tonight, I won't be able to work properly tomorrow."
- "If I sleep only four hours again, it will ruin my health."
- "I must fall asleep right now, or tomorrow everything will go wrong."

In CBT-I the patient learns to test such thoughts for realism, look for evidence, reduce catastrophising and build a more flexible attitude to sleep. These questions are used:

- What evidence supports my thought?
- How realistic is the worst-case scenario?
- Will I be able to cope with this situation?
- What would I tell a friend if she were in this situation?

The main goal is to reduce the inner pressure of "I have to fall asleep". The harder a person tries to force sleep, the more their physical and emotional arousal can rise — and that, in turn, gets in the way of sleep arriving naturally.

## 5. Relaxation and breathing techniques

Therapy also uses:

- breathing techniques;
- progressive muscle relaxation;
- visualisation;
- methods for lowering physical arousal.

They help reduce tension and learn to regulate the body's stress response better. But it's important to understand: on their own, relaxation techniques usually aren't enough to treat chronic insomnia. They work as a supporting part of comprehensive CBT-I.

## 6. Mindfulness

Another important component is mindfulness practice. It helps you learn to notice your own thoughts without getting emotionally caught up in them, and to gently bring your attention back to the present moment. Regular practice is recommended, for example 5 minutes a day — preferably during the day, not only when you have trouble falling asleep.

Gradually a person learns:

- not to cling to anxious thoughts;
- not to spin out negative scenarios;
- not to try to control every aspect of sleep;
- to let go of night-time rumination more easily.

The goal isn't to stop the flow of thoughts completely, but to learn not to react to every thought as a threat.

## 7. When does CBT-I need an additional approach?

If insomnia comes together with restless legs syndrome, obstructive sleep apnea, marked anxiety or depression, it's important to identify and treat these co-existing conditions. CBT-I can work even when other conditions are present, but in such cases a comprehensive approach with the right specialists is often needed. In some cases a doctor may prescribe medication for the co-existing conditions or for the sleep problems. Sometimes a combined approach is used — CBT-I together with medication.

## How effective is CBT-I?

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, recommended by leading international professional organisations. It has a strong evidence base and gives clinically meaningful improvement in sleep for a large share of patients. Across studies, roughly 60–70% of patients show clinically meaningful improvement, although results depend on the group, the therapy protocol and how outcomes are measured.

The most important components of therapy:

- stimulus control;
- restriction or compression of time in bed;
- working with anxiety around sleep;
- cognitive restructuring;
- relaxation techniques;
- mindfulness practices;
- following the recommendations consistently.

## Changing your attitude to sleep

And perhaps one of the most important points is the need to change your very attitude to sleep. Sleep can't be controlled by willpower. Trying too hard to make yourself fall asleep often only makes insomnia worse. So it matters to develop a more flexible attitude to sleep, reduce catastrophising and let go of the belief that every night has to be perfect.

## Consistency is the key to success

It's important to follow the recommendations regularly, keep to a routine and use the therapeutic techniques. It's precisely being systematic and consistent that leads to a lasting result. Following the recommendations irregularly can make therapy less effective.

Insomnia is a real and serious problem. But it can be treated. The most effective approach is:

- a calm, compassionate attitude to yourself;
- less anxiety around sleep;
- sleep hygiene;
- professional cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I);
- consistent, regular use of the therapeutic techniques.
