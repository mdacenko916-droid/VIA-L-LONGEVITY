# Why sleep gets worse after 40 and what to do about it

*5 min read*

You used to fall asleep in ten minutes; now you lie awake for an hour. Or you drift off easily, but at three in the morning your eyes are open and your thoughts won't let you sleep again. Sleep is one of the first things to change in the transition. And one of the most rewarding: here, habits work better than pills.

## What really changes

To be honest: part of the decline in sleep over the years is simply age. But the transition has its own signs.

**It is harder to fall asleep.** Progesterone feeds the brain's natural "calming system". When there is less of it, falling asleep becomes harder.

**Your mind won't switch off.** At night it is as if someone is holding a finger on the button — thoughts spin, the body is tired, but sleep does not come. This happens even without any hot flashes.

**Hot flashes break up the night.** Most night-time hot flashes coincide with waking up.

**The evening won't let go.** A disrupted daily stress rhythm keeps the body from "winding down" by night — hence waking at 3–4 am.

If you wear a ring or watch: they show total sleep, falling asleep and the number of awakenings reliably. "Deep" and "REM" sleep, however, are only approximate; treat them as a hint, not a fact.

## What works best

**A wake-up anchor.** Get up at the same time all seven days, weekends included, within half an hour. Regularity matters more than how long you sleep. And get 10–15 minutes of daylight in the first hour after waking — outdoors, even on a cloudy day.

**Bed is for sleep only.** Don't lie awake: if you can't sleep for more than 15–20 minutes, get up and come back when you feel sleepy. Don't work or scroll your phone in bed, and don't look at the clock at night.

**A cool, dark bedroom.** Around 16–19 °C, breathable fabrics, clothing in layers. This reduces awakenings from hot flashes.

**A warm, dim evening.** For 30–60 minutes before bed — soft warm light and no screens.

## What to remove — this matters more than what to add

- **Alcohol in the evening.** It is a "false friend": it helps you fall asleep but breaks up the second half of the night and makes hot flashes worse — even in small amounts.
- **Late coffee.** Coffee and strong tea only before 2 pm.
- **A late dinner.** Your last heavy meal — three hours before bed.

Daytime movement helps sleep — but evening alcohol cancels that benefit.

## What about supplements?

Their effect is modest. **Magnesium** in the evening has weak evidence; it makes sense if you get little from food (pumpkin seeds, leafy greens, pulses). **Melatonin** mainly helps those whose sleep is already disrupted and depends on when it is taken — discuss it with a specialist. Valerian is not supported by evidence, and we do not recommend adaptogens such as ashwagandha: there are risks for the liver and thyroid.

## When to see a doctor

- Snoring with pauses or a feeling of choking during sleep, unrefreshing sleep despite enough hours, falling asleep during the day — especially at the wheel. After menopause, breathing problems during sleep are common and often missed.
- An irresistible urge to move your legs in the evening that eases with movement — it is worth checking your iron stores (ferritin).
- Poor sleep for more than three months despite all of the above.
- Severe hot flashes break up the night — treating them improves sleep too.

## What to try this week

- Get up at the same time all seven days.
- Don't lie awake: if you can't sleep, get up and come back when you feel sleepy.
- Coffee before 2 pm, an evening without alcohol.
