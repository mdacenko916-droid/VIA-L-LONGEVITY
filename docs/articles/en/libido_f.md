# Desire and intimacy in the transition: what changes and how to get comfort back

*5 min read*

Desire comes less often, intimacy is sometimes unpleasant or painful, and it feels awkward to talk about even with a doctor. You are not alone: this is one of the most common changes in the transition — and one of the most solvable.

## What changes with desire

In the transition, **spontaneous** desire — the kind that arises "by itself" — often fades. This is not a fault. For many women desire is **responsive**: it comes during intimacy rather than before it — and that is a healthy pattern.

Desire is affected by more than hormones. The biggest obstacles are:

- **tiredness and lack of sleep** — when there is no energy, there is no desire either;
- **mood and stress**;
- **the relationship** — what happens between people, not just in the body;
- **medication** — for example, some antidepressants lower desire; if you suspect this, discuss it with your doctor;
- **dryness and discomfort** — more on these below.

## Dryness and discomfort: no need to put up with it

Dryness, burning, pain during intimacy, frequent urges or recurring cystitis affect half of women after menopause or more. Yet they are rarely talked about: only about half tell their doctor, and most do not know this can be dealt with.

It is important to know: unlike hot flashes, this **does not go away on its own** and tends to get worse over time. But it responds well to help. And one more thing: pain leads to avoiding intimacy, and avoidance leads to less desire. Removing the discomfort often means getting desire back too.

## What you can start right away, without a prescription

**A moisturiser and a lubricant are different things.** They are often confused:

- a **vaginal moisturiser** is used regularly, 2–3 times a week, regardless of intimacy — it keeps moisture in the tissues;
- a **lubricant** is used only during intimacy, to reduce friction. Water-based or silicone, without fragrance or "warming" additives.

Regularity matters as much as the product itself: "expensive" does not mean "effective".

**Pelvic floor exercises** and regular intimacy or stimulation support blood flow and comfort.

**Remove irritants:** intimate soaps and gels, douching, scented products, tight synthetic underwear. Drink enough water, don't smoke.

## What a doctor can offer

If a moisturiser and lubricant are not enough, it is worth talking to a gynaecologist. There are local treatments, including low-dose oestrogen: they act where they are applied, are barely absorbed into the blood and, according to many studies, noticeably reduce both dryness and urinary symptoms. There are non-hormonal options too. Which one suits you is for a doctor to decide.

## What doesn't help

- **Supplements "for libido"** and "for moisture from within" — there is no evidence.
- **"Laser rejuvenation"** and radiofrequency procedures heavily marketed by clinics: in studies comparing them with a sham procedure, there was no difference. They are expensive and unproven.

## When to see a doctor

- **Any bleeding or spotting after menopause** — even a drop, even after intimacy. Most often the cause is harmless, but you should always get it checked.
- Severe itching, white patches, cracks or sores that don't go away with a moisturiser — see a gynaecologist.
- Cystitis keeps coming back, or you have a fever with lower back pain.

## What to try this week

- Sleep and rest come first: go to bed half an hour earlier on at least three evenings.
- If you have dryness, start a vaginal moisturiser 2–3 times a week, and use a lubricant for intimacy.
- A few minutes of pelvic floor exercises every day.
