# Mood swings, anxiety and irritability: what is happening

*5 min read*

"I don't recognise myself." You snap over little things, cry for no reason, anxiety hits out of nowhere, and things that used to bring you joy now leave you cold. Your character has not gone bad. In the transition, this happens to a great many women — and there is an explanation.

## Why it swings

Oestrogen supports the brain chemicals responsible for mood and pleasure. In perimenopause it does not simply fall — it swings, and it is the size of these swings that unsettles mood. On top of that, progesterone declines, and with it the brain's natural "calming system". Hence anxiety, irritability, bursts of anger, tears, and the feeling that it has become harder to cope with pressure.

Irritability and anger are often stronger than sadness. This is a hormonal picture, not a "bad character".

And something important, so you are not frightened: for most women this is a passing reaction. It usually peaks in perimenopause and then settles. Those who have had serious periods of low mood before should be more watchful.

## How to tell what exactly is going on

The key is **when** you feel bad:

- **only in the last week before your period**, easing in the first days after it starts;
- **all month**, but worse before your period;
- **all month**, with no link to your cycle.

What will help depends on this. And you can only tell the difference with a diary covering at least two cycles — in hindsight, memory blames everything on "before my period". Log your mood and the first day of your period in VIA-L — after a couple of months the pattern will be clear.

## What helps

**Sleep.** Broken sleep and built-up tiredness amplify the hormonal mood swings. If your sleep is broken, start there.

**Movement.** The best-proven lever, for many comparable in strength to medication. About 150 minutes a week: walking, strength training — and gentle practices such as yoga and tai chi ease anxiety the most. Strength training helps women especially.

**Morning light.** Bright daylight right after waking noticeably lifts mood in perimenopause. A walk in daylight is a simple daily step.

**Slow breathing.** 5–10 minutes a day with a longer out-breath — about six breaths a minute. It acts on anxiety directly, without "willpower".

**Food.** A Mediterranean style, fibre, fermented foods, a protein breakfast. Magnesium is best from food — dark leafy greens, pumpkin seeds, pulses; a supplement makes sense if there is little in your diet.

## What to remove

- **Alcohol.** It calms you for an hour, and then anxiety comes back stronger — especially in perimenopause.
- **A lot of coffee.** Caffeine winds up anxiety and makes hot flashes worse — only before midday.
- **"Stress formulas" with vitamin B6** are not safe when taken long term.

## What a doctor can offer

If low mood or anxiety gets in the way of life, the foundation of help is psychotherapy (including programmes designed specifically for menopause) and, if needed, medication. In perimenopause, especially together with hot flashes, hormone therapy can also help mood — this is for a doctor to decide.

## When to seek help straight away

- Any thoughts of harming yourself, or that it would be "better not to live" — contact a specialist or a crisis helpline immediately. This is not something to wait out.
- Low mood or loss of interest almost every day for more than two weeks, or anxiety that gets in the way of life and work — see a doctor in the next few days.
- You are sleeping much less yet have lots of energy, your speech has sped up, you feel drawn to impulsive spending and decisions — see a doctor.

- A walk in daylight in the first hour after waking.
- Get up at the same time all seven days.
