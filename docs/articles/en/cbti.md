# CBT-I: how to learn to sleep again

*4 min read*

CBT-I is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. It isn't a pill or a list of "sleep hygiene" tips — it's a set of techniques that change the habits and thoughts that keep insomnia going. American and European medical societies call it the first choice for chronic insomnia, ahead of medication. It works in perimenopause, in menopause and in men over 40.

## Why sleep breaks down in midlife

There are usually several causes, and one can hide another.

- **Hormones, hot flashes, anxiety, stress.** Harder to fall asleep, waking more often, getting up earlier.
- **Restless legs.** An unpleasant feeling in the legs in the evening that keeps you from falling asleep. Often linked to low iron.
- **Sleep apnea.** Light snoring, pauses in breathing, short awakenings you don't remember. In the morning the night feels bad for no clear reason; during the day, tiredness. Apnea is more common after menopause. Apnea and insomnia often come together and make each other worse.
- **A shift in the body clock.** The body starts falling asleep and waking earlier. Waking at 4–5 a.m. with a good energy level during the day is often the clock resetting, not insomnia.

## What CBT-I is made of

- **A sleep diary** — the real picture instead of an impression.
- **The bed is for sleep only** — don't lie awake; get up and come back when you feel sleepy.
- **A sleep window** — time in bed is matched to your actual sleep, with the same wake-up time every day.
- **Working with thoughts** — easing the pressure of "I must fall asleep no matter what".
- **Relaxation and mindfulness** — helpers that lower tension.

## How well it works

About 60–70% of people sleep noticeably better. The effect lasts longer than with sleeping pills, because it changes what was keeping insomnia going. Digital programmes and sessions by phone work too.

What matters most is consistency. One bad night decides nothing; what you do day after day for a few weeks does.

## When CBT-I alone isn't enough

Snoring with pauses in breathing, restless legs, strong anxiety or low mood need to be found and treated on their own — otherwise sleep won't settle. Treating anxiety without working on sleep helps less, and when sleep improves, the rest often gets easier too. Sometimes a doctor adds medication — that doesn't replace CBT-I, it complements it.
