Below is a comprehensive evidence-based reference module for the full thyroid panel, integrating guideline-based reference ranges, nutritional interventions, marker interactions, and red flags.

**TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)**

**Standard vs. Functional Ranges — Why 0.5-4.5 mIU/L Is Too Broad**

The NHANES III disease-free reference population (n=13,344) established a TSH reference range (2.5th-97.5th percentile) of **0.45-4.12 mIU/L**, with a geometric mean of **1.4 mIU/L**.[1] However, this single range obscures critical age-dependent variation. A 2025 cross-sectional analysis of 8,308 NHANES participants demonstrated that age-, sex-, and race-specific reference intervals **reclassified 48.5% of persons with subclinical hypothyroidism as normal**, particularly women and White participants.[2]

**What Drives TSH Elevation Before T4/T3 Become Abnormal:**

TSH rises as the earliest compensatory response to declining thyroid reserve — the pituitary increases TSH output to maintain FT4/FT3 within the reference range. This "subclinical" phase can persist for years. Drivers include: autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's — 90%+ of primary hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions), iodine excess or deficiency, medications (lithium, amiodarone, immune checkpoint inhibitors), and aging.[9][13]

**FREE T4 (Free Thyroxine)**

**Reference Ranges and Clinical Significance**

Typical reference range: **0.8-1.8 ng/dL (10-23 pmol/L)**, though this varies significantly by assay platform.[14][9] FT4 is the primary circulating thyroid hormone and serves as the prohormone for the biologically active T3. In primary hypothyroidism, FT4 falls only after TSH has been elevated for a sustained period — making FT4 a later marker than TSH.[9]

For patients on levothyroxine for primary hypothyroidism, the treatment target is **TSH normalization** (not FT4 targeting). For central (secondary) hypothyroidism, where TSH is unreliable, the target FT4 should be in the **upper half of the reference range**.[15]

**What Impairs T4 Production:**

- Autoimmune destruction of thyroid tissue (Hashimoto's)
- Iodine deficiency (globally the most common cause)
- Thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine ablation
- Medications: lithium, amiodarone, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors
- Central causes: pituitary or hypothalamic disease

---

**FREE T3 (Free Triiodothyronine)**

**Reference Ranges and the FT3/FT4 Ratio**

Typical reference range: **2.3-4.2 pg/mL (3.5-6.5 pmol/L)**. T3 is the biologically active thyroid hormone — approximately **80-90% of circulating T3 is produced by peripheral conversion of T4** via type 1 (DIO1) and type 2 (DIO2) deiodinase enzymes, both of which are selenoproteins.[16][17]

**The FT3/FT4 Ratio as a Marker of Peripheral Conversion Efficiency:**

The FT3/FT4 ratio reflects the activity of peripheral deiodinases. A **low FT3/FT4 ratio** suggests impaired T4→T3 conversion, which can occur in:

- **Selenium deficiency:** Selenium is the catalytic center of all three deiodinases. Selenium deficiency reduces hepatic DIO1 activity by up to 85%, decreasing T3 production by ~30% and increasing T4 levels. However, deiodinases rank high in the selenium supply hierarchy — their function is impaired only during **severe** selenium deficiency.[18][19][16]
- **Chronic illness / inflammation:** Proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) reduce DIO1 activity while increasing DIO3 (the inactivating enzyme), producing the classic low-T3/high-rT3 pattern of nonthyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS). Cytokine-induced oxidative stress depletes thiol cofactors required for deiodinase function.[20][17]
- **Caloric restriction:** Severe energy deficit reduces T3 production as a metabolic adaptation to conserve energy
- **Chronic stress / elevated cortisol:** Glucocorticoids increase DIO3 expression, favoring T4 inactivation to rT3 over activation to T3
- **Iron deficiency:** Thyroid peroxidase is a heme-containing enzyme; iron deficiency impairs thyroid hormone synthesis[21][22]
- **DIO2 polymorphism (Thr92Ala):** Present in ~12-36% of the population; associated with reduced DIO2 activity and potentially impaired intracellular T4→T3 conversion. Some patients with this polymorphism may prefer combination T4/T3 therapy, though evidence remains limited.[11]

**Nutritional Interventions to Support T4→T3 Conversion:**

- **Selenium (100-200 µg/day as selenomethionine):** Cofactor for DIO1 and DIO2; see detailed discussion below
- **Zinc (15-30 mg/day):** An RCT in hypothyroid women showed zinc supplementation significantly increased FT3 and the FT3/FT4 ratio[23]
- **Adequate caloric intake:** Caloric restriction is one of the most potent suppressors of T4→T3 conversion
- **Iron repletion:** Ferritin >100 µg/L ameliorated persistent hypothyroid symptoms in two-thirds of women despite adequate levothyroxine therapy[22]

**REVERSE T3 (rT3)**

**Clinical Controversy: When Is Elevated rT3 Meaningful?**

The ATA-commissioned review (2023) states clearly: measurement of rT3 is widely cited in the lay press as a potential marker to guide therapy, but **there is currently no evidence to support this application**. Serum rT3 typically rises as T3 falls during nonthyroidal illness; consequently, measurement of rT3 adds little to this diagnosis unless NTIS is confounding the diagnosis of central hypothyroidism. **Except for rare genetic conditions and consumptive hypothyroidism, there is no need to measure rT3 in routine clinical practice.**[14]

**What Drives Elevated rT3:**

rT3 is produced by the inactivating enzyme DIO3, which converts T4 to the inactive rT3 (inner-ring deiodination) rather than the active T3 (outer-ring deiodination). Elevated rT3 reflects increased DIO3 activity and/or decreased DIO1 activity:[17][24][25][26][27]

- **Nonthyroidal illness (acute and chronic):** The most common cause — any significant illness triggers the low-T3/high-rT3 pattern as a metabolic adaptation
- **Caloric restriction / fasting:** Reduces DIO1 activity, shifting T4 metabolism toward rT3
- **Chronic stress / elevated cortisol:** Glucocorticoids upregulate DIO3
- **Medications:** Amiodarone, propranolol, glucocorticoids inhibit DIO1
- **Iron deficiency:** May contribute to impaired deiodinase function
- **Acute myocardial infarction:** Increased DIO3 activity in ischemic tissue[24]

**The rT3/FT3 Ratio:**

In noncritical diseases, the FT3/rT3 ratio better reflects peripheral conversion abnormalities than either marker alone.[17] However, rT3 assays are not widely available, not standardized, and measurement of T3 alone is more practical, cheaper, and equally effective for identifying NTIS.[14][27]

**Clinical Bottom Line:** Elevated rT3 in an outpatient setting most commonly reflects physiological adaptation to illness, caloric restriction, or stress — not a primary thyroid disorder. The appropriate intervention is to address the underlying cause (adequate nutrition, stress management, treating the illness), not to prescribe T3 supplementation based on rT3 levels. Routine rT3 measurement is not recommended by any major endocrine society guideline.

**TPO ANTIBODIES (Anti-Thyroid Peroxidase)**

**Clinical Significance and Titers**

TPO antibodies are the hallmark of autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's) and are present in **>90% of Hashimoto's patients**.[9][13] Standard laboratory reference: **[13][12]

**At What Titers Do They Become Clinically Relevant?**

Any detectable TPO antibody level above the assay-specific cutoff is clinically meaningful, as it confirms autoimmune etiology and predicts progression risk. Higher titers generally correlate with greater thyroid inflammation and faster progression, though the relationship is not perfectly linear. In pregnancy, even low-positive TPO antibodies increase the risk of postpartum thyroiditis and miscarriage.[28]

**Evidence for Gluten Elimination in Hashimoto's:**

The first meta-analysis investigating gluten-free diet (GFD) in Hashimoto's (4 studies, 87 patients without celiac disease) showed a **trend toward reduction** in both TgAb (ES -0.39, p=0.06) and TPOAb (ES -0.40, p=0.07) — approaching but not reaching statistical significance. TSH decreased significantly (ES -0.35, p=0.02) and FT4 increased (ES +0.35, p=0.02). The effect was more pronounced in patients with **gluten-related conditions** (non-celiac gluten sensitivity).[29]

A pilot RCT in drug-naïve Hashimoto's women with incidentally positive anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies showed that 6 months of GFD **significantly reduced both TPOAb and TgAb titers** and slightly increased the SPINA-GT index (a marker of thyroid secretory capacity).[30] However, a 12-month RCT in 62 women without gluten sensitivity found **no significant differences** in TPOAb or TgAb between GFD and control groups, except a reduction in TSH.[31]

A 2025 Mendelian randomization study found **no genetic causal relationship** between gluten-free diet and autoimmune thyroid disease, suggesting that any benefits of GFD may operate through non-genetic mechanisms (e.g., improved gut permeability, reduced intestinal inflammation).[32]

**Clinical Bottom Line:** GFD may benefit Hashimoto's patients who have **concurrent celiac disease or non-celiac gluten sensitivity**, but current evidence is insufficient to recommend universal gluten elimination for all Hashimoto's patients.[29][33] Screening for celiac disease (anti-tTG IgA) is warranted in Hashimoto's patients given the well-established comorbidity.[34]

**Nutritional Interventions to Reduce TPO Antibody Titers:**

- **Selenium (see detailed section below):** The strongest evidence base for antibody reduction
- **Vitamin D (2,000-4,000 IU/day):** Inverse associations between vitamin D status and TPO/TgAb titers have been reported, though a network meta-analysis found vitamin D alone did not significantly reduce TPOAb at 6 months[35][21]
- **Anti-inflammatory dietary pattern:** Mediterranean diet, omega-3 fatty acids, polyphenols — reduce systemic inflammation that drives autoimmune progression[36]

**THE SELENIUM-IODINE-ZINC TRIAD**

**Selenium — The Most Evidence-Based Thyroid Micronutrient**

The thyroid gland contains the **highest selenium concentration per gram of any tissue** in the body.[28] Selenium is the catalytic center of three critical selenoprotein families in the thyroid:[16][39][40]

1. **Glutathione peroxidases (GPx):** Protect thyrocytes from H₂O₂ generated during thyroid hormone synthesis
2. **Iodothyronine deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, DIO3):** Control T4→T3 conversion and T3/T4 inactivation
3. **Thioredoxin reductases (TrxR):** Maintain intracellular redox balance

**Evidence for Selenium Supplementation in Hashimoto's:**

The most comprehensive meta-analysis (35 RCTs, 2,358 participants, GRADE: moderate certainty) found:[41]

- **TPOAb significantly decreased** (SMD -0.96, 95% CI -1.36 to -0.56)
- **TSH decreased in patients without thyroid hormone replacement** (SMD -0.21, 95% CI -0.43 to -0.02)
- **Malondialdehyde (oxidative stress marker) decreased** (SMD -1.16)
- **No significant changes** in FT4, T4, FT3, T3, TgAb, or thyroid volume
- **Adverse effects comparable to placebo** (OR 0.89)
- **Selenomethionine** is more effective than sodium selenite or selenium yeast[37]

**Optimal Selenium Dose:** **100-200 µg/day as selenomethionine**. The upper tolerable intake level is 400 µg/day; chronic intake >300 µg/day may increase type 2 diabetes risk. In regions of low selenium intake, 50-100 µg/day may be sufficient.[28][21]

**Food Sources:** Brazil nuts (1-2 nuts provide ~100-200 µg), seafood, organ meats, eggs, sunflower seeds, mushrooms.

**Iodine — The U-Shaped Risk Curve**

Iodine is essential for thyroid hormone synthesis — both T4 and T3 contain iodine atoms. However, the relationship between iodine and autoimmune thyroiditis follows a **U-shaped curve**: both deficiency and excess increase risk.[21][43][44]

**Why Excess Iodine Worsens Autoimmune Thyroiditis Without Adequate Selenium:**

Chronic high iodine intake induces autoimmune thyroiditis through multiple mechanisms:[21][44]

- **Highly iodinated thyroglobulin is more immunogenic** — triggering stronger autoimmune responses
- Iodine excess increases intra-thyroid **Th17 cells** and inhibits **T regulatory (Treg) cell** development
- Iodine triggers abnormal expression of **TRAIL** in thyrocytes, inducing apoptosis and parenchymal destruction
- Iodine excess increases **H₂O₂ production** in the thyroid — without adequate selenium (GPx), this H₂O₂ causes oxidative damage to thyrocytes

The critical interaction: **selenium-dependent GPx enzymes protect thyrocytes from the H₂O₂ generated during iodine-mediated thyroid hormone synthesis**. When selenium is deficient, the thyroid cannot neutralize the oxidative stress created by iodine, amplifying tissue damage and autoimmune activation.[28][21][44]

**Optimal Iodine Intake:** **150 µg/day for adults** (WHO/IOM recommendation); 250 µg/day during pregnancy/lactation. Avoid exceeding 500-1,100 µg/day chronically. Urinary iodine concentration (UIC) of 100-199 µg/L indicates adequate intake.

**Food Sources:** Seaweed (highly variable — can contain 1,000-10,000+ µg per serving; use with caution), iodized salt, dairy, eggs, fish.

**Zinc — The Third Pillar**

Zinc is involved in thyroid hormone synthesis, receptor binding, and T4→T3 conversion. Patients with hypothyroidism have significantly lower zinc concentrations than healthy controls (Hedges' g = -0.86, p=0.035).[45] An NHANES analysis (n=6,402) found a significant interaction between dietary selenium and zinc intakes on hypothyroidism risk (OR 5.99 for low selenium + low zinc combined).[46]

**Evidence from RCTs:**

- Zinc supplementation (30 mg/day as zinc gluconate) in hypothyroid women significantly increased FT3 and the FT3/FT4 ratio, suggesting enhanced peripheral conversion.[23]
- Co-supplementation with zinc (30 mg), magnesium (250 mg), and vitamin A (25,000 IU twice weekly) for 10 weeks significantly increased FT4 and decreased hs-CRP in hypothyroid patients.[47]
- A systematic review concluded that evidence for zinc supplementation on thyroid hormones remains **inconclusive** due to study heterogeneity, but observational data consistently show lower zinc in hypothyroid patients.[48]

**Optimal Zinc Dose:** **15-30 mg/day as zinc gluconate or zinc picolinate**. Take separately from levothyroxine (zinc can impair absorption). Upper tolerable intake: 40 mg/day. Chronic supplementation >40 mg/day can induce copper deficiency.

**Food Sources:** Oysters (highest), red meat, poultry, beans, nuts, whole grains.

**ASHWAGANDHA AND THYROID FUNCTION — A CAUTIONARY NOTE**

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) has gained popularity for thyroid support. One small RCT (n=50) in subclinical hypothyroid patients showed that 600 mg/day for 8 weeks significantly improved TSH, T3, and T4 compared to placebo.[54] A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 RCTs found no significant effect on TSH or T3, but a modest increase in T4 (MD +0.61 µg/dL, p=0.02).[55]

However, **significant safety concerns** exist: ashwagandha has been implicated in cases of **thyrotoxicosis** (excessive thyroid hormone elevation), and a 2025 safety review concluded it poses significant risks to patients with thyroid disorders, recommending restricted use in this population.[56][57] The JCEM review on thyroid and alternative approaches concluded that ashwagandha has **"little to no evidence of any impact on thyroid disorders"** and noted the limited generalizability of existing studies.[33]

**Clinical Bottom Line:** Ashwagandha should be used with caution in patients with thyroid disease, particularly those on levothyroxine, as it may cause unpredictable thyroid hormone elevation. Monitoring thyroid function is essential if patients choose to use it.

- **Rapidly growing thyroid nodule, especially if firm or fixed:** High-risk feature for thyroid malignancy — requires urgent thyroid ultrasound and FNA biopsy[58][59][60]
- **New-onset hoarseness with thyroid nodule:** Suggests recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion by thyroid cancer — urgent ENT evaluation and thyroid ultrasound[59][61]

- **Central hypothyroidism pattern (low FT4 + low/normal TSH):** Suggests pituitary or hypothalamic pathology — pituitary MRI and full anterior pituitary hormone panel mandatory[9][15]
- **Thyroid storm (tachycardia, fever, altered mental status, thyrotoxicosis):** Medical emergency — ICU admission, beta-blockers, thionamides, iodine, glucocorticoids
- **Myxedema coma (severe hypothyroidism with hypothermia, altered consciousness, bradycardia):** Medical emergency — IV levothyroxine, glucocorticoids, supportive care
