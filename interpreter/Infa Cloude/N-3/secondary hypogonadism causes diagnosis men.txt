**Pathophysiology by Etiology**

**Obesity-related secondary hypogonadism** operates through multiple converging mechanisms: (1) adipocyte aromatase converts testosterone to estradiol, which suppresses hypothalamic GnRH pulsatility via negative feedback; (2) hyperinsulinemia suppresses hepatic SHBG production, lowering total testosterone (though free testosterone may initially be preserved); (3) proinflammatory adipocytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and dysregulated leptin signaling further suppress the HPG axis.[8] The European Male Aging Study (EMAS) prospectively demonstrated that obesity (BMI ≥30) was the strongest predictor of incident secondary hypogonadism (OR 2.86, P [9]

An important caveat: many obese men have **"pseudohypogonadism"** — low total testosterone due to suppressed SHBG but normal free testosterone, normal gonadotropins, and no clinical features of androgen deficiency. These men are eugonadal and do not require testosterone therapy.[3][8] Calculated free testosterone (using total testosterone and SHBG) is essential to avoid misdiagnosis in this population.[3]

**Opioid-induced hypogonadism (OIH)** results from direct suppression of hypothalamic GnRH pulsatility via μ-opioid receptors, with possible direct testicular effects.[10] The prevalence is approximately **63%** in a meta-analysis of 15 studies (n = 3,250, 99.5% male).[5] Key modifiers include:[10]

- **Opioid type**: Full μ-agonists (methadone, morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone) cause the most suppression; **buprenorphine** (partial μ-agonist/κ-antagonist) has milder or no suppressive effects
- **Dose**: Positive dose-response relationship; nearly all opioids at doses equivalent to ≥30 mg methadone suppress testosterone[1]
- **Duration of action**: Long-acting opioids cause greater and more sustained suppression than short-acting formulations
- **Reversibility**: A 20-year cohort study (2026) confirmed that central hypogonadism was significantly less common among opioid-free participants, providing the first long-term prospective evidence that OIH resolves with sustained abstinence.[11]

**Glucocorticoid-induced hypogonadism** occurs through direct inhibition of GnRH synthesis. Prednisone ≥15 mg/day suppresses testosterone within 3 days, with magnitude proportional to dose.[6]

**Diagnostic Algorithm**

The 2026 JAMA review and the Endocrine Society guideline provide a stepwise approach:[3][1]

**Step 1: Confirm hypogonadism.** Serum total testosterone must be low ([3]

**Step 2: Classify as primary vs. secondary.** Measure serum **FSH and LH**:[3]

- Elevated FSH/LH → primary hypogonadism (consider karyotype for Klinefelter syndrome)
- Low or inappropriately normal FSH/LH → secondary hypogonadism → proceed to etiologic workup

**Step 3: Etiologic workup for secondary hypogonadism:**[3][1]

- **Medication review**: Opioids, glucocorticoids, exogenous androgens/AAS, prolactin-elevating drugs (risperidone, haloperidol, metoclopramide), checkpoint inhibitors
- **Serum prolactin**: To detect prolactinoma or medication-induced hyperprolactinemia
- **Iron studies** (transferrin saturation and ferritin): In men 20 units RBC transfusions in ≤2 years
- **Cushing syndrome screening**: If clinical features present (purple striae, easy bruising, proximal weakness)
- **Assessment of other pituitary hormones**: If clinically indicated (e.g., symptoms of hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, growth hormone deficiency)

**Cannot-Miss Diagnoses**

Several etiologies of secondary hypogonadism require urgent recognition:

- **Pituitary apoplexy**: Acute hemorrhage or infarction of a pituitary adenoma presenting with sudden severe headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, and acute hypopituitarism. Requires emergent neurosurgical evaluation.
- **Sellar/suprasellar mass lesions**: Pituitary macroadenomas, craniopharyngiomas, meningiomas, germinomas, and Rathke cleft cysts can all cause secondary hypogonadism through mass effect on gonadotropes or hypothalamic compression. Red flags include visual field deficits (classically bitemporal hemianopia), headaches, and deficiencies of multiple pituitary hormones.[13][7]
- **Infiltrative/inflammatory disease**: Hemochromatosis (iron overload preferentially damages gonadotropes), sarcoidosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and lymphocytic hypophysitis can cause secondary hypogonadism. Hemochromatosis is particularly important because it is **reversible if treated before permanent gonadotrope damage** — iron saturation and ferritin should be checked in all young men with unexplained secondary hypogonadism.[3][1]

---

**Management Principles**

**Reversible secondary hypogonadism** should be managed by treating the underlying cause:[3][1]

- **Obesity**: Weight loss of ≥5–10% is first-line therapy. A 2024 meta-analysis demonstrated mean testosterone increases of **72 ng/dL** with low-calorie diets and **207 ng/dL** after bariatric surgery (median follow-up 6 months). The testosterone rise is proportional to weight lost, and weight regain leads to testosterone returning to baseline.[3][14]

- **Medications**: Discontinue the causative drug when possible. Recovery of HPG-axis function typically occurs within **weeks to several months** for medication-induced hypogonadism of [3]
- **Opioids**: If discontinuation is not feasible, consider switching to buprenorphine (more favorable endocrine profile) or dose reduction. Testosterone replacement may be considered for men with persistent sexual symptoms on chronic opioids unlikely to be discontinued.[1][10]

**Permanent secondary hypogonadism** requires testosterone replacement therapy. Fertility can potentially be restored with **gonadotropin therapy** (hCG ± FSH) in secondary hypogonadism — a key distinction from primary hypogonadism where fertility options are limited.[1][3]
