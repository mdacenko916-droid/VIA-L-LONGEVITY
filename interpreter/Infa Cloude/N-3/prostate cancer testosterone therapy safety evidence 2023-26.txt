### NEJM Review

According to an invited review in the New England Journal of Medicine, testosterone treatment in middle-aged and older men with hypogonadism **has not been reported to increase the risk of prostate cancer** in clinical trials, though important caveats limit the generalizability of this conclusion.[1]

The review details the prostate safety data from the three major RCTs. In the **TTrials** (24 months of observation), prostate cancer was diagnosed in 3 of 335 men (0.9%) in the testosterone group versus 1 of 318 (0.3%) in the placebo group. In the **TRAVERSE trial** (mean 33 months), prostate cancer was diagnosed in 12 of 2,596 men (0.46%) in the testosterone group versus 11 of 2,602 (0.42%) in the placebo group. **High-grade prostate cancer** (Gleason score ≥4+3) was diagnosed in 5 men (0.19%) in the testosterone group versus 3 (0.12%) in the placebo group. In the **T4DM trial** (2-year treatment period), 4 of 504 men (0.8%) in the testosterone group versus 5 of 503 (1%) in the placebo group were diagnosed with prostate cancer.[1]

The review emphasizes that all three trials **excluded men considered to be at high risk for prostate cancer** and used protocols to manage PSA elevations and detect prostate cancer. Therefore, these results may not apply to the broader population of men with hypogonadism.[1] The concern about TRT and prostate cancer is grounded in the biological reality that testosterone promotes the growth of metastatic prostate cancer, androgen-deprivation therapy retards its growth, and men with hypogonadism appear to have a lower lifetime risk of prostate cancer.[1]

An important practical consideration highlighted in the review is that testosterone treatment **increases PSA levels**, which increases the likelihood of prostate biopsy, which in turn increases the risk of detecting subclinical low-grade prostate cancer — a cascade that can lead to unnecessary treatment with its attendant risks (erectile dysfunction, urinary incontinence, bowel dysfunction).[1]

The review recommends that older men for whom testosterone treatment is being considered should undergo a **baseline evaluation for prostate cancer risk and lower urinary tract symptoms** before initiating therapy. Although trials have not shown increased prostate cancer risk, men at high risk for prostate cancer were excluded from those trials.[1]

**Mendelian Randomization: Genetically Higher Testosterone Increases Prostate Cancer Risk**

In contrast to the reassuring short-term RCT data, MR studies consistently show that **genetically predicted higher free testosterone is associated with increased prostate cancer risk**:[5][6][7][8]

- **Watts et al. (2022)**: Free testosterone was associated with aggressive prostate cancer in MR analysis (OR per 1 SD = **1.23**, 95% CI 1.08–1.40) and overall prostate cancer (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.34). SHBG and total testosterone were inversely associated with overall prostate cancer in blood-based analyses but null in MR.[6]
- **Chang et al. (2022)**: Genetically predicted bioavailable testosterone was significantly associated with increased prostate cancer risk (OR **1.17**, 95% CI 1.09–1.26, P = 2.51 × 10⁻⁵).[7]
- **Mohammadi-Shemirani et al. (2020)**: Lifelong increased free testosterone had adverse effects including increased prostate cancer risk.[8]
- **Rhomberg-Kauert et al. (2025)**: A systematic review of 29 MR studies found that elevated genetically predicted free testosterone **consistently** correlated with increased prostate cancer risk.[5]

**The Saturation Model: Reconciling the Paradox**

The **androgen receptor saturation model** (Morgentaler & Traish, 2009) provides the theoretical framework for understanding why TRT in hypogonadal men does not appear to stimulate prostate cancer growth:[9][10][11]

- Prostate growth is exquisitely sensitive to androgen concentrations at **very low levels** (near-castrate range) but becomes **insensitive to changes above the saturation point** (~150–250 ng/dL)
- Maximal androgen receptor binding is achieved at serum testosterone concentrations **well below the physiologic range**
- This explains why castration causes dramatic tumor regression (removing androgens below the saturation point) while restoring testosterone to the normal range in hypogonadal men produces minimal additional prostatic stimulation
- Intraprostatic testosterone and DHT concentrations do not change significantly with TRT once the saturation point is exceeded[10]

However, the saturation model has limitations: it does not account for the MR findings of increased prostate cancer risk with lifelong higher testosterone, nor does it address the biology of castration-resistant prostate cancer, where AR amplification, mutations, and intratumoral androgen synthesis can reactivate AR signaling at castrate serum levels.[12][13]

**Observational Evidence in Prostate Cancer Survivors**

Two 2025 systematic reviews provide the most comprehensive observational data:

**Santucci et al. (BJU International, 2025)** — 19 studies across three clinical settings:[14]

- **Active surveillance** (5 studies): Progression rates 0%–32%, not significantly different from non-exposed controls at follow-up as long as 70 months
- **Post-radical prostatectomy** (10 studies): Biochemical recurrence rates **0%–7%**
- **Post-radiotherapy** (6 studies): Biochemical recurrence rates **0%–6%** with up to 60 months of follow-up

**Gibson et al. (International Journal of Impotence Research, 2025)** — 12 studies (sample sizes 10–152):[15]

- TRT was **not associated with increased biochemical recurrence or cancer progression** in any included study
- Several studies showed **lower** recurrence rates in TRT groups compared to controls
- TRT consistently improved hypogonadal symptoms

A large Finnish population-based study (Siltari et al., 2023; n = 78,615; 18-year follow-up) found that TRT users had **lower prostate cancer mortality** than non-users (HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.3–0.91) and slightly lower CVD and all-cause mortality.[16]

A Medicare claims analysis (Baik et al., 2025; n = 546,964 matched pairs) found TRT was associated with a **16% reduction** in the hazard of prostate cancer (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.82–0.86), including reductions across long-term, short-term, parenteral, and topical use. However, TRT was associated with a **13% increase** in the hazard of BPH (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.11–1.14).[17]

**Post-Radiation Therapy: Emerging Data**

**Chandra et al. (2026)** reported on 33 men with pathology-confirmed prostate cancer treated with radiation ± ADT who subsequently received TRT. Median testosterone increased from 66 to 299 ng/dL. Only 1 patient (3%) developed metastatic disease 3 years after starting TRT. TRT was associated with improvements in fatigue, mood, sexual symptoms, and correction of anemia in 43% of anemic patients. A narrative review of published case reports found a weighted mean biochemical recurrence rate of **3.3%** during mean 42.6 months of follow-up.[18]
