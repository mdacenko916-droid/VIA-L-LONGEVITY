The relationship between dietary protein, testosterone, and muscle mass in aging men involves **three distinct but interconnected axes**: (1) protein's effect on SHBG and testosterone bioavailability, (2) protein's direct role in muscle protein synthesis and sarcopenia prevention, and (3) the interaction between protein intake and anabolic hormones (including testosterone) in determining muscle outcomes. The evidence reveals a nuanced picture where **adequate protein intake (~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day) is critical for both testosterone bioavailability and muscle preservation, but protein intake above this range does not augment lean mass without concurrent resistance exercise or anabolic stimuli**.[1][2]

**Protein Intake and Testosterone/SHBG**

**Low protein intake raises SHBG and reduces testosterone bioavailability.** The Massachusetts Male Aging Study (n = 1,552 men, aged 40–70) demonstrated that protein intake was **inversely correlated with SHBG** (P [3]

**The protein-to-carbohydrate ratio modulates both testosterone and cortisol.** Anderson et al. showed in a crossover study of 7 men that 10 days on a high-protein diet (44% protein, 35% carbohydrate) produced **significantly lower total testosterone** (371 ± 23 ng/dL) compared to a high-carbohydrate diet (10% protein, 70% carbohydrate; 468 ± 34 ng/dL, P [4]

**In the context of weight loss, macronutrient composition does not differentially affect testosterone.** A 52-week RCT (n = 118 overweight/obese men) comparing high-protein (35% protein) versus high-carbohydrate (17% protein) energy-restricted diets found that both groups experienced **equivalent increases in total testosterone, SHBG, and free testosterone** (all P [5]

**Acute protein ingestion transiently suppresses testosterone.** Whey protein ingestion acutely decreases plasma testosterone, potentially through leucine-mediated mTOR activation increasing androgen receptor expression and tissue testosterone uptake, thereby lowering circulating levels. This effect appears to be transient and may not reflect reduced testosterone production but rather enhanced peripheral utilization.[6]

A comprehensive review of dietary patterns and testosterone concluded that the **strongest dietary determinant of testosterone is body composition** (adiposity), with macronutrient composition exerting secondary effects primarily through its influence on body weight and insulin sensitivity.[7][8]

---

**Protein Intake and Muscle Mass in Aging Men**

**Age-related anabolic resistance** is the central challenge. Older adults require approximately **67% more protein per meal** than younger adults to maximally stimulate postprandial muscle protein synthesis (MPS), due to reduced sensitivity of the mTORC1 signaling pathway to amino acid stimulation.[9][10] This anabolic resistance can be partially overcome by:

- **Higher per-meal protein doses** (~25–30 g/meal, containing at least 2.5–3 g leucine)[11][12]
- **Leucine supplementation** (5 g/meal), which enhanced integrated myofibrillar protein synthesis in free-living older men regardless of whether total daily protein was at the RDA (0.8 g/kg) or higher (1.2 g/kg)[13]
- **Resistance exercise**, which extends the "muscle full" set-point and increases sensitivity to amino acids for up to 48 hours[10]

**Protein source matters.** A recent RCT in 31 older men (mean age 72) demonstrated that supplementing 50 g/day of whey or pea protein above the RDA significantly increased integrated MPS, while **collagen protein did not** — highlighting that protein quality (essential amino acid and leucine content) is as important as quantity.[14] Observational data also favor **animal over plant protein** for sarcopenia-related parameters.[2][15]

---

**The Critical Bhasin RCT: Protein Does Not Augment Testosterone's Anabolic Effect**

The most definitive trial addressing the protein–testosterone–muscle interaction is the **Bhasin et al. (2018) JAMA Internal Medicine RCT** — a 2×2 factorial design in 92 functionally limited older men (mean age 73) randomized to protein at the RDA (0.8 g/kg/day) versus higher intake (1.3 g/kg/day), with or without testosterone supplementation, for 6 months.[1]

Key findings:

- **Protein intake above the RDA did not increase lean body mass** (difference: 0.31 kg, 95% CI −0.46 to 1.08, P = 0.43), appendicular lean mass, muscle strength, power, walking speed, or well-being — regardless of testosterone status
- **Testosterone administration substantially increased lean body mass** (~4–5 kg) regardless of protein level
- Higher protein **reduced fat mass** (difference: −1.12 kg, P = 0.02) without affecting lean mass
- Critically, **higher protein did not augment the anabolic response to testosterone**

**Protein Supplementation + Resistance Exercise: The Synergistic Effect**

A comprehensive meta-analysis of 28 RCTs (Kirwan et al. 2022) found that protein supplementation alone **did not significantly increase lean body mass or strength** in older adults. However, when combined with resistance exercise, protein supplementation produced significant gains in **appendicular lean mass** (WMD: 0.54 kg, P = 0.03) and **handgrip strength** (WMD: 1.71 kg, P = 0.03). Notably, the significant effects on appendicular lean mass occurred **only in sarcopenic/frail populations** (WMD: 0.88 kg, P [19]

This finding aligns with the narrative review by Campbell et al. (2023), which concluded that protein intakes above the RDA (~1.3 g/kg/day) positively influence lean body mass changes only in the presence of purposeful **catabolic stressors (energy restriction) or anabolic stressors (resistance exercise)**, not under non-stressed conditions.[2]

---

**Practical Synthesis: Protein Optimization for Testosterone and Muscle in Aging Men**

The evidence converges on several actionable principles:

- **Avoid protein intakes below 0.8 g/kg/day**, which exacerbate SHBG elevation, reduce testosterone bioavailability, and accelerate sarcopenia.[3][2]
- **Target 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** for healthy older men, consistent with ESPEN and PROT-AGE guidelines, distributed across meals with **25–30 g of high-quality protein per meal** to overcome anabolic resistance.[11][17]
- **Protein distribution matters more than total daily intake** — the typical Western pattern of low-protein breakfast and high-protein dinner is suboptimal. Redistributing protein to achieve ≥25 g at each meal, particularly breakfast, maximizes cumulative MPS stimulation.[11][12]
- **Leucine content is the key driver** of per-meal anabolic response. Each meal should contain at least **2.5–3 g leucine** (equivalent to ~25 g whey protein or ~30–35 g of most animal proteins). Leucine supplementation (5 g/meal) can enhance MPS even at RDA-level protein intakes.[10][13]
- **Resistance exercise is the most potent amplifier** of protein's anabolic effect and is essential for translating adequate protein intake into meaningful muscle preservation. Without resistance exercise, protein supplementation above the RDA has minimal effect on lean mass.[19][2]
- **Protein source quality matters** — whey and pea protein increase MPS in older men, while collagen protein does not. Animal protein sources are generally favored over plant sources for sarcopenia prevention, though pea protein appears to be an effective plant-based alternative.[14][15]
- **Very high protein intakes (>35% of calories) may transiently lower total testosterone and raise cortisol** relative to higher-carbohydrate diets, though this effect is modest and may not affect free testosterone or clinical outcomes when energy balance is maintained.[4]
