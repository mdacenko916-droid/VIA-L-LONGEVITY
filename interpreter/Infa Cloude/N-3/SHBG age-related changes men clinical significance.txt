In men, **SHBG increases linearly with age at approximately 1.3–2.5% per year**, driven by increased hepatic SHBG synthesis via upregulation of the transcription factor HNF-4α and downregulation of inhibitory factors PPAR-γ and COUP-TF.[1][2] This age-related rise has major clinical significance for the diagnosis and management of hypogonadism.

**Magnitude and Determinants of the Age-Related SHBG Increase**

Longitudinal data from two Australian cohorts showed SHBG increases of **2.3–2.5% per year** in middle-aged and older men — notably steeper than in younger men — while total testosterone declined by approximately 0.9–1.3% per year.[2] The UK Biobank analysis confirmed that SHBG rises progressively with age, while total testosterone shows only a modest decline; however, **BMI exerts a substantially greater influence on testosterone levels than age itself**.[3]

Key positive determinants of SHBG include age, total testosterone, and free thyroxine (fT4), while negative determinants include **abdominal fat mass, triglycerides, insulin, and estradiol**.[4] Conditions that further elevate SHBG beyond the age-related increase include hepatopathy/compensated cirrhosis, untreated HIV, hyperthyroidism, severe energy deficit, chronic alcohol use, and enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital).[5][6]

**Clinical Significance: Impact on Testosterone Bioavailability**

The rising SHBG with age results in a **steeper decline in free (bioavailable) testosterone than in total testosterone**.[3][7] This creates two clinically important diagnostic pitfalls:

1. **Masked hypogonadism:** An older man with elevated SHBG may have a total testosterone in the normal or borderline range, yet have **low free testosterone and symptomatic androgen deficiency**. Total testosterone alone can be falsely reassuring in this setting.[5][8][6]
2. **Pseudohypogonadism (the opposite scenario):** In men with low SHBG (most commonly from obesity, type 2 diabetes, or insulin resistance), total testosterone may be low while free testosterone remains normal — leading to overdiagnosis of hypogonadism if only total testosterone is measured.[6][9]

A study of over 4,000 men with sexual dysfunction demonstrated that SHBG is independently associated with androgen deficiency features (lower PSA, lower hematocrit) even after adjusting for total testosterone, supporting the concept that elevated SHBG reduces tissue-level androgen bioactivity.[10]

- The **Endocrine Society** recommends measuring free testosterone (by equilibrium dialysis or calculated from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin) in men with conditions that alter SHBG or whose total testosterone is borderline (200–400 ng/dL).[8]
- The **Society for Endocrinology/ACLM (2024)** specifies that calculated free testosterone has **no diagnostic value when SHBG is within the reference range**, but when SHBG is above the reference range, calculated free testosterone may help diagnose hypogonadism despite normal total testosterone levels.[11]
- The **2026 JAMA review** notes that a lower limit of normal for free testosterone of **50–65 pg/mL** has been suggested, though the reference range is not firmly established.[6]
- Direct analog-based free testosterone immunoassays are **inaccurate and should not be used**; equilibrium dialysis or calculated free testosterone (using total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin) are the recommended methods.[8][6]

**Practical Implications**

In aging men being evaluated for hypogonadism, SHBG should be measured alongside total testosterone, particularly when total testosterone is borderline or when SHBG-altering conditions are present. The combination of rising SHBG and declining testosterone production with age means that **calculated free testosterone is the most clinically informative marker of androgen status in older men**, as it more closely correlates with symptoms, bone mineral density, and hematopoiesis than total testosterone alone.[5][7]
