The Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) supports male hormonal health through **multiple synergistic mechanisms — anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, body composition–modifying, and endothelial — rather than through a direct testosterone-raising effect**. Paradoxically, cross-sectional data from NHANES show that men adhering to a Mediterranean diet have **modestly lower total testosterone** (~413 vs. 444 ng/dL) compared to nonrestrictive diets, but this finding likely reflects the diet's relatively lower fat content and higher fiber intake rather than a clinically meaningful hormonal disadvantage.[1] The MedDiet's greatest hormonal value lies in its effects on **visceral fat reduction, inflammation, insulin sensitivity, cortisol modulation, and erectile function preservation** — all of which indirectly support the HPG axis and testosterone bioavailability.

---

**The NHANES Paradox: Lower Total Testosterone but Better Hormonal Milieu**

The Fantus et al. (2020) NHANES analysis (n = 3,128 men) found that both low-fat and Mediterranean diets were associated with lower mean serum testosterone compared to nonrestrictive diets. However, on multivariable analysis controlling for age, BMI, activity, diabetes, and comorbidities, only the **low-fat diet** reached statistical significance (β = −57.2 ng/dL, P [1] This finding is consistent with the well-established observation that **dietary fat intake positively correlates with total testosterone** — the Dorgan et al. controlled feeding study showed 13% higher total testosterone on a high-fat (41%) versus low-fat (19%) diet.[2]

However, total testosterone is a poor surrogate for androgenic bioactivity. The MedDiet's effects on SHBG, inflammation, insulin sensitivity, and body composition may produce a net positive effect on **free testosterone and androgen receptor signaling** despite modestly lower total testosterone. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) analysis of NHANES (n = 4,151 men) found that men consuming the most pro-inflammatory diets had **29.6% higher odds of testosterone deficiency** (P trend = 0.025), independent of BMI — and the MedDiet is among the most anti-inflammatory dietary patterns.[3]

**Mechanisms by Which the Mediterranean Diet Supports Testosterone and Hormonal Health**

**1. Olive oil and testicular steroidogenesis.** Olive oil — the cornerstone fat of the MedDiet — enhances testosterone production through multiple pathways. In rat models, olive oil diets produced the **highest testicular testosterone levels and steroidogenic enzyme activity** (3β-HSD and 17β-HSD) among four dietary oils tested (olive, coconut, soybean, grapeseed), with the lowest testicular oxidative stress.[4][5] The mechanism involves olive oil's restoration of the **SREBP2 cholesterol pathway** (providing cholesterol substrate for steroidogenesis) and enhancement of testicular antioxidant capacity.[6] Oleuropein, a phenolic compound in extra-virgin olive oil, increased testicular testosterone and decreased plasma corticosterone in rats, with evidence of **dose-dependent LH stimulation** from the pituitary — suggesting a central mechanism.[7]

Notably, oleic acid (the predominant MUFA in olive oil) **increases hepatic SHBG production** by downregulating PPARγ in hepatocytes. Men using olive oil for cooking had significantly higher SHBG levels than those using sunflower oil, with MUFA independently accounting for 20.4% of SHBG variance.[8] This creates a nuanced picture: olive oil may simultaneously enhance testicular testosterone production while increasing SHBG — potentially resulting in stable or slightly lower free testosterone but improved total hormonal milieu and cardiovascular protection (low SHBG is an independent CVD risk factor).

**2. Polyphenol-mediated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.** The MedDiet's bioactive polyphenols modulate testosterone through several pathways:[6][9]

- **Oleocanthal** (extra-virgin olive oil) — inhibits COX-1 and COX-2 enzyme activity, reducing testicular inflammation that impairs Leydig cell function
- **Resveratrol** (red wine, grapes) — reduces NO production, ROS levels, and LDL cholesterol; in obese mice, resveratrol supplementation improved StAR protein expression and steroidogenic enzyme activity, leading to higher serum testosterone and LH[10]
- **Flavonoids** (fruits, vegetables) — associated with lower erectile dysfunction incidence, particularly in younger men[11]

However, resveratrol's effects on steroidogenesis are dose-dependent and context-specific. At higher concentrations, resveratrol **inhibits** CYP17 and CYP21 expression and androgen production in adrenal cells, making it a candidate for treating hyperandrogenic disorders like PCOS rather than boosting testosterone.[12] The net effect in the context of dietary intake (low doses from wine and food) is likely anti-inflammatory and protective rather than directly steroidogenic.

**3. Visceral fat reduction — the most potent indirect testosterone mechanism.** The MedDiet's strongest hormonal benefit may be its effect on visceral adipose tissue (VAT), which is the primary driver of obesity-related testosterone decline through aromatase-mediated estradiol production, inflammatory cytokine release, and insulin resistance. Three landmark RCTs demonstrate this:

- **DIRECT-PLUS trial** (n = 294, 88% men): A green-MedDiet enriched with polyphenols produced **14.1% VAT reduction** (vs. 6.0% standard MedDiet, 4.2% healthy dietary guidelines), independent of weight loss. Higher polyphenol intake and lower red meat consumption were independently associated with greater VAT loss.[13]
- **CENTRAL trial** (n = 278, 89% men): MedDiet/low-carbohydrate diet was **superior to low-fat diet** in decreasing intrahepatic, intrapericardial, and pancreatic fat. Exercise had an independent additive effect on VAT loss (−6.67 cm², P [14]
- **PREDIMED-Plus trial** (n = 1,521, 52% men): An energy-reduced MedDiet with physical activity significantly reduced visceral fat (−126 g at 1 year vs. control, P [15]

**4. Cortisol reduction.** The DIRECT-PLUS trial also demonstrated that 18 months of MedDiet adherence significantly reduced fasting morning cortisol (−1.6% for standard MedDiet, −1.8% for green-MedDiet, P [16]

**5. Omega-3 fatty acids and testicular function.** Fish — a key MedDiet component — provides omega-3 PUFAs that support testicular function. A cross-sectional study of 1,679 young Danish men found that fish oil supplement users had **higher semen volume, total sperm count, larger testes, lower LH, and a 15% higher free testosterone-to-LH ratio** (indicating better Leydig cell capacity), with a dose-response relationship.[17] A secondary analysis of an RCT in overweight/obese men found that DHA-enriched fish oil supplementation (860 mg DHA + 120 mg EPA/day for 12 weeks) **significantly increased total testosterone** in men, with changes positively correlated with erythrocyte membrane omega-3 content and inversely correlated with omega-6 content.[18] However, omega-3 intake was positively associated with testicular volume but MUFA intake was **inversely** associated with free testosterone in young Spanish men, highlighting the complexity of fatty acid–hormone interactions.[19]

**Mediterranean Diet and Erectile Function**

The most clinically actionable hormonal benefit of the MedDiet may be its effect on **erectile function**. **Clinical Synthesis**

The MedDiet's relationship with testosterone is best understood as **indirect but multifaceted**. While it does not acutely raise total testosterone (and may modestly lower it relative to higher-fat diets), its effects on the upstream determinants of testosterone — visceral adiposity, systemic inflammation, insulin sensitivity, cortisol, and oxidative stress — make it the most evidence-supported dietary pattern for long-term male hormonal health. The strongest clinical evidence supports the MedDiet for **erectile function preservation** (HR 0.78–0.93 for incident ED), **visceral fat reduction** (14% VAT loss with polyphenol-enriched version), and **cortisol modulation** — all of which address the pathophysiology of functional hypogonadism in aging men. For men specifically seeking to maximize total testosterone, ensuring adequate dietary fat intake (≥30% of calories) within a Mediterranean framework — emphasizing extra-virgin olive oil, fatty fish, nuts, and moderate red wine — represents a reasonable evidence-based approach that balances hormonal optimization with cardiovascular and metabolic protection.[6][24][13]
