The relationship between testosterone deficiency and cardiovascular risk in men is **complex and paradoxical**: observational data consistently associate low endogenous testosterone with increased CV mortality, yet Mendelian randomization studies suggest that genetically higher testosterone may actually increase coronary artery disease risk.[1][2] The landmark **TRAVERSE trial** resolved the most pressing clinical question by demonstrating that testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) is **noninferior to placebo for major adverse cardiac events (MACE)** in high-risk men, though it raised new safety signals for atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and pulmonary embolism.[3]

---

**Observational Evidence: Low Testosterone and CV Risk**

A 2024 individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis (9 studies, 255,830 participant-years) using mass spectrometry–measured testosterone found **nonlinear associations** with mortality:[4]

- Testosterone **10 IU/L**: independently associated with all-cause mortality (suggesting primary hypogonadism carries greater risk than functional)
- **No association** between low testosterone and incident CV events in the fully adjusted model

Critically, the association between low testosterone and mortality **lost significance after full adjustment** (for BMI, diabetes, smoking, physical activity, etc.) except at the very lowest testosterone thresholds.[5] The UK Biobank — the largest prospective cohort — found lower testosterone associated with higher all-cause mortality but **not with incident CV events or CV deaths**.[1]

In men with established coronary artery disease, low free testosterone (≤7 pg/mL) was associated with nearly **3-fold higher CV mortality** over ~100 months of follow-up in a prospective cohort of 834 men with stable CAD:[6]

Multiple observational studies have demonstrated associations between low testosterone and atherosclerotic burden, including negative correlations between testosterone and carotid intima-media thickness, angiographic coronary disease severity, and Framingham Risk Score.[7] However, as noted in the JACC review, "it is unclear if this is a causal association or due to low T being a biomarker of poor health."[7]

---

**Mendelian Randomization: A Contradictory Signal**

MR studies — which use genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causality — present a strikingly different picture from observational data:

- **Morbey et al. (2026)**: Higher genetically predicted testosterone conferred **increased CAD risk** in men (OR 1.17, 95% CI 1.07–1.27, P = 3.32 × 10⁻⁴), mediated by higher blood pressure. The directionally opposite observational association was attributed to confounding by BMI and type 2 diabetes.[2]
- **Luo et al. (2019)**: Genetically predicted testosterone (via JMJD1C variants) was positively associated with **thromboembolism** (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.27–3.46) and **heart failure** (OR 7.81, 95% CI 2.56–23.8) in men.[8]
- **Yuan et al. (2022)**: Higher genetically predicted testosterone was associated with **lower risk of type 2 diabetes and coronary atherosclerotic outcomes** in males — but this appeared mediated through SHBG, and multivariable MR showed no direct causal effect of testosterone on CHD independent of SHBG.[9]
- **Li et al. (2021)**: In multivariable MR, neither SHBG nor testosterone had a direct causal effect on CHD; the apparent protective effect of testosterone was largely mediated through SHBG (accounting for 67% of the total effect).[10]

The MR data suggest that the observational association between low testosterone and CV disease is substantially driven by **reverse causation** (illness lowers testosterone) and **confounding** (obesity, diabetes, and poor health lower both testosterone and CV outcomes). The genetic evidence points toward testosterone itself potentially increasing thrombotic and hemodynamic CV risk, while SHBG may be the truly protective factor.[2][8][10]

---

**Mechanistic Pathways**

The mechanisms linking testosterone to CV risk operate in **opposing directions**, which helps explain the paradoxical epidemiologic findings:

**Potentially protective mechanisms of testosterone:**

- **Endothelial function**: Middle-aged/older men with low testosterone (269 ± 48 ng/dL) had significantly reduced brachial artery flow-mediated dilation (3.7% vs. 5.7%) compared to age-matched men with normal testosterone. Acute vitamin C infusion restored FMD in the low-testosterone group, implicating **oxidative stress** as the mediating mechanism. IL-6 and CRP were inversely correlated with FMD.[11]
- **Body composition**: Testosterone reduces visceral fat mass, increases skeletal muscle mass, and improves insulin sensitivity through upregulation of insulin receptor β subunit, IRS-1, and GLUT4 in adipose tissue, and AMPK in skeletal muscle.[12]
- **Anti-inflammatory effects**: Testosterone suppresses proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and NF-κB signaling.[12]
- **Vasodilation**: Testosterone produces acute coronary and peripheral vasodilation through calcium channel antagonism and potassium channel activation.[13]

**Potentially harmful mechanisms of testosterone:**

- **Erythrocytosis/polycythemia**: Testosterone stimulates erythropoiesis by suppressing hepcidin, increasing hematocrit and blood viscosity, which may increase thrombotic risk.[12][13]
- **Thrombosis**: Testosterone may enhance platelet aggregation and thromboxane A2 receptor expression.[13]
- **Blood pressure**: Small but consistent increases in systolic blood pressure observed with TRT.[3]
- **Proarrhythmic effects**: Testosterone shortens QTc interval (potentially antiarrhythmic) but may increase atrial fibrillation risk through mechanisms not yet fully elucidated.[3]

**Primary endpoint (MACE)**: HR 0.96 (95% CI 0.78–1.17; P [3]

**Safety signals requiring attention:**

- **Atrial fibrillation**: 3.5% vs. 2.4% (testosterone vs. placebo) — an unexpected finding. A subsequent TriNetX validation study (n = 4,268) did not replicate the AF signal (RR 1.48, 95% CI 0.93–2.37), suggesting the TRAVERSE finding may have been a chance observation.[15]
- **Acute kidney injury**: 2.3% vs. 1.5% — validated in the TriNetX study (RR 1.53, 95% CI 1.07–2.18), suggesting this is a real signal.[15]
- **Pulmonary embolism**: 0.9% vs. 0.5% — consistent with testosterone's known effects on erythrocytosis and thrombosis.[3]

“Among 5198 patients who received testosterone or placebo for a mean duration of 22 months, testosterone-replacement therapy was noninferior to placebo with respect to the incidence of major adverse cardiac events. The hazard ratio for a primary end-point event was 0.96 (95% CI, 0.78 to 1.17).”

**Testosterone and Metabolic Risk: The T4DM and TRAVERSE Diabetes Substudies**

Two major RCTs have examined testosterone's effects on diabetes prevention:

- **T4DM trial**: In men with dysglycemia, testosterone treatment combined with a lifestyle program reduced the risk of type 2 diabetes by **40% after 2 years**.[1][16]
- **TRAVERSE Diabetes Substudy** (2024): TRT **without** concurrent lifestyle intervention did **not** prevent progression from prediabetes to diabetes, and did not meaningfully improve fasting glucose or HbA1c. This suggests that lifestyle intervention may be required to unlock testosterone's metabolic benefits, or that testosterone has more favorable effects early in the disease course.[17]

A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (n = 1,415) in men with T2DM or metabolic syndrome found TRT reduced HbA1c (MD −0.67%), improved HOMA-IR, decreased LDL and triglycerides, reduced body weight by 3.9 kg, and decreased waist circumference by 2.8 cm.[18]

---

**The Androgen Society Position (2024)**

Following TRAVERSE, the Androgen Society issued a position paper concluding that "it has now been **conclusively determined** that TTh is not associated with increased risks of heart attack, stroke, or CV death," citing the consistency of TRAVERSE with 2 additional large RCTs, multiple smaller RCTs, several large observational studies, and 19 meta-analyses.[19] However, this position has been critiqued as potentially overstating the evidence, given that TRAVERSE was a noninferiority trial (not a superiority trial for benefit), had limited follow-up (mean 33 months), and identified new safety signals.[1]
