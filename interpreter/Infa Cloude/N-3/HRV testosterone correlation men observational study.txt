The evidence linking heart rate variability (HRV) and testosterone in men is **limited to small observational and preclinical studies, with no large population-based studies directly correlating resting HRV metrics (RMSSD, SDNN) with serum testosterone levels**. The available data consistently show that **testosterone deficiency is associated with reduced HRV and impaired cardiovagal function**, but the direction of causality remains unclear, and the effect appears to be mediated primarily through inflammation and autonomic remodeling rather than a direct hormonal–cardiac pacemaker interaction.

---

**Key Observational Evidence**

**Idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IHH) — the clearest human model.** Ermis et al. (2010) compared 24-hour Holter-derived HRV in 22 young men with IHH (mean age ~21) versus 22 age-matched healthy controls. Hypogonadal men had **significantly lower SDNN** (147 ± 56 vs. 194 ± 41 ms, P [1] The reductions in both LF and HF components suggest a **global depression of autonomic modulation** rather than a simple sympathovagal shift. Notably, the authors found significant negative correlations between FSH, LH, testosterone levels and most HRV parameters — though the direction of this correlation (negative) is counterintuitive and may reflect the compensatory gonadotropin elevation in hypogonadism rather than a direct testosterone–HRV relationship.

**Age-related testosterone decline and cardiovagal baroreflex sensitivity.** Babcock et al. (2022) studied 26 men categorized as young (age ~31, testosterone ~535 ng/dL), middle-aged/older with normal testosterone (age ~56, testosterone ~493 ng/dL), and middle-aged/older with low testosterone (age ~57, testosterone ~262 ng/dL). Middle-aged/older men with low testosterone had **dramatically reduced cardiovagal baroreflex sensitivity (cBRS)** — 5.5 ± 3.2 ms/mmHg versus 10.7 ± 4.0 ms/mmHg in age-matched men with normal testosterone (P = 0.039) and 17.0 ± 6.5 ms/mmHg in young men (P = 0.028).[2] Critically, **IL-6 was significantly elevated** in the low-testosterone group and inversely correlated with cBRS (r = −0.594, P = 0.007), suggesting that inflammation — not testosterone per se — may be the proximate mediator of autonomic impairment. Carotid artery compliance was reduced equally in both middle-aged/older groups regardless of testosterone status, indicating that the cBRS difference was neural rather than vascular in origin.

---

**Preclinical Evidence: Testosterone Modulates Vagal Cardiac Control**

Animal studies provide the strongest mechanistic evidence for a direct testosterone–autonomic link:

- **El-Mas et al. (2001)** demonstrated in rats that castration significantly reduced baroreflex sensitivity for reflex bradycardia (BRS: −0.85 vs. −1.51 beats/min/mmHg in sham-operated rats), and testosterone replacement fully restored it. The effect was **mediated through enhancement of cardiomotor vagal (parasympathetic) activity** — muscarinic blockade with atropine caused a 55% reduction in BRS in intact rats, but this atropine-sensitive component was significantly attenuated in castrated rats and restored by testosterone replacement. Beta-adrenergic blockade had no significant effect, indicating the testosterone–autonomic interaction is predominantly **vagal, not sympathetic**.[3]
- **Pongkan et al. (2015)** showed that orchiectomized rats had impaired ejection fraction and HRV, both of which were **restored by 8 weeks of testosterone replacement**. During ischemia-reperfusion, testosterone-replaced rats had lower arrhythmia scores, smaller infarct size, and better cardiac mitochondrial function. The cardioprotective effect was mediated through mitochondrial protection and reduced oxidative stress.[4]

---

**Testosterone Administration and HRV in Humans**

The only placebo-controlled RCT examining testosterone's effect on HRV in humans is the **Lowe et al. (2025)** military operations study. Thirty-two physically active men were randomized to a single injection of testosterone undecanoate or placebo during 20 days of simulated military operations with varying stress levels. - **Testosterone did not significantly affect SDNN, RMSSD, LF, or HF power** — these metrics increased similarly in both groups over time, suggesting adaptation to the exercise protocol

These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that testosterone supports cardiovagal function, and its abrupt withdrawal produces autonomic destabilization — though the metabolic derangements of ADT make it impossible to isolate the direct autonomic effect.

---

**Proposed Mechanistic Framework: Testosterone → HRV**

The available evidence suggests **three pathways** through which testosterone may influence HRV:

1. **Direct vagal enhancement.** Testosterone appears to facilitate cardiomotor vagal activity, increasing parasympathetic tone and baroreflex sensitivity. This is supported by the El-Mas castration/replacement model showing that testosterone's baroreflex effect is atropine-sensitive (vagally mediated). The hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus and dorsomedial nucleus — which receive HPG-axis inputs — project to the nucleus ambiguus (NAmb) and nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), the brainstem centers controlling cardiovagal outflow.[3][13]
2. **Anti-inflammatory mediation.** The Babcock et al. finding that IL-6 inversely correlates with cBRS (r = −0.594) and is elevated in low-testosterone men suggests that testosterone's anti-inflammatory effects may protect autonomic neural pathways from cytokine-mediated damage. Systemic inflammation impairs baroreflex function through effects on the NTS and vagal afferents.[2]
3. **Indirect metabolic effects.** Testosterone deficiency promotes visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome — all of which independently reduce HRV. The Lifelines Cohort Study (n = 149,205) showed that BMI is inversely associated with RMSSD, though the effect size is small (r = −0.10). The metabolic consequences of testosterone deficiency may therefore contribute to HRV reduction through a pathway independent of direct neural modulation.[14][15]

---

**Gonadal Hormones and Stress Reactivity**

Barel et al. (2018) demonstrated that gonadal hormones (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone) modulate both HPA-axis and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) reactivity to psychosocial stress. Controlling for sex hormone levels **diminished the impact of stress on both cortisol and alpha-amylase (an SNS marker) reactivity**, and eliminated the differential cortisol response pattern among stress responders.[16] This suggests that testosterone's influence on autonomic function extends beyond resting HRV to include the **dynamic autonomic stress response** — a finding with implications for understanding why hypogonadal men may have exaggerated cardiovascular responses to stress.

---

**Limitations and Clinical Interpretation**

The HRV–testosterone literature has several critical gaps:

- **No large population-based study** has directly correlated resting HRV metrics with serum testosterone in men. The Lifelines Cohort (n = 149,205) and Fitbit cross-sectional study (n = 8.2 million) characterized HRV determinants extensively but did not include testosterone measurements.[14][17]
- **Age and sex explain ~18–22% of HRV variance**, while lifestyle factors (physical activity, alcohol, smoking) add less than 0.5% additional explanatory power. Where testosterone fits within this variance structure is unknown.[14]
- **Confounding is pervasive** — testosterone, HRV, and cardiovascular risk share common determinants (age, BMI, physical activity, inflammation, sleep quality), making it difficult to establish independent associations.
- **HRV measurement methodology varies widely** across studies (24-hour Holter vs. 5-minute resting vs. exercise HRV; time-domain vs. frequency-domain metrics), limiting comparability.

For clinical purposes, HRV should be considered a **nonspecific marker of autonomic health** that may be reduced in testosterone-deficient men, but it is not validated as a diagnostic or monitoring tool for hypogonadism. The strongest evidence supports testosterone's role in maintaining cardiovagal baroreflex sensitivity — a finding with cardiovascular risk implications given that reduced cBRS independently predicts cardiac mortality — but this has not been translated into clinical HRV-based screening or treatment algorithms.
