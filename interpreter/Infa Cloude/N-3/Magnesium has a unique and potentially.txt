Magnesium has a unique and potentially clinically significant relationship with testosterone in men, operating through a mechanism distinct from other micronutrients: **direct modulation of testosterone bioavailability by displacing testosterone from its binding proteins (SHBG and albumin)**, in addition to supporting testicular steroidogenesis.[1][2] However, the clinical trial evidence remains limited to small, low-quality studies, and no large RCT or meta-analysis has definitively confirmed that magnesium supplementation raises testosterone in men.

---

**Mechanism 1: Magnesium Increases Free/Bioavailable Testosterone via Binding Protein Displacement**

This is the most distinctive aspect of the magnesium–testosterone relationship. Approximately **98% of circulating testosterone is protein-bound** — ~65% to SHBG (high affinity) and ~33% to albumin (low affinity) — with only ~2% circulating as free testosterone.[3] Magnesium modulates this equilibrium at both binding sites:

**SHBG displacement.** Using a novel biochromatographic approach with immobilized SHBG, Excoffon et al. demonstrated that Mg²⁺ binds to **negatively charged, nonspecific areas of SHBG** via electrostatic attraction, producing an **uncompetitive inhibition** of the testosterone–SHBG interaction. Within the biological magnesium concentration range (0.75–0.95 mM), increasing magnesium concentration reduces testosterone–SHBG binding affinity, thereby enhancing bioavailable testosterone. The thermodynamic data showed the Mg²⁺ binding heat effect was approximately 17 kJ/mol, consistent with electrostatic attraction. Each SHBG monomer contains three divalent cation–binding sites (one calcium, two zinc), and magnesium likely competes at or near these sites.[2] Importantly, the effect is modest — the number of Mg²⁺ ions linked to each binding event is low — but operates continuously across the physiological magnesium range, meaning even small changes in serum magnesium could shift the free testosterone fraction.[2]

**Albumin displacement.** Guillaume's group demonstrated in vitro that adequate magnesium concentrations **displaced testosterone from its human serum albumin (HSA) binding site**. Since DHEA and testosterone share the same HSA binding cavity, magnesium may also potentiate the DHEA-mediated displacement of testosterone from albumin, further increasing bioavailable testosterone.[1]

The net effect of these two mechanisms is that magnesium acts as a **modulator of testosterone bioavailability** without necessarily changing total testosterone production — a distinction that is clinically important because free and bioavailable testosterone are the biologically active fractions that correlate with symptoms and tissue effects.

---

**Mechanism 2: Support of Testicular Steroidogenesis**

Magnesium also directly supports testosterone biosynthesis within Leydig cells:

- In animal models, magnesium supplementation significantly enhanced the activity of **Δ5-3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD)** and **17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (17β-HSD)** — two key enzymes in the testosterone biosynthetic pathway — resulting in increased serum testosterone levels. The effects were dose- and duration-dependent, with longer treatment and higher doses producing more pronounced results.[1]
- Magnesium depletion in animal models targets testicular and epididymal tissue, and age-related testosterone decline in animal models has been associated with disrupted cation distribution (including magnesium) in the testis and accessory glands.[1]

---

**Mechanism 3: Anti-Inflammatory and Antioxidant Effects**

Magnesium deficiency promotes low-grade systemic inflammation and oxidative stress — both of which independently suppress testosterone production at the hypothalamic, pituitary, and testicular levels. Magnesium is essential for maintaining antioxidant capacity and controlling reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Inadequate intracellular magnesium reduces mitochondrial efficiency and increases ROS, causing structural and functional impairment of proteins and DNA. Both magnesium and testosterone are reduced during systemic inflammation, suggesting a shared vulnerability to inflammatory insult.[1]

---

**Observational Evidence**

The **InCHIANTI Study** (n = 399 men ≥65 years) provided the strongest observational data, demonstrating a **strong, independent positive association between serum magnesium and total testosterone** (and IGF-1) in older men. This relationship persisted after adjustment for BMI, IL-6, DHEAS, SHBG, insulin, IGF-1, grip strength, and comorbidities.[1]

A study of 313 men aged 50–75 years confirmed that men with total testosterone deficiency had **significantly lower serum magnesium** concentrations, with a positive correlation between magnesium and total testosterone (TT-Mg correlation).[4]

Key observations from these trials:

- **Cinar et al.** found that 4 weeks of magnesium supplementation (10 mg/kg/day) increased both free and total testosterone in sedentary men and athletes, with the **greatest increases in men who both exercised and received magnesium**. This suggests a synergistic effect between exercise and magnesium on testosterone.[5]
- The **Brilla & Conte ZMA trial** showed a 33% increase in free testosterone in supplemented athletes versus a 10% decline in placebo, but this study used a combination supplement (zinc + magnesium + B6), making it impossible to isolate the magnesium effect.[1]
- The **Maggio pilot RCT** in elderly hypomagnesemic men used only a single IV dose of magnesium sulfate and found that total testosterone was preserved in the treatment group while it declined in the placebo group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.12), and bioavailable testosterone did not change.[1]

No large, well-powered, placebo-controlled RCT of oral magnesium supplementation alone with testosterone as the primary endpoint has been conducted in men.

---

**Prevalence of Magnesium Deficiency: A Highly Underrecognized Problem**

The clinical relevance of the magnesium–testosterone relationship is amplified by the remarkably high prevalence of magnesium insufficiency:

- **Overt hypomagnesemia** ([6]
- **Chronic latent magnesium deficiency (CLMD)** — defined as serum magnesium [7]
- Approximately **50% of Americans consume less than the Estimated Average Requirement** for magnesium.[8]
- Critically, **serum magnesium is a poor marker of total body magnesium** — less than 1% of body magnesium is in plasma, and intracellular stores can be depleted even when serum levels appear normal. Controlled depletion–repletion studies confirm this disconnect.[6]

The **RDA for magnesium in adult men is 400–420 mg/day**, with the tolerable upper intake level for supplemental magnesium set at 350 mg/day (this does not include dietary magnesium).[8]

---

**Clinical Implications**

The magnesium–testosterone relationship is biologically plausible and supported by consistent observational data, but the interventional evidence remains **preliminary and insufficient to recommend magnesium supplementation specifically for testosterone optimization**. Key takeaways:

- Magnesium's most distinctive mechanism — **displacement of testosterone from SHBG and albumin** — could increase bioavailable testosterone without changing total testosterone, meaning standard total testosterone assays may miss the effect. This has important implications for study design: future trials should measure free and bioavailable testosterone, not just total testosterone.[1][2]
- Given the extraordinarily high prevalence of chronic latent magnesium deficiency (~68% of US adults), correcting magnesium insufficiency is reasonable as part of comprehensive metabolic optimization in men with low testosterone, even in the absence of definitive RCT evidence for testosterone-specific benefit.[7]
- The synergistic effect with exercise observed by Cinar et al. suggests that magnesium supplementation may be most beneficial in physically active men or those initiating exercise programs.[5]
- Magnesium supplementation at recommended doses (200–400 mg/day elemental magnesium) has an excellent safety profile and provides well-established benefits for cardiovascular health, insulin sensitivity, and bone metabolism independent of any testosterone effect.[6]
