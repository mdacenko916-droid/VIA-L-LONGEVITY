Boron supplementation has generated considerable interest for its effects on steroid hormones, with the most notable finding being a **rapid decrease in SHBG and increase in free testosterone within days of supplementation at 6–10 mg/day** in men.[1] However, the clinical evidence base is remarkably thin — consisting of only a handful of small, uncontrolled or poorly controlled human studies — and the hormonal effects appear to be highly context-dependent, varying by sex, dose, and baseline nutritional status.

---

**Proposed Mechanisms of Action**

**1. SHBG displacement (primary mechanism).** Bello et al. hypothesized that boric acid disrupts the interaction between steroid hormones and their plasma carrier proteins — specifically **uncoupling testosterone and estrogens from SHBG** — thereby increasing the free (bioavailable) fraction of these hormones.[2] This mechanism is analogous to the magnesium–SHBG displacement discussed previously, but operates through a different chemical interaction: boron's ability to form **boroester complexes with cis-hydroxyl groups** on glycoproteins (SHBG is a glycoprotein) may alter the protein's conformation or binding affinity for steroid ligands.[3] Importantly, this mechanism would increase free testosterone without necessarily changing total testosterone production — explaining why some studies show free testosterone increases without total testosterone changes.

**2. Inhibition of catechol estrogen methylation.** Beattie & Weersink demonstrated in vitro that borate inhibits **catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT)**, the enzyme responsible for methylation (and thus inactivation) of catechol estrogens.[4] By slowing catechol estrogen catabolism, boron could theoretically increase circulating estrogen levels. However, a subsequent in vivo study in ovariectomized rats found **no effect of dietary boron on estradiol catabolism or catechol estrogen metabolism**, failing to confirm this mechanism.[5]

**3. Anti-inflammatory effects.** The Naghii et al. study found that boron supplementation significantly reduced **hsCRP, TNF-α, and IL-6** within hours of a single 10 mg dose, with further reductions after 7 days.[1] Since systemic inflammation suppresses HPG-axis function and testosterone production, boron's anti-inflammatory properties may indirectly support testosterone levels.

**4. Vitamin D metabolism.** Boron influences the hydroxylation of vitamin D, potentially increasing 25(OH)D levels.[6] Given the mechanistic (though clinically unproven) link between vitamin D and testosterone discussed previously, this represents another indirect pathway.

**5. Steroidogenic enzyme modulation.** Boron influences the activity of metabolic enzymes involved in steroid hormone metabolism, though the specific enzymes and mechanisms remain incompletely characterized.[6]

The most frequently cited study — **Naghii et al. (2011)** — is the only human trial to report a significant increase in free testosterone in men with boron supplementation.[1] However, this study has critical limitations that substantially weaken its conclusions:

- **No placebo control or blinding** — an open-label design with only 8 subjects
- **No washout period** — the "placebo day" (day 0) served as the baseline, followed immediately by supplementation
- **Very short duration** — only 7 days of supplementation
- **No dietary control** — subjects consumed their habitual diets
- **Supraphysiological dose** — 10 mg/day is at or above the EFSA tolerable upper intake level

In contrast, the only **placebo-controlled trial in men** — Green & Ferrando (1994) — found **no effect whatsoever** of 2.5 mg/day boron supplementation for 7 weeks on total testosterone, free testosterone, lean body mass, or strength in young male bodybuilders.[7] The authors concluded that "boron supplementation affects these variables not at all."

The **Naghii & Samman (1997)** study in 18 healthy men found that 10 mg/day for 4 weeks significantly increased **estradiol** (not testosterone), with only a non-significant trend toward testosterone elevation.[8] This is an important finding because it suggests that boron's primary hormonal effect in men may be **estrogenic rather than androgenic** — a concern for men seeking testosterone optimization.

---

**The Estradiol Paradox: Boron May Raise Estrogen More Than Testosterone**

A critical and often overlooked finding across the boron literature is that the most consistent hormonal effect of boron supplementation is an **increase in estradiol**, not testosterone:

- In postmenopausal women, 3 mg/day boron markedly elevated both 17β-estradiol and testosterone, with the estradiol effect being the more prominent finding.[10][11]
- In healthy men, 10 mg/day for 4 weeks significantly increased estradiol (P [8]
- In ovariectomized rats, boron supplementation increased estradiol concentration and upregulated estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) expression in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating clear **estrogen-modulatory effects**.[12]
- The Naghii 2011 study is the outlier, reporting decreased estradiol alongside increased free testosterone after 7 days — but this was uncontrolled and may reflect acute vs. chronic effects.[1]

This pattern suggests that boron may function more as a **steroid hormone bioavailability enhancer** (displacing both testosterone and estradiol from SHBG) rather than a selective testosterone booster. In men with adequate aromatase activity, the net effect could be a proportionally greater increase in bioavailable estradiol than testosterone — potentially counterproductive for men with symptoms of relative estrogen excess.

---

**Dosing and Safety**

- The **WHO acceptable safe range** of population mean intakes for boron is **1–13 mg/day**.[13][14]
- The **EFSA tolerable upper intake level** is **10 mg/day** for adults.[15]
- Typical dietary intake is **1–2 mg/day** in Western diets, with higher intakes (up to 7 mg/day) in diets rich in fruits, vegetables, nuts, and legumes.[3][14]
- A supplemental dose of **3 mg/day** has been most consistently studied for bone health and is well below the upper limit.[15]
- Boron has **high oral bioavailability** — approximately 84% of a supplemented dose is recovered in urine.[8]
- At high doses, boron has **reproductive toxicity** in animal models: the critical NOAEL for reproductive effects is 9.6 mg boron/kg body weight/day, with epididymal toxicity as the most sensitive endpoint. This is far above supplemental doses, but underscores the importance of not exceeding recommended intakes.[16]
- Intakes below **1 mg/day** appear to inhibit the health benefits of boron, and dietary surveys indicate such low intakes are not uncommon.[3]

---

**Clinical Implications**

The evidence for boron as a testosterone-boosting supplement in men is **weak and inconsistent**. The single positive study (Naghii 2011) was small, uncontrolled, and used a dose at the upper safety limit, while the only placebo-controlled trial in men (Green & Ferrando 1994) was entirely negative.[1][7] The more consistent finding across studies is an increase in estradiol, which may be undesirable in men with functional hypogonadism or symptoms of estrogen excess.

Boron supplementation at **3 mg/day** is reasonable for bone health and general mineral metabolism, particularly in individuals with low dietary intake ([15][3] However, recommending boron specifically for testosterone optimization is not supported by the current evidence. The mechanistic hypothesis of SHBG displacement is plausible but unconfirmed by rigorous clinical trials, and the potential for proportionally greater estradiol elevation warrants caution.[2][8]
