Chronic stress suppresses testosterone in men primarily through **HPA axis–mediated inhibition of the HPG axis at multiple levels**, and the **cortisol-to-testosterone (C/T) ratio** has emerged as a composite biomarker reflecting the balance between catabolic stress and anabolic reproductive function.[1][2][3]

---

**Mechanisms of HPA–HPG Cross-Talk**

Glucocorticoids and CRH exert inhibitory effects on the male reproductive axis at every level — hypothalamus, pituitary, and testis:[2][4]

**Hypothalamic suppression.** CRH neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) potently suppress pulsatile GnRH/LH secretion. This occurs partly through **CRH receptor type 1 (CRHR1)** signaling — particularly in psychogenic stress — and through **urocortin 2/CRHR2** signaling, which directly suppresses arcuate kisspeptin neurons, the master regulators of GnRH pulsatility.[5][6][7] Chronic glucocorticoid exposure reduces LH pulse frequency in primates and other species.[8] Importantly, CRH appears to suppress LH pulsatility **indirectly** rather than through direct inhibition of GnRH neurons themselves; the precise intermediary pathways (including GABAergic, noradrenergic, and RFRP-3/GnIH neurons) remain under active investigation.[7][9]

**Pituitary suppression.** Glucocorticoids inhibit gonadotropin (LH and FSH) synthesis and release from pituitary gonadotropes via glucocorticoid receptor–mediated mechanisms.[2][4]

**Testicular suppression.** Glucocorticoids act directly on Leydig cells through several mechanisms:[10][4]

- Inhibition of steroidogenic enzymes (StAR, CYP11A1, 17β-HSD), reducing testosterone biosynthesis
- Promotion of **Leydig cell apoptosis** when glucocorticoid levels exceed the protective capacity of **11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (11β-HSD)**, the enzyme that inactivates cortisol/corticosterone within the testis
- Chronic stress also damages Leydig cell mitochondria via downregulation of **Atp5a1**, further impairing steroidogenesis[11]

Additionally, the newly characterized **gonadotropin-inhibitory hormone (GnIH/RFRP-3)** is upregulated by stress and exerts an independent inhibitory effect on the HPG axis.[12]

**The Cortisol-to-Testosterone Ratio as a Biomarker**

The **C/T ratio** reflects the net balance between HPA (catabolic) and HPG (anabolic) axis activity and has been studied across several contexts:

**Overtraining and military stress.** The C/T ratio has long been proposed as an indicator of physiological strain. The ACSM/ECSS consensus statement notes that the ratio **decreases in relation to training intensity and duration** (reflecting rising cortisol and falling testosterone), but emphasizes that it indicates **current physiological strain rather than a diagnostic marker of overtraining syndrome**.[13][14] Military field studies confirm that the bedtime C/T ratio decreases during sustained operational stress (sleep deprivation, energy deficit, physical exertion) and may not fully recover even after several days of rest.[15]

**Metabolic disease.** An increased C/T ratio has been positively correlated with **insulin resistance, coronary heart disease risk, and metabolic syndrome** parameters in men.[16] In men with diabetes-associated late-onset hypogonadism, functional hypercortisolism (FH) — defined by HPA axis dysregulation — is associated with significantly **worse metabolic profiles** (higher glycemia, triglycerides, lower HDL) and greater abdominal fat accumulation, even after adjusting for BMI and testosterone levels.[17][18]

**Sexual function.** In men with concurrent diabetes and late-onset hypogonadism, functional hypercortisolism is independently associated with **worse erectile function and lower IIEF scores**, with cortisol AUC negatively correlating with erectile function scores (β = −0.0008, P = .015).[19]

---

**Acute vs. Chronic Stress: Divergent Temporal Dynamics**

The relationship between cortisol and testosterone is **time-dependent and context-specific**:[20][21]

- **Acute stress** (minutes to hours): Cortisol and testosterone often rise **simultaneously** (positive contemporaneous correlation), reflecting a coordinated mobilization response. Salivary data from athletic and healthy men show a contemporaneous positive correlation (r = 0.04–0.46).[20]
- **Lagged effect** (hours to days): A rising cortisol concentration predicts a **fall in testosterone at later time points**, with the inhibitory effect peaking at approximately **0.5–0.7 days** and persisting for around 3 days.[20]
- **Prolonged stress** (weeks to months): Sustained stress produces a progressive decline in morning testosterone. In military officer cadets, morning testosterone decreased over the first 12 weeks of training but recovered by week 15 when stress diminished — demonstrating the **reversibility** of the suppression.[21]
- **Chronic stress** (months to years): Prolonged unpredictable stress in animal models produces cumulative, dose-dependent testicular damage — including progressive disruption of the blood-testis barrier, reduced spermatogenesis, and declining FSH, LH, and testosterone — with severity proportional to both magnitude and duration of stress exposure.[22]

---

**Stress-Associated Testosterone Suppression: Adaptation vs. Pathology**

A 2026 review in JCEM argues that stress-associated testosterone suppression should be recognized as a **centrally mediated, reversible adaptive phenotype** rather than true hypogonadism.[1] Key evidence supporting this distinction:

- **HCG stimulation studies** in energy-depleted military personnel and endurance athletes confirm **preserved Leydig cell responsiveness**, indicating the suppression is hypothalamic-pituitary in origin, not gonadal failure.[1]
- Testosterone suppression tracks **energetic and psychological constraint** and reverses with restoration of energy balance and recovery.[1]
- **Energy deficit, not exercise modality**, is the principal determinant — resistance-trained men do not suppress basal testosterone when energy availability is maintained.[1]

This distinction has direct clinical implications: men presenting with low testosterone in the context of chronic stress, energy deficit, or overtraining should not be reflexively diagnosed with hypogonadism or started on testosterone replacement. Instead, the underlying stressors — particularly **energy availability, sleep, and psychological stress** — should be addressed first, with reassessment of testosterone after recovery.[1]

---

**Clinical Implications**

- The C/T ratio is a useful **research and monitoring tool** reflecting physiological strain but is **not validated as a standalone diagnostic marker** for overtraining or hypogonadism.[14][3]
- In men with diabetes or metabolic syndrome and concurrent hypogonadism, screening for **functional hypercortisolism** (via dexamethasone suppression or urinary free cortisol) may identify a subgroup with worse metabolic and sexual outcomes who may benefit from targeted cortisol-lowering strategies.[17][19]
- Stress-associated testosterone suppression is typically **reversible** with correction of energy deficit, sleep restoration, and stress management — distinguishing it from organic hypogonadism that requires hormonal replacement.[1]
