The direct association between **resting heart rate (RHR) and testosterone levels in men has not been established in large population-based studies**. A Mendelian randomization analysis of UK Biobank data found **no causal association between genetically predicted testosterone concentrations and resting heart rate** in men.[1] However, the relationship between testosterone and cardiac chronotropy is more nuanced than a simple RHR correlation — the evidence points to testosterone's effects on **autonomic modulation of heart rate** (particularly vagal tone and baroreflex sensitivity) rather than on baseline heart rate itself.

---

**Mendelian Randomization: No Causal Link to RHR**

Wang et al. (2024) used large GWAS data from the UK Biobank to examine causal relationships between sex hormones and ECG indices in men. Genetically predicted total testosterone showed **no significant association with resting heart rate, P wave duration, PR interval, QRS duration, QTc interval, or ST duration** in males.[1] The only significant finding was that higher genetically predicted total testosterone was associated with **lower heart rate variability** — a finding that appears paradoxical given the observational data showing reduced HRV in hypogonadal men, and may reflect the complex nonlinear relationship between testosterone and autonomic function (both low and high testosterone may impair autonomic modulation).[1]

Similarly, the Lundberg et al. (1990) correlational study of 30 healthy men (mean age ~41) found **no systematic relationship between serum testosterone or estradiol and resting heart rate** in men, though positive correlations between testosterone and both blood pressure and heart rate were observed in women.[2]

---

**Testosterone and Cardiac Chronotropy: Preclinical Evidence**

Despite the absence of a population-level RHR–testosterone association, preclinical data suggest testosterone has direct effects on cardiac pacemaker function:

**Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) produces negative chronotropy.** Sánchez et al. (2009) demonstrated in isolated rat right atria that acute administration of 5α-dihydrotestosterone (a non-aromatizable androgen) elicited a **negative chronotropic effect** (slowing of spontaneous beating rate) combined with a positive inotropic effect (increased contractile force). DHT increased the **sinoatrial node recovery time**, suggesting a direct effect on the mechanisms of spontaneous diastolic depolarization involved in atrial pacemaking. This effect was not hormonally regulated — it occurred similarly in estrogenized females and gonadectomized animals — indicating a direct, nongenomic action on sinoatrial cells.[3]

This finding suggests that testosterone, through its conversion to DHT, may have a **direct heart rate–lowering effect** at the sinoatrial node level. However, this acute pharmacological effect in isolated tissue does not necessarily translate to measurable RHR differences in vivo, where multiple compensatory mechanisms (autonomic reflexes, catecholamines, metabolic rate) modulate heart rate.

---

**Testosterone and Autonomic Heart Rate Control: The Baroreflex Connection**

The strongest evidence linking testosterone to heart rate regulation comes from studies of **cardiovagal baroreflex sensitivity (cBRS)** — the reflex slowing of heart rate in response to blood pressure increases — rather than resting heart rate per se.

**Babcock et al. (2022)** showed that middle-aged/older men with low testosterone (262 ± 31 ng/dL) had dramatically reduced cBRS compared to age-matched men with normal testosterone (5.5 vs. 10.7 ms/mmHg, P = 0.039). IL-6 was elevated in the low-testosterone group and inversely correlated with cBRS (r = −0.594, P = 0.007), suggesting inflammation as a mediating pathway.[4] Importantly, resting R-R intervals (the inverse of heart rate) were not reported as significantly different between groups — the deficit was in the **dynamic responsiveness** of heart rate to perturbation, not in the resting set point.

**El-Mas et al. (2001)** provided the mechanistic basis: castration in rats reduced baroreflex sensitivity for reflex bradycardia by ~44% (BRS: −0.85 vs. −1.51 beats/min/mmHg), and testosterone replacement fully restored it. The effect was **mediated through enhancement of cardiac vagal (parasympathetic) activity** — atropine blocked 55% of the baroreflex response in intact rats, but this vagal component was significantly attenuated after castration and restored by testosterone.[5]

**Caminiti et al. (2009)** translated this to humans in a double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of long-acting intramuscular testosterone in elderly men with chronic heart failure (CHF). Testosterone supplementation significantly **improved baroreflex sensitivity**, consistent with enhanced vagal cardiac control. The authors noted that animal studies show testosterone facilitates baroreceptor control of heart rate through enhancement of cardiac efferent vagal activity, likely acting at brainstem nuclei (where androgen receptors have been characterized) involved in baroreflex cardiac regulation.[6]

---

**Testosterone Replacement and Heart Rate Turbulence**

**Poliwczak et al. (2014)** studied 80 men, including 30 with metabolic syndrome and testosterone deficiency syndrome (MS+TDS+), who received testosterone replacement (Omnadren 250 IM) for 9 weeks. Men with MS+TDS+ had **irregular heart rate turbulence (HRT) parameters** at baseline compared to healthy controls. Testosterone replacement **improved HRT** — a marker of autonomic cardiac regulation that reflects the baroreflex-mediated heart rate response to premature ventricular contractions. Abnormal HRT is an independent predictor of cardiac mortality. The authors concluded that testosterone deficiency may increase ventricular arrhythmia frequency by **inhibiting the parasympathetic system**, and that testosterone replacement restores autonomic balance.[7]

---

**Testosterone and QTc Interval: A More Robust Electrophysiological Association**

While the RHR–testosterone link is weak, the association between testosterone and **QTc interval** is better established. Testosterone shortens the QT interval through stimulation of the ultra-rapid potassium channel–operated repolarization current in cardiac myocytes.[8][9] This has clinical implications:

- Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) for prostate cancer is associated with **QT/QTc prolongation** and increased arrhythmia risk[10]
- The UK Biobank analysis (n = 173,498 men) found that the lowest quartile of testosterone was associated with increased risk of **bradyarrhythmia** (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.02–1.20) and atrial fibrillation (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.00–1.12), while higher free testosterone was associated with increased AF and ventricular arrhythmia risk — suggesting a **U-shaped relationship** between testosterone and arrhythmia risk[10]

---

**Why RHR May Not Correlate Directly With Testosterone**

Several factors explain the disconnect between testosterone's clear effects on autonomic cardiac regulation and the absence of a measurable RHR association:

1. **RHR is multiply determined.** Age, BMI, physical fitness, sleep, medications, and genetic factors collectively explain the vast majority of RHR variance. The Quer et al. (2019) study of 92,457 adults with wearable-derived RHR data showed that mean RHR ranged from 40 to 109 bpm across individuals, with age, sex, BMI, and sleep duration as the primary determinants. Any testosterone effect would be small relative to these dominant influences.[11]
2. **Testosterone affects dynamic autonomic responsiveness, not the resting set point.** The baroreflex and HRV data consistently show that testosterone modulates the **gain** of autonomic cardiac control (how effectively heart rate responds to perturbation) rather than the tonic resting rate. A man with low testosterone may have a normal RHR but impaired ability to mount appropriate heart rate responses to hemodynamic challenges.
3. **Opposing direct and indirect effects.** Testosterone has a direct negative chronotropic effect on sinoatrial cells (tending to slow heart rate) but also reduces systemic vascular resistance through vasodilation (which would reflexively increase heart rate to maintain cardiac output). These opposing effects may cancel out at the level of resting heart rate.[3][8]
4. **Nonlinear relationships.** Both low and high testosterone are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. A U-shaped relationship would not produce a simple linear correlation with RHR in population studies.[12][13]

---

**Clinical Implications**

Resting heart rate is **not a useful clinical marker for testosterone status** in men. The cardiovascular autonomic consequences of testosterone deficiency are better captured by:

- **Heart rate variability** (particularly SDNN, RMSSD, and HF power) — reduced in hypogonadal men[14]
- **Cardiovagal baroreflex sensitivity** — significantly impaired in men with low testosterone, independent of vascular stiffness[4]
- **Heart rate turbulence** — abnormal in testosterone-deficient men with metabolic syndrome, improved by testosterone replacement[7]
- **QTc interval** — prolonged in testosterone deficiency, shortened by testosterone replacement[8]

These measures reflect the **dynamic autonomic cardiac control** that testosterone modulates, rather than the static resting heart rate. For clinical assessment of cardiovascular autonomic function in men with suspected hypogonadism, HRV analysis or baroreflex testing would be more informative than isolated RHR measurement.
