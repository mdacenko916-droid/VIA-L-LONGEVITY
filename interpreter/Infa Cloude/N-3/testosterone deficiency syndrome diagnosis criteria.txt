### Practice Guideline

The **American Urological Association (AUA)** recommends that the diagnosis of testosterone deficiency (TD) requires **both consistently low serum total testosterone levels and the presence of symptoms and/or signs** — neither criterion alone is sufficient.[1]

**Biochemical Criteria**

- A total testosterone level **below 300 ng/dL** is the recommended diagnostic cut-off (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).[1]
- The diagnosis should be made only after **two total testosterone measurements on separate occasions**, both drawn in the **early morning** and preferably using the same laboratory and assay method (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade A).[1]

**Clinical Criteria**

Symptoms associated with TD include **reduced sex drive, changes in erectile function**, reduced energy, reduced endurance, diminished work and/or physical performance, fatigue, depression, reduced motivation, poor concentration, impaired memory, irritability, and infertility.[1] Visual field changes (bitemporal hemianopsia) and anosmia may indicate pituitary or hypothalamic pathology.[1]

The physical examination should evaluate general body habitus, virilization status (body hair patterns), BMI or waist circumference, gynecomastia, testicular presence/size/consistency, varicocele, and prostate size and morphology.[1]

**Special Populations for Testing**

The AUA recommends measuring testosterone even in the **absence of classic symptoms** in men with unexplained anemia, bone density loss, diabetes, prior chemotherapy or testicular radiation, HIV/AIDS, chronic narcotic use, male infertility, pituitary dysfunction, or chronic corticosteroid use (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).[1]

**Screening Questionnaires**

Validated questionnaires (e.g., ADAM) are **not recommended** for defining candidates for testosterone therapy or monitoring symptom response, due to variable sensitivity and specificity (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C).[1]

**Endocrine Society Criteria**

The Endocrine Society similarly requires **symptoms plus unequivocally low testosterone on two fasting morning samples** (drawn between 7–10 AM).[2] The harmonized lower limit of normal using CDC-certified assays is **264 ng/dL (9.2 nmol/L)**, though many laboratories use 300 ng/dL as a practical threshold.[2][3] Free testosterone should be assessed when SHBG-altering conditions are present (obesity, diabetes, aging, liver disease) or when total testosterone is borderline — using equilibrium dialysis or calculated methods, never direct analog immunoassays.[2]

**Society for Endocrinology / ACLM (UK) Thresholds**

The 2024 joint position statement from the Society for Endocrinology and Association of Clinical Biochemistry and Laboratory Medicine uses a tiered approach due to immunoassay variability across UK laboratories:[4]

- **12 nmol/L (~346 ng/dL):** Usually excludes hypogonadism; even a single value >12 nmol/L generally rules out the diagnosis

Calculated free testosterone adds diagnostic value **only when SHBG is outside the reference range** — it has no incremental value when SHBG is normal.[4]

**Distinguishing Primary vs. Secondary Hypogonadism**

Once TD is biochemically confirmed, **FSH and LH** should be measured:[5][6]

- **Elevated LH/FSH** → primary (testicular) hypogonadism
- **Low or inappropriately normal LH/FSH** → secondary (hypothalamic-pituitary) hypogonadism; measure **prolactin** to evaluate for pituitary adenoma

**Functional vs. Organic Hypogonadism**

A critical diagnostic distinction is between **organic hypogonadism** (structural/genetic causes such as Klinefelter syndrome, pituitary tumors, or bilateral orchidectomy) and **functional hypogonadism**, where testosterone suppression is driven by reversible factors — most commonly **obesity, type 2 diabetes, opioid use, or acute illness**.[7][3] Functional hypogonadism should prompt treatment of the underlying cause (e.g., weight loss) before considering testosterone replacement.[7][3]
