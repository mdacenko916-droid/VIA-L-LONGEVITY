The relationship between insulin resistance and testosterone in men is **bidirectional and self-perpetuating**: low testosterone promotes insulin resistance through impaired glucose metabolism and visceral fat accumulation, while insulin resistance suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis and reduces testosterone production — creating a vicious cycle that drives both metabolic and reproductive dysfunction.[1][2][3]

---

**Direction 1: How Insulin Resistance Suppresses Testosterone**

Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinemia suppress testosterone through multiple converging mechanisms:

**Central (hypothalamic-pituitary) suppression.** Insulin normally acts on hypothalamic neurons to facilitate GnRH release. In insulin-resistant states, impaired hypothalamic insulin signaling reduces GnRH pulsatility and LH secretion. Neuron-specific insulin receptor knockout mice show a 60–90% decrease in serum LH, with preserved pituitary responsiveness to exogenous GnRH — confirming the defect is at the hypothalamic level.[4] Experimentally, the combination of hyperinsulinemia and elevated lipids (mimicking metabolic syndrome) acutely suppresses both LH and FSH in healthy men, while neither alone is sufficient — suggesting that the metabolic milieu acts synergistically.[5]

**Testicular suppression.** Insulin resistance directly impairs Leydig cell steroidogenesis. Chronic hyperinsulinemia induces **DAX-1** (dosage-sensitive sex reversal gene), a transcriptional repressor of steroidogenic enzymes, thereby reducing testosterone synthesis at the testicular level.[6] Pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) elevated in insulin-resistant states further inhibit Leydig cell function.[4][1]

**SHBG suppression.** Hyperinsulinemia suppresses hepatic SHBG synthesis, lowering total testosterone. While this initially increases free testosterone availability (temporarily masking the deficiency), the excess free testosterone is shunted to aromatization in visceral adipose tissue, ultimately depleting both total and free testosterone.[1][7]

**Direction 2: How Low Testosterone Causes Insulin Resistance**

Testosterone deficiency promotes insulin resistance through both body composition changes and direct molecular effects on insulin signaling:

**Body composition effects.** Testosterone deficiency promotes preferential visceral fat deposition and loss of lean muscle mass. Since skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin-mediated glucose disposal, the shift from muscle to fat mass directly worsens insulin sensitivity.[8][3] Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in prostate cancer provides a "natural experiment" — ADT increases fat mass, insulin resistance, and the risk of incident type 2 diabetes, confirming a causal role of testosterone deficiency.[9]

**Molecular mechanisms in target tissues.** Adipose tissue biopsies from hypogonadal men with type 2 diabetes show reduced expression of key insulin signaling proteins — **insulin receptor β subunit, IRS-1, AKT-2, and GLUT-4** — all of which are restored after 24 weeks of testosterone replacement.[10][11] In skeletal muscle, testosterone increases **AMPK expression and phosphorylation** (the same pathway activated by metformin and exercise), which independently promotes glucose uptake via GLUT-4 translocation.[12] Testosterone also reduces circulating free fatty acids, which otherwise induce oxidative stress and interfere with insulin signal transduction.[10]

**Anti-inflammatory effects.** Testosterone replacement reduces CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and leptin while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 — collectively reducing the inflammatory milieu that drives insulin resistance.[11][13]

---

**Clinical Evidence: Effect of Testosterone Replacement on Insulin Resistance**

The metabolic effects of testosterone replacement on insulin resistance show a **nuanced pattern** — improvement in insulin sensitivity measures but limited translation to glycemic endpoints:

- A meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (1,415 patients) found TRT significantly improved **HOMA-IR (SMD −1.94)**, reduced HbA1c by 0.67%, fasting glucose by 0.86 mmol/L, and fasting insulin by 1.23 units, along with reductions in body weight (−3.9 kg) and waist circumference (−2.8 cm).[13]
- The Moscow Study RCT demonstrated that testosterone undecanoate reduced HOMA-IR significantly after 18 weeks, with progressive improvement through 138 weeks (fasting insulin decreased by 35.5%). Baseline HOMA-IR was the strongest predictor of improvement (r² ≥ 0.64), suggesting men with the greatest insulin resistance derive the most benefit.[14]
- The TIMES2 trial showed TRT reduced HOMA-IR by **15.2% at 6 months** in hypogonadal men with T2DM/MetS.[15]
- A euglycemic clamp study demonstrated that hypogonadal men with T2DM had **35% greater insulin resistance** than eugonadal diabetic men, and testosterone replacement increased insulin sensitivity by **32% over 24 weeks** — comparable to the effect of thiazolidinediones or significant weight loss.[11][10]

However, the **TRAVERSE Diabetes substudy** — the largest placebo-controlled RCT (5,204 men) — found that TRT **did not prevent progression from prediabetes to diabetes** and did not significantly improve fasting glucose or HbA1c, suggesting that testosterone's insulin-sensitizing effects may be insufficient to alter hard glycemic endpoints without concurrent lifestyle intervention.[9]

“The findings of this study suggest that TRT alone should not be used as a therapeutic intervention to prevent or treat diabetes in men with hypogonadism.”

---

**Clinical Implications**

The bidirectional relationship has direct management implications: in men with obesity/MetS and functional hypogonadism, **addressing insulin resistance and obesity should be the first-line approach** before considering testosterone replacement.[7] Weight loss through lifestyle modification or pharmacotherapy (including GLP-1RAs) can restore endogenous testosterone production while preserving fertility — an important consideration in men of reproductive age.[17][19] Testosterone replacement improves insulin sensitivity and body composition but, based on the TRAVERSE data, should not be relied upon as a standalone metabolic intervention.[9]
