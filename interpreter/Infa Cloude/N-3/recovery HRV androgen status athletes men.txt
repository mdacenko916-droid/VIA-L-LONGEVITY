The evidence linking recovery HRV and androgen status in male athletes is **convergent but indirect** — no study has directly demonstrated that testosterone levels predict post-exercise HRV recovery kinetics. Instead, the data show that **HRV and testosterone respond in parallel to training load**, both reflecting the balance between adaptive stress and recovery, and that **HRV-guided training produces superior hormonal outcomes** compared to predetermined training schedules.[1] The testosterone/cortisol (T/C) ratio, once considered a marker of overtraining, is now understood to reflect **acute physiological strain rather than a diagnostic threshold for overtraining syndrome (OTS)**.[2][3]

---

**HRV and Cortisol in Young Athletes: HRV Is More Sensitive Than Hormones**

**Mishica et al. (2021)** monitored 8 young endurance athletes (age ~16) over 7 weeks during training and competition seasons, measuring nocturnal RMSSD, salivary cortisol, and sleep duration biweekly. Key findings:[5]

- **HRV and cortisol were inversely correlated** throughout the study (r = −0.552, P = 0.01), with the strongest correlation during the highest training load week (r = −0.879, P = 0.01)
- Changes in easy training volume (K1) positively correlated with HRV changes during weeks 1–3 (r = 0.863, P = 0.006), then negatively during weeks 3–5 (r = −0.760, P = 0.029) — reflecting the transition from adaptation to accumulated fatigue
- **HRV reflected recovery status more rapidly than cortisol**, which required a longer period to show significant change

This study supports the concept that HRV is a **more sensitive and responsive marker of recovery** than hormonal measures, and that cortisol (the catabolic counterpart to testosterone) tracks inversely with parasympathetic recovery. The implication for androgen status is indirect but logical: if cortisol rises as HRV falls (indicating poor recovery), the T/C ratio necessarily declines, reflecting a shift toward catabolism.

---

**The Testosterone/Cortisol Ratio: Strain Marker, Not Diagnostic Tool**

The T/C ratio has a long history in sports science but its utility is now well-defined — and limited. The **ECSS/ACSM Joint Consensus Statement on Overtraining Syndrome** (2013) concluded:[2]

- The T/C ratio **decreases in relation to the intensity and duration of training** and reflects the actual physiological strain of training
- It **cannot be used for diagnosis of overreaching (OR) or OTS**
- Resting cortisol is not a useful measurement because 24-hour cortisol secretion in endurance-trained athletes at rest is normal
- OTS must be viewed on a continuum of HPA-axis disturbance, from adaptation to maladaptation

The **ACSM/AMSSM Consensus Statement on Load, Overload, and Recovery** (2019) similarly stated that no single hormonal measure can diagnose overtraining, but noted that in overtraining there is **often a decreased ratio of free testosterone to cortisol**, with endogenous testosterone levels declining.[6]

**Urhausen et al. (1995)** provided the foundational framework: testosterone and cortisol are competitive agonists at the receptor level of muscular cells, and the T/C ratio indicates the anabolic/catabolic balance. This ratio decreases with exercise intensity and duration and can be reversed by regenerative measures. Correlations with training-induced strength changes have been noted, but the ratio more likely indicates **current physiological strain** rather than a pathological state.[3]

---

**Overtraining Syndrome: Hormonal and Autonomic Dysregulation**

The **EROS-BASAL study** (Cadegiani & Kater, 2019) compared 14 OTS-affected male athletes, 25 healthy athletes, and 12 sedentary controls — all training at the same intensity and frequency. OTS was characterized by:[7]

- **Lower testosterone** in OTS vs. healthy athletes (but similar to sedentary controls)
- **Higher estradiol** and lower testosterone:estradiol ratio in OTS
- **Higher creatine kinase and lactate** — suggesting muscle tissue overreaction to exertion
- Immune changes (lower neutrophils in OTS vs. healthy athletes)

The testosterone:cortisol ratio was not significantly different between groups, reinforcing the consensus that this ratio reflects strain rather than OTS per se. The more discriminating hormonal marker was the **testosterone:estradiol ratio**, suggesting that aromatase upregulation (converting testosterone to estradiol) may be a more specific feature of OTS than simple testosterone suppression.

Regarding HRV in OTS, the ECSS/ACSM consensus noted that findings are **inconsistent**: some studies show no change in HRV during overreaching, others show decreased HRV in the upright position (suggesting vagal withdrawal), and one case report showed paradoxically **increased HRV** in an overtrained athlete (increased parasympathetic activity).[2] A meta-analysis found that short-term overload (2 weeks) intensified training.[2]

**Clinical Synthesis**

The evidence supports a model in which HRV and testosterone are **parallel outputs of the autonomic-endocrine recovery system** rather than directly causally linked:

- **HRV (particularly nocturnal RMSSD) is the most practical, responsive, and non-invasive marker** of recovery status in athletes. It reflects parasympathetic reactivation within hours to days and can be monitored daily via consumer wearables.[11][12][14]
- **Testosterone responds more slowly** (over weeks) and reflects the cumulative anabolic-catabolic balance. It is most useful for detecting chronic overreaching or OTS, not for day-to-day training decisions.[7][9]
- **HRV-guided training produces superior hormonal outcomes.** The Nuuttila et al. study demonstrated that prescribing high-intensity sessions based on RMSSD recovery led to both greater performance gains and rising testosterone — suggesting that respecting autonomic recovery signals prevents the HPG-axis suppression associated with excessive load.[1]
- **The T/C ratio reflects strain, not pathology.** Per the ECSS/ACSM consensus, it should not be used to diagnose overtraining but can indicate when training load exceeds current recovery capacity.[2]
- **In elite youth athletes**, HRV appears to be more sensitive to acute recovery status than cortisol, which requires longer periods to show significant change. Testosterone was not found to influence HR recovery in elite youth basketball players.[5][15]
- **For the team physician**, the ACSM/AMSSM consensus recommends that HRV interpretation in the context of overtraining is "evolving" and that decreased HRV is found in some but not all overtrained athletes. No single biomarker — autonomic or hormonal — is sufficient for OTS diagnosis, which remains a diagnosis of exclusion.[6][16]
