Zinc supplementation **raises testosterone only in men who are zinc-deficient**; in zinc-replete men, supplementation does not significantly increase testosterone levels.[1][2] The clinical relevance of zinc therefore hinges on identifying deficiency, which is more common than often appreciated — particularly in older adults, vegetarians, and men with chronic disease.

---

**Mechanisms: How Zinc Regulates Testosterone Synthesis**

Zinc participates in testosterone biosynthesis at multiple levels of the HPG axis and within the Leydig cell itself:

**Leydig cell steroidogenesis.** Zinc is required for the activity of key steroidogenic enzymes. Zinc transporters **ZnT7 and ZnT8** are expressed in Leydig cells and co-localize with steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR). ZnT8 facilitates zinc accumulation in Leydig cell mitochondria upon gonadotropin (hCG) stimulation; ZnT8 gene silencing or knockout inhibits progesterone and testosterone production by downregulating phosphorylated StAR expression via the **PKA signaling pathway**.[3] Similarly, ZnT7 gene silencing downregulates StAR, P450scc (cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme), and 3β-HSD, reducing testosterone output.[4] In zinc-deficient animal models, testicular 3β-HSD and 17β-HSD activities are significantly reduced, with corresponding decreases in serum FSH and testosterone.[5]

**Leydig cell survival.** Zinc protects Leydig cells from apoptosis induced by heat stress and oxidative damage by inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways (GRP78/CHOP), maintaining cell viability and testosterone production capacity.[6]

**Aromatase inhibition.** Zinc acts as a natural aromatase inhibitor, reducing the conversion of testosterone to estradiol. This may contribute to the testosterone-raising effect observed in zinc-deficient men by preserving the androgen pool.[1]

**HPG axis regulation.** Zinc deficiency suppresses both LH and FSH secretion, indicating a central (hypothalamic-pituitary) component in addition to the direct testicular effects. Zinc repletion restores gonadotropin levels.[5][7]

**Key Clinical Evidence**

**Evidence supporting supplementation in zinc-deficient men:**

- **Prasad et al. (1996)** — the foundational human study — demonstrated two critical findings: (1) Dietary zinc restriction (3–5 mg/day for 20 weeks) in young men caused a **dramatic fall in testosterone from 39.9 to 10.6 nmol/L** (a 73% decline, P = 0.005); (2) Zinc supplementation (30 mg/day as zinc gluconate) in marginally zinc-deficient elderly men for 6 months **nearly doubled testosterone from 8.3 to 16.0 nmol/L** (P = 0.02). Cellular zinc concentrations correlated significantly with serum testosterone (r = 0.43, P = 0.006).[8]

**Prevalence of Zinc Deficiency and At-Risk Populations**

The **RDA for zinc in adult men is 11 mg/day** (US) to 14–16 mg/day (European, depending on phytate intake), with a tolerable upper intake level of 40 mg/day.[13][14] Zinc deficiency is more prevalent than commonly recognized:

- The WHO estimates **31% of the global population** is at risk for zinc deficiency.[13]
- In the US, approximately **25% of adults ≥51 years** have inadequate zinc intake (NHANES 2007–2014 data), rising to **35–45% in older adults** when accounting for reduced absorption.[15][16]
- Populations at particular risk include: older adults, vegetarians/vegans (phytate-rich diets reduce zinc bioavailability), men with chronic kidney disease, alcoholics, and those with malabsorptive conditions.[13][16]

Marginal zinc deficiency — sufficient to impair testosterone synthesis — can develop within weeks on a restricted diet (3–5 mg/day) and produces a constellation of findings including decreased testosterone, oligospermia, decreased lean body mass, and impaired immune function, all of which are reversible with supplementation.[7][8]

---

**Clinical Implications**

The evidence supports a clear, **threshold-dependent model**: zinc is necessary for normal testosterone production, and deficiency causes hypogonadism that is reversible with repletion. However, supplementation above physiological requirements does not further increase testosterone and may cause harm (increased DNA fragmentation, GI symptoms).[11][2]

In men presenting with low testosterone, particularly those with risk factors for zinc deficiency (advanced age, CKD, vegetarian diet, malabsorption, alcoholism), **checking serum zinc** (normal range 60–120 μg/dL) is a reasonable and low-cost step. A serum zinc [17] If deficiency is confirmed, repletion with 15–30 mg elemental zinc daily is appropriate, with reassessment of testosterone after 3–6 months.[1][8] Supraphysiologic zinc supplementation (>40 mg/day long-term) should be avoided due to the risk of copper deficiency and other adverse effects.[13]
