**Mechanisms of Action**

Ashwagandha's hormonal effects operate through several interconnected pathways:

**HPA-axis modulation (cortisol reduction).** The most consistent and well-supported mechanism. Ashwagandha's bioactive withanolides — particularly withaferin A and sitoindosides — modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, reducing cortisol secretion. A systematic review of HPA-axis–modulating phytochemicals identified ashwagandha as having the **most consistent cortisol-lowering effect** among all plants studied.[3] This is clinically relevant because chronic cortisol elevation directly suppresses GnRH pulsatility, LH secretion, and Leydig cell testosterone production — the cortisol–testosterone antagonism discussed in prior context. By reducing cortisol, ashwagandha may disinhibit the HPG axis, allowing testosterone recovery.[2][4]

**GABA-mimetic activity.** Withanolides have demonstrated GABA-mimetic properties, which may contribute to both anxiolytic effects and enhanced GnRH pulsatility. GABA-ergic signaling at the hypothalamic level facilitates GnRH secretion, potentially improving downstream LH and testosterone output.[5]

**Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects.** Ashwagandha reduces oxidative stress markers in seminal plasma and testicular tissue, protecting Leydig cells and spermatogenic cells from ROS-mediated damage. In oligospermic men, ashwagandha root extract significantly reduced seminal plasma levels of reactive oxygen species while increasing antioxidant enzyme activity.[5][6]

**Central regulatory pathway modulation.** A comprehensive review concluded that ashwagandha's hormonal effects likely result from **modulation of central regulatory pathways rather than direct receptor binding** — distinguishing it from exogenous hormones or direct enzyme inhibitors.[2]

**Ashwagandha Extract Standardization: KSM-66 vs. Other Formulations**

A critical consideration is the significant variability among ashwagandha extracts:

- **KSM-66** — a full-spectrum root extract standardized to ≥5% withanolides — is the most widely studied formulation in male reproductive health trials, including the Ambiye oligospermia trial.[18]
- **Shoden** — a root and leaf extract standardized to 35% withanolide glycosides — uses a much higher withanolide concentration at lower total doses (60–240 mg vs. 300–675 mg for KSM-66).[4][16]
- **Witholytin®** — a novel root extract — showed testosterone and LH effects in overweight middle-aged men.[15]
- The Fornalik meta-analysis suggested **dose-response effects based on withanolide content**, implying that the active withanolide dose — not the total extract weight — may be the key determinant of efficacy.[1]

---

**Safety Considerations: Hepatotoxicity Signal**

While ashwagandha has generally demonstrated a favorable safety profile in clinical trials — with only mild, transient adverse events (somnolence, GI discomfort) reported across 30+ human studies — an emerging hepatotoxicity signal warrants attention:[19]

- **Philips et al. (2023)** reported the **largest case series of ashwagandha-induced liver injury (HILI)** from India: 8 confirmed single-ingredient cases out of 23 total. The presentation was predominantly **cholestatic hepatitis**. Critically, 5 patients had underlying chronic liver disease, and 3 of these developed acute-on-chronic liver failure — **all 3 died**. Chemical analysis confirmed no adulteration or contamination.[20]
- **Bokan et al. (2023)** reported two additional cases with RUCAM scores of 7 ("probable" causality), presenting with hepatocellular and mixed liver injury patterns after 45 days to 6 months of use at standard doses (450 mg TID). Both resolved after discontinuation.[21]
- **Lubarska et al. (2023)** described a case of severe cholestatic hepatitis requiring plasmapheresis after 1 year of ashwagandha use.[22]
- A comprehensive safety review identified that metabolic transformation of withanolides via **cytochrome P450 enzymes can form reactive intermediates**, leading to oxidative stress and hepatotoxicity. The endocrine-modulating activity has also been implicated in rare cases of thyrotoxicosis and adrenal suppression.[23]
- A preclinical safety review noted that liver toxicity cases were **not predicted by preclinical evidence**, and further work is needed to clarify the responsible constituents and patient-related risk factors.[24]
- A 12-month prospective observational study (n = 191) found no clinically significant changes in ALT or AST, supporting general safety at standard doses in healthy adults without pre-existing liver disease.[17]

**Key safety takeaway:** Ashwagandha should be **avoided in patients with pre-existing liver disease** given the risk of acute-on-chronic liver failure. In healthy individuals, periodic liver function monitoring is prudent with prolonged use, and patients should be counseled about symptoms of hepatotoxicity (jaundice, pruritus, dark urine).[23][21][20]

---

**Clinical Implications**

The evidence supports ashwagandha as the **most evidence-backed adaptogenic herb for cortisol reduction**, with a secondary testosterone-raising effect in men that appears to be mediated primarily through HPA-axis normalization. The testosterone effect (~57 ng/dL mean increase) is clinically modest — roughly equivalent to the effect of correcting zinc deficiency — and is most pronounced in men with elevated baseline cortisol or stress. Ashwagandha is not a substitute for testosterone replacement therapy in men with confirmed hypogonadism, but may serve as a reasonable adjunctive strategy in men with stress-related testosterone suppression, particularly when cortisol is elevated. The hepatotoxicity signal, while rare, necessitates caution in patients with liver disease and informed consent regarding this risk.
