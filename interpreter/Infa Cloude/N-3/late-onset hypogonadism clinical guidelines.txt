Here is a comprehensive summary of current clinical guidelines for **late-onset hypogonadism (LOH)**, synthesizing recommendations from major societies and recent landmark evidence.

---

**Definition and Epidemiology**

Late-onset hypogonadism is defined as a clinical syndrome of **androgen deficiency symptoms combined with consistently low serum testosterone levels** in aging men, in the absence of organic hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular disease.[1][2] The European Male Ageing Study (EMAS) defined LOH as at least three sexual symptoms (decreased sexual interest, morning erections, and erectile dysfunction) with total testosterone [2] LOH affects only a small minority of aging men (**2%–6%**), and is largely attributable to comorbidities — particularly **obesity** — causing gonadotropin suppression.[3][2]

---

**Diagnosis**

All major guidelines agree on a stepwise diagnostic approach:

- **Symptom assessment:** Testing should be reserved for men with signs and symptoms of androgen deficiency — most specifically **decreased libido, decreased spontaneous/morning erections, and small testes**. The AUA, Endocrine Society, and AAFP recommend against screening asymptomatic men.[3][4][5][6]
- **Biochemical confirmation:** Diagnosis requires **two fasting morning serum total testosterone measurements** (drawn between 7–10 AM) on separate days, both below the diagnostic threshold:[4][5][3]
  - **AUA and Endocrine Society:** [4][5]
  - **Endocrine Society (CDC-certified assays):** [5]
  - **Society for Endocrinology/ACLM (UK):** 12 nmol/L usually excludes hypogonadism[7]
- **Free testosterone:** Should be assessed (via equilibrium dialysis or calculated from total testosterone, SHBG, and albumin) in men with conditions that alter SHBG — particularly **obesity, diabetes, and aging** — or when total testosterone is borderline. Direct analog-based free testosterone immunoassays are inaccurate and should not be used.[3][5][7]
- **Distinguish primary vs. secondary:** Measure **FSH and LH** to differentiate primary hypogonadism (elevated gonadotropins) from secondary (low/inappropriately normal gonadotropins). If LH is low or low-normal, measure **prolactin** to evaluate for pituitary adenoma.[3][6][4]

**First-Line Management: Address Reversible Causes**

For men with **obesity-induced (functional) hypogonadism**, the recommended first-line intervention is **weight loss**, not testosterone therapy.[3][9] Weight loss of at least 5% typically increases serum total testosterone significantly and is associated with improved physical function, libido, and erectile function.[3] Other reversible causes to address include opioid use, corticosteroids, severe illness, and medications causing hyperprolactinemia.[3]

---

**Testosterone Replacement Therapy (TRT)**

**Indications:** TRT is recommended for men with **permanent hypogonadism** (organic causes) or those unable to discontinue causative medications, after shared decision-making regarding benefits and risks.[3][5][9][6]

For LOH specifically, guidelines are more conservative:

- The **EMAS 2023 position statement** recommends TRT only for symptomatic older men with confirmed low testosterone, after explaining uncertainties about long-term safety.[9]
- The **ACP 2020 guideline** recommends discussing TRT initiation only in men with age-related low testosterone and **sexual dysfunction** who want to improve sexual function, and recommends against TRT solely to improve energy, vitality, physical function, or cognition.[10][11]

**Treatment goals and formulations:**

- Target **mid-normal testosterone range** (300–900 ng/dL per Endocrine Society; 450–600 ng/dL per recent European Urology review)[6][13][5]
- Formulations include transdermal gels, intramuscular injections (cypionate, enanthate, undecanoate), and oral testosterone undecanoate — selected based on patient preference, pharmacokinetics, adverse effects, and cost[6][14]
- **Short-acting transdermal preparations** are preferred for TRT initiation in older men[9]

**Cardiovascular Safety: The TRAVERSE Trial**

The **TRAVERSE trial** (2023) was the first RCT powered to assess cardiovascular outcomes of TRT. Among **5,246 men** aged 45–80 with hypogonadism and preexisting or high-risk cardiovascular disease, transdermal testosterone was **noninferior to placebo** for MACE (HR 0.96; 95% CI, 0.78–1.17; P[16] However, a higher incidence of **pulmonary embolism** (0.9% vs. 0.5%), **atrial fibrillation** (3.5% vs. 2.4%), and **acute kidney injury** (2.3% vs. 1.5%) was observed in the testosterone group.[16][6]

Based on these and subsequent data, the **FDA recently removed the black box cardiovascular warning** from testosterone product labels, though a warning for a possible 2–4 mm Hg increase in systolic blood pressure has been added.[3]

---

**Monitoring on TRT**

- **At 3 months** (and after any dose change): Measure serum total testosterone and hematocrit[9][3][5]
- **At 6 and 12 months, then annually:** Serum total testosterone, hematocrit, PSA (in men ≥40), and clinical symptom assessment[9][6][3]
- **Hematocrit >54%** during therapy warrants discontinuation and hematology referral[6]
- **PSA increase >1.5 ng/mL** or absolute PSA >4 ng/mL warrants urologic evaluation[15][3]
- Bone density assessment should also be considered in older hypogonadal men[9]

---

**Key Takeaways Across Guidelines**

1. LOH diagnosis requires both **sexual symptoms and confirmed biochemical hypogonadism** on two fasting morning samples.
2. **Weight loss is first-line** for obesity-associated functional hypogonadism.
3. TRT is appropriate for **permanent hypogonadism** or refractory cases after addressing reversible causes.
4. The TRAVERSE trial has largely resolved the cardiovascular safety debate — **no increased MACE risk** — but vigilance for pulmonary embolism and erythrocytosis remains important.
5. Monitoring should include testosterone levels, hematocrit, and PSA at structured intervals.
