### Practice Guideline

The **American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP)** recommends that **over-the-counter, hormone-free vaginal products are reasonable first-line therapies for genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)**.[1] Vaginal moisturizers should be used several times per week to mitigate symptoms related to vaginal dryness, and vaginal lubricants are recommended on an as-needed basis for dyspareunia.[1]

For women who prefer or require hormonal therapy, **vaginal estrogen** may also benefit urge incontinence and prevention of recurrent urinary tract infections. Endometrial protection with progesterone is generally not necessary with low-dose vaginal estrogen, but only short-term data support this. Patients with a history of breast cancer should discuss vaginal estrogen use with their oncologists before initiation.[1]

**FDA-approved non-estrogen therapies** for vaginal atrophy and dyspareunia include:[1]

- **Oral ospemifene** (a selective estrogen receptor modulator): A 12-week multicenter RCT showed superiority over placebo for reducing vaginal dryness and improving dyspareunia and sexual function scores, with no serious treatment-related adverse effects including endometrial hyperplasia or carcinoma
- **Intravaginal DHEA (prasterone, 6.5 mg daily):** A 12-week RCT showed significant improvement in dyspareunia and vaginal dryness for women with moderate to severe symptoms at baseline

Notably, a 2018 RCT directly comparing vaginal estradiol (10 mcg) with a vaginal moisturizer and placebo found that **all three formulations significantly reduced atrophic symptom severity and increased sexual function regardless of preparation**, suggesting a substantial placebo/moisturizer effect.[1]

The physiological description is accurate: **estrogen receptors are densely expressed throughout the vulva, vagina, urethra, and bladder trigone, and estrogen withdrawal causes vaginal epithelial thinning, loss of rugae, glycogen depletion, vaginal pH elevation from ~4.5 to >5.0, decreased collagen/elastin, and diminished lubrication — a progressive condition affecting 27-84% of postmenopausal women that worsens without treatment**.[2][3][4] Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which often resolve spontaneously, GSM is chronic and progressive.[2][3] The NAMS 2020 Position Statement confirms that GSM remains severely underdiagnosed and undertreated.[2]

## 1. NUTRITION — Remove/Add

**Add:**

**Phytoestrogen-rich foods (soy isoflavones, 50-100 mg/day):** A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1,325 women) found soy isoflavones **significantly improved vaginal dryness** (SMD = −1.88, p = 0.006) and urogenital symptoms (SMD = −0.33, p = 0.04), with dose-dependent effects optimal at 50-100 mg/day.[5] A separate 2025 meta-analysis of 47 RCTs confirmed isoflavones significantly reduced vaginal dryness (SMD = −1.147, p = 0.016) and increased vaginal maturation value (SMD = 0.605, p = 0.022).[6] A JAMA meta-analysis of 62 studies also confirmed phytoestrogens were associated with modest improvements in vaginal dryness scores.[7]

- **Sources:** Tofu, tempeh, edamame, miso, soy milk (40-80 mg isoflavones/day from food)

**Omega-3 fatty acids:** Combined vitamin E and omega-3 supplementation has been shown to lower hot flush intensity, and omega-3s support anti-inflammatory pathways relevant to mucosal health.[8]

- **Sources:** Fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel — 2-3 times weekly), walnuts, chia seeds, flaxseeds

**Flaxseeds (ground, 1-2 tablespoons/day):** Rich in lignans (phytoestrogens) that have mild estrogenic activity and may reduce perimenopausal symptoms.[8]

**Prebiotic and probiotic-rich foods:** Support Lactobacillus-dominant vaginal microbiome, which is critical for maintaining vaginal pH [9]

- **Sources:** Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, prebiotic fibers (garlic, onions, asparagus, bananas)

**Adequate hydration:** 2-2.5 L/day — supports mucosal hydration systemically.

**Vitamin D-rich foods:** Vitamin D regulates vaginal epithelial cell growth and may alleviate genitourinary tract symptoms.[10][11]

- **Sources:** Fatty fish, egg yolks, fortified foods

**Remove:**

- **Excess sugar and refined carbohydrates:** Promote inflammation and may worsen vaginal dysbiosis
- **Excess alcohol:** Dehydrates mucosal tissues, disrupts hormonal balance, and impairs sleep
- **Excess caffeine:** Can worsen urinary urgency and frequency

## 2. NUTRACEUTICALS — Compounds, Doses, Timing

**Vaginal hyaluronic acid (topical gel/suppository):** Applied 3-5 times/week.[12][13][14][15]

- **Evidence:** A 2026 meta-analysis of placebo-controlled RCTs showed vaginal HA significantly improved vaginal health index (SMD = 3.40, p [14][15][12]

**Soy isoflavones (oral supplement):** 50-100 mg/day.[5][6]

- **Timing:** With meals, divided doses
- **Evidence:** Meta-analyses confirm significant improvement in vaginal dryness and urogenital symptoms at this dose range.[5][6]

**Vaginal vitamin E suppositories:** 100-400 IU, 2-3 times/week.[16]

- **Evidence:** An RCT comparing vaginal vitamin E suppositories with conjugated estrogen vaginal cream found comparable improvements in sexual function scores (ASFQ) over 12 weeks, with no significant difference between groups. Vaginal vitamin E may be an alternative for women who cannot use hormonal therapy.[16]

**Vaginal vitamin D suppositories:** 1,000 IU/day.[17][11]

- **Evidence:** A meta-analysis showed topical vitamin D significantly improved vaginal maturation index (MD 25.16, p [17][11]

**Oral vitamin D:** 2,000-4,000 IU/day (target 30-50 ng/mL).[11]

- **Timing:** Morning with fat-containing meal

**Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA+DHA):** 2,000-3,000 mg/day.

- **Timing:** With meals
- **Evidence:** Anti-inflammatory effects support mucosal health

**Vaginal probiotics (Lactobacillus-based):** Intravaginal Lactobacillus crispatus or L. rhamnosus formulations.[9][18][19][20]

- **Evidence:** A 2026 systematic review found intravaginal Lactobacillus showed a preventive effect for recurrent cystitis, though RCT results were mixed compared to antibiotic prophylaxis. In postmenopausal women with recurrent cystitis, vaginal Lactobacillus suppositories restored vaginal Lactobacillus abundance (from undetectable to 19% median relative abundance) and reduced average cystitis episodes from 6.3 to 2.4 per year (p = 0.0015). An RCT showed vaginal probiotics reduced recurrent UTI incidence from 70.4% to 40.9% at 4 months.[20][9][18]

## 3. ELIMINATION — Strict Avoidance

**Vulvovaginal irritants:** Scented soaps, douches, perfumed products, bubble baths, and harsh detergents on undergarments — these disrupt the already fragile vaginal microbiome and worsen mucosal irritation.[21][3][4]

**Tight, non-breathable clothing:** Synthetic underwear traps moisture and promotes pathogen growth. Cotton underwear is preferred.

**Smoking:** Accelerates estrogen metabolism, worsens mucosal atrophy, and impairs blood flow to genital tissues.

**Excess alcohol:** Dehydrates mucosal tissues and disrupts hormonal balance.

**Unnecessary antibiotics:** Deplete protective vaginal Lactobacillus, promoting dysbiosis and recurrent infections.[9][22]

**Anticholinergic medications (when possible):** For urinary symptoms, β-3 agonists (mirabegron) are preferred over antimuscarinics due to a safer adverse event profile in older adults, particularly regarding dementia risk.[12]

## 4. LAB MARKERS — Testing Recommendations

**Clinical assessment (most important — GSM is primarily a clinical diagnosis):**[4][23][24][25]

- **Pelvic examination:** Assess for vulvovaginal atrophy — loss of labial fat pad, thinning of labia minora, pale/dry mucosa, loss of vaginal rugae, introital narrowing, petechiae
- **Vaginal pH:** Typically >5.0 in GSM (normal estrogenized vagina: 3.5-5.0); elevated pH in the absence of infection supports the diagnosis[24][25]
- **Vaginal Maturation Index (VMI):** Proportion of parabasal, intermediate, and superficial cells — shift toward parabasal cells indicates estrogen deficiency[24][25]
- **Vaginal Health Index (VHI):** Composite score assessing elasticity, fluid volume, pH, epithelial integrity, and moisture[24]

**Hormonal assessment:**[23][26]

- **Estradiol (or FSH if symptoms of deficiency):** Confirm menopausal status and degree of estrogen decline
- **Testosterone (total and free), DHEA-S:** Assess androgen status — androgens influence libido, arousal, and genital tissue health[26]
- **SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin):** Elevated SHBG reduces free testosterone availability
- **Prolactin:** If libido is significantly reduced — hyperprolactinemia suppresses sexual desire[26]
- **TSH:** Hypothyroidism can cause fatigue, low libido, and vaginal dryness[26]

**Screening for secondary causes:**

- **Urinalysis and urine culture:** If urinary symptoms are present — rule out UTI before attributing symptoms to GSM
- **HbA1c, fasting glucose:** Diabetes worsens vaginal dryness, increases infection risk, and impairs wound healing
- **25(OH) vitamin D:** Deficiency may worsen vulvovaginal atrophy[17][11]

**Sexual function assessment:**[21][23]

- **Validated questionnaires:** Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS) — quantify dysfunction and monitor treatment response
- **Screen for psychosocial factors:** Relationship quality, depression, anxiety, history of sexual trauma — these significantly influence sexual function and may require targeted intervention[21][23]

## 5. LIFESTYLE — Sleep, Stress Management, Physical Activity

**Pelvic Floor Muscle Training (PFMT) — First-Line Conservative Therapy:**

A 2025 meta-analysis of 21 RCTs showed pelvic floor exercises demonstrated a **strong effect on vaginal/genital discomfort symptoms** (Hedges' g = 1.187, p = 0.009), moderate effects on sexual function (g = 0.549, p = 0.001), quality of life (g = 0.438, p = 0.008), urinary incontinence (g = 0.335, p [27] A feasibility study showed 91% completion rate with significant reductions in GSM symptoms, signs, and impacts on quality of life and sexual function after 12 weeks of PFMT.[28]

- **Supervised pelvic floor physical therapy** is recommended as first-line treatment for genito-pelvic pain disorders.[21][29][12]
- **Kegel exercises:** 3 sets of 10-15 contractions daily (5-10 second holds), progressing in intensity
- **Referral to a pelvic floor physiotherapist** is ideal for proper technique assessment and individualized programming[29]

**Regular Sexual Activity or Vaginal Stimulation:**

Regular vaginal sexual activity (with a partner or self-stimulation) maintains vaginal blood flow, elasticity, and lubrication capacity. The NAMS Position Statement and Mayo Clinic review both note that regular sexual activity helps maintain vaginal health.[2][3]

- **Vaginal dilators:** May be recommended for women with introital narrowing or vaginismus, especially when combined with psychotherapy[21]

**Sleep Optimization:**

Poor sleep worsens GSM symptoms through multiple mechanisms — reduced growth hormone secretion impairs mucosal repair, elevated cortisol degrades connective tissue, and sleep deprivation increases pain sensitivity.

- **Target 7-8 hours of quality sleep**
- **Address nocturnal discomfort:** Vaginal moisturizer before bed can reduce nighttime irritation
- **CBT-I for insomnia:** First-line treatment

**Stress Management:**

Chronic stress elevates cortisol, which suppresses estrogen and testosterone production, worsens vaginal dryness, and reduces libido.

- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction:** Has been shown to improve several types of female sexual dysfunction including sexual interest/arousal disorder[23]
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):** Effective for sexual dysfunction, particularly when psychological factors contribute[21][23]

**Physical Activity:**

- **Moderate aerobic exercise:** 150-300 min/week — improves blood flow to pelvic tissues, reduces stress, and supports hormonal balance
- **Yoga:** May improve pelvic floor awareness and reduce anxiety around intimacy

## 6. PRIORITY #1 — Single Most Impactful Action

**Begin using a vaginal moisturizer (3-5 times/week) and a lubricant during intercourse immediately, while scheduling a physician consultation to discuss whether low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, or ospemifene is appropriate.**

**Why this dual approach is most critical:**

The clinical profile described — vaginal dryness, painful intercourse, urinary urgency/frequency, recurrent UTIs, loss of libido, and anxiety around intimacy — represents **moderate to severe GSM that is progressive and will worsen without treatment**.[2][3] Unlike vasomotor symptoms, GSM does not resolve spontaneously.

**1. Immediate symptom relief with non-prescription products:** Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants are recommended as first-line therapy by NAMS, ACOG, and the AAFP.[1][2] They provide immediate relief from dryness and dyspareunia, reduce anxiety around intimacy, and can be started today without a prescription. A well-designed RCT showed that even placebo gel significantly reduced symptom severity, suggesting that regular vaginal moisture application itself has therapeutic value.[30]

**2. Physician consultation for prescription therapy is essential for moderate-to-severe symptoms:** The evidence-based prescription options — **low-dose vaginal estrogen** (the criterion standard), **vaginal DHEA (prasterone 6.5 mg/day)**, and **oral ospemifene (60 mg/day)** — all demonstrate efficacy for dryness, dyspareunia, and treatment satisfaction.[31][2][21] Vaginal estrogen additionally prevents recurrent UTIs, which is directly relevant to this pattern.[1][32][22] Low-dose vaginal estrogen does not require concomitant progestogen and has minimal systemic absorption, making it safe for most women.[1][2] For women with a history of hormone-sensitive cancer, non-hormonal options (vaginal moisturizers, hyaluronic acid, ospemifene) should be discussed with the oncologist.[2]

**3. Addressing the cycle of pain → avoidance → worsening atrophy:** Dyspareunia creates a vicious cycle — pain leads to avoidance of sexual activity, which reduces vaginal blood flow and elasticity, worsening atrophy and further increasing pain. Breaking this cycle early with adequate lubrication/moisturization and, when indicated, local hormonal therapy prevents progression and preserves sexual function and relationship quality.[2][3]

**Expected timeline:** Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants provide immediate symptom relief. Vaginal estrogen and DHEA typically show significant improvement in dryness and dyspareunia within **4-12 weeks** of initiation.[31][1] Urinary symptoms may take longer to improve. Pelvic floor physical therapy shows significant benefits by 12 weeks.[28][27] By 3-6 months of combined therapy, substantial improvement in vaginal health, sexual function, urinary symptoms, and quality of life is expected.
