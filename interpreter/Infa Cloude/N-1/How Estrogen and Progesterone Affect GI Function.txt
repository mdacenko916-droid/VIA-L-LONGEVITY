## GI Function in the Hormonal Transition: Bloating, IBS, and Reflux in Perimenopause

**Estrogen and progesterone exert direct, multifaceted effects on gastrointestinal motility, visceral sensitivity, and mucosal barrier function** — explaining why bloating, IBS symptoms, and reflux intensify during the perimenstrual phase and worsen across the menopausal transition.[1][2][3][4] Approximately **60% of FGID patients** are women, and IBS is **2–3× more prevalent in women** than men in Western countries, with symptoms fluctuating in concert with ovarian hormone levels.[3][5][6]

---

**How Estrogen and Progesterone Affect GI Function**

**Estrogen and serotonin in the gut**

A landmark 2025 study in Science identified an **estrogen-responsive paracrine circuit** in the colon that explains heightened gut sensitivity in females. Estrogen signaling upregulates the short-chain fatty acid receptor **Olfr78** on colonic PYY-expressing L cells, increasing PYY release. Elevated PYY then acts on neighboring **enterochromaffin (EC) cells** via NPY1R, enhancing **serotonin (5-HT) release** and amplifying gut pain signaling. This estrogen → L cell → EC cell → serotonin pathway provides a cellular basis for the female predominance of visceral pain disorders.[7]

Additional estrogen-serotonin interactions include:[8][6][9][10]

- **~95% of the body's serotonin** is produced in the gut by EC cells, and estrogen modulates both 5-HT synthesis and reuptake transporter (SERT) expression. Gender differences exist in postprandial serotonin levels in IBS-D patients.
- Estrogen activates **mast cell degranulation** via the G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER/GPR30), releasing tryptase and histamine — key mediators of visceral hypersensitivity. GPER antagonism blocks estrogen-induced visceral hypersensitivity in animal models.[9]
- Estrogen has a **dual effect on visceral pain**: vagal-mediated antinociception (protective) via ERβ and spinal-mediated pronociception. The net effect depends on hormone levels, stress state, and receptor expression patterns.[10]
- Estrogen modulates the **HPA axis stress response** and autonomic nervous system function, both of which regulate gut-brain axis signaling. Women with IBS show enhanced stress-induced GI symptoms during low-estrogen phases.[6][11]

**Progesterone and slowed motility**

A comprehensive review concluded that progesterone has a **dose-dependent and sex-dependent effect** on GI function:[4]

- **Slows gastric emptying** (especially at high luteal-phase levels)
- **Reduces overall GI motility** via smooth muscle relaxation (progesterone receptors are expressed throughout the GI tract)
- **Reduces lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure**, promoting gastroesophageal reflux
- **Reduces gallbladder contractility**, potentially contributing to biliary symptoms
- During pregnancy (when progesterone is highest), nausea, constipation, and reflux are most pronounced — the same mechanisms operate at a lesser degree during the luteal phase

**Why symptoms worsen perimenstrually and in perimenopause:**

IBS symptoms are **heightened at menses** when both estrogen and progesterone decline rapidly from high luteal-phase levels.[12][5] Women with IBS report significantly more stomach pain, nausea, and diarrhea during the perimenstrual phase, and IBS is **3-fold more common** in women with dysmenorrhea.[12] During perimenopause, the erratic hormonal fluctuations — with unpredictable estrogen surges and progesterone deficiency from anovulatory cycles — create a state of chronic GI dysregulation. After menopause, estrogen deficiency leads to **increased visceral hypersensitivity, slower colonic transit, impaired mucosal barrier function, and increased GERD risk** due to reduced LES pressure and impaired esophageal mucosal protection.[1][2]

**Nutrition: Low-FODMAP Diet, Fiber, and Reflux Triggers**

**Low-FODMAP diet for IBS**

The low-FODMAP diet is the **most extensively studied dietary intervention** for IBS. The ACG gives it a **conditional recommendation** (very low quality evidence), and a network meta-analysis ranked it **first** for all endpoints studied.[13][14][15]

Key evidence:

- **RR of remaining symptomatic:** 0.69 (95% CI 0.54–0.88) vs. comparators; **NNT = 5** (95% CI 3–11)[14]
- **Superior to BDA/NICE dietary advice** for abdominal bloating/distension (RR 0.72; 95% CI 0.55–0.94)[15]
- Response rates: ~57% with low-FODMAP vs. ~38% with control diets[16]
- The diet should be implemented in **3 phases**: (1) strict elimination (2–6 weeks), (2) systematic reintroduction to identify individual triggers, (3) personalized long-term diet. Dietitian guidance is recommended to prevent nutritional deficiencies and disordered eating.[17][18]
- A recent RCT found that a **personalized FODMAP approach** (targeting only individually identified triggers) may be as effective as full elimination while being more sustainable.[17]

**Fiber for constipation-predominant symptoms**

Soluble fiber (particularly **psyllium**) is recommended for IBS-C and constipation-related bloating. The ACG recommends **soluble fiber** (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence) and recommends **against insoluble fiber** (wheat bran), which may worsen bloating and pain.[13]

**GERD dietary triggers and lifestyle**

The ACG GERD guideline notes that supporting data for specific food avoidance are **limited and variable**, but commonly recommended modifications include:[19][20][21]

- **Strongest evidence:** Weight loss in overweight patients (dose-dependent improvement; 40% reduction in frequent GERD with BMI reduction ≥3.5); head-of-bed elevation; avoiding meals [19][20]

**Supplements: Peppermint Oil and Digestive Enzymes**

**Peppermint oil (enteric-coated)**

The ACG conditionally recommends peppermint oil for overall symptom improvement in IBS.[13] The mechanism involves **L-menthol blockade of calcium channels** causing smooth muscle relaxation, plus modulation of transient receptor potential channels affecting visceral sensation, and anti-inflammatory effects.[13]

Meta-analytic evidence (12 RCTs, 835 patients): **RR for overall symptom improvement = 2.39** (95% CI 1.93–2.97; P [13] However, two recent rigorous RCTs using FDA-recommended endpoints showed **no significant difference** from placebo on primary endpoints, though secondary outcomes (pain, discomfort, IBS severity) improved significantly with small-intestinal-release peppermint oil.[33][34]

**Dosing:** Enteric-coated peppermint oil **180–200 mg, 2–3 times daily**, taken 30–60 minutes before meals. Enteric coating is essential to prevent heartburn from LES relaxation — peppermint oil can **worsen GERD symptoms**.[13][19]

**Digestive enzymes**

Two small RCTs (n = 40 and n = 120) of multi-enzyme blends in functional dyspepsia showed significant improvement in quality of life, pain severity, and sleep quality vs. placebo over 60 days.[35][36] However, the AGA and ACG guidelines do not include digestive enzyme supplementation in their IBS or bloating recommendations. The European consensus on functional bloating (ESNM/UEG 2025) does not list digestive enzymes among recommended treatments.[37] The primary role of enzyme supplementation is in **documented enzyme deficiencies** (e.g., lactase deficiency, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency) rather than empiric use for functional bloating.[38]

**Red Flags → Gastroenterologist Referral**

The Rome IV criteria, ACG, AGA, and NEJM reviews consistently identify the following alarm features that require further investigation and should not be attributed to functional GI disorders without evaluation:[39][40][41][42][21][43]

**IBS alarm features (lower GI):**

- **Unintentional weight loss** (>10% of ideal body weight)
- **Rectal bleeding** not attributable to hemorrhoids or anal fissures
- **Iron deficiency anemia**
- **Nocturnal symptoms** (diarrhea waking patient from sleep — functional disorders rarely cause nocturnal symptoms)
- **New symptom onset after age 50**
- **Family history** of colorectal cancer, IBD, or celiac disease
- **Recent change in bowel habits** (progressive, not fluctuating)
- **Abnormal physical examination** findings (abdominal mass, lymphadenopathy, rectal mass)
- **Abnormal laboratory studies** (elevated CRP, elevated fecal calprotectin, anemia)

**GERD alarm features (upper GI):**

- **Dysphagia** (difficulty swallowing — progressive dysphagia to solids suggests stricture or malignancy)
- **Odynophagia** (painful swallowing)
- **Unintentional weight loss**
- **GI bleeding/hematemesis**
- **Anorexia**
- **Persistent vomiting**

All patients with alarm symptoms should undergo **upper endoscopy** (for upper GI symptoms) or **colonoscopy** (for lower GI symptoms).[21][43] For IBS without alarm features, a **positive diagnostic strategy** using Rome IV criteria is recommended rather than treating IBS as a diagnosis of exclusion — with limited testing including CBC, CRP, celiac serology (tissue transglutaminase IgA), and fecal calprotectin (in diarrhea-predominant patients) to exclude organic disease.[40][44][45]
