The interplay between the **HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis, cortisol, and testosterone** is clinically significant in men over 40, as chronic cortisol excess suppresses the HPG axis at multiple levels. Among adaptogens, **ashwagandha has the strongest evidence** for reducing cortisol and modestly increasing testosterone in men, though the clinical magnitude of these effects remains limited compared to established interventions like weight loss or pharmacotherapy.

---

**HPA–HPG Axis Crosstalk: How Cortisol Suppresses Testosterone**

Glucocorticoids inhibit the male reproductive axis at **every level** — hypothalamus, pituitary, and testes — creating a well-characterized mechanism by which chronic stress contributes to functional hypogonadism in aging men.[1][2][3]

- **Hypothalamic:** Cortisol and CRH suppress GnRH pulsatility, reducing LH pulse amplitude and frequency. This is the dominant mechanism in stress-associated testosterone suppression.[1][4]
- **Pituitary:** Glucocorticoids directly inhibit gonadotropin (LH/FSH) synthesis and release from pituitary gonadotropes.[2][3]
- **Testicular:** Glucocorticoid receptors on Leydig cells directly inhibit steroidogenic enzyme activity (StAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1), reduce LH receptor expression, and at high concentrations promote Leydig cell apoptosis. The enzyme 11β-HSD type 2 normally inactivates cortisol within the testis, but this protective mechanism is overwhelmed during chronic stress.[3][5]

A recent perspective emphasizes that testosterone suppression during sustained stress (energy deficit, sleep disruption, psychogenic stress) is typically a **centrally mediated, reversible adaptation** rather than intrinsic testicular failure — hCG stimulation studies confirm preserved Leydig cell responsiveness, supporting hypothalamic-pituitary inhibition as the dominant mechanism.[4] This distinction is critical: in men over 40, the combination of age-related HPT axis attenuation, obesity, and chronic stress creates a "perfect storm" for functional hypogonadism, with cortisol excess compounding the effects of adiposity and comorbidities.[6][7][8]

Corticosteroids are specifically listed among medications that suppress hypothalamic GnRH secretion and increase the likelihood of hypogonadism in men over 50.[8]

---

**Adaptogens: Evidence by Agent**

A systematic review of 52 RCTs examining phytonutrient effects on HPA-axis hormones found that the **most consistent finding was a morning cortisol-lowering effect from ashwagandha**; for most other phytonutrients, effects on HPA-axis activity in humans were unclear.[9]

**Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera)** — Strongest evidence

A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (1,706 participants) found ashwagandha significantly **reduced cortisol** (SMD = −1.18, p [10] A separate meta-analysis confirmed ashwagandha increased testosterone (SMD = 0.33, 95% CI 0.13–0.54, p = 0.001) and muscle strength (SMD = 0.58) in adults.[11]

Key individual trials in relevant populations:

- In overweight/mildly obese adults aged **40–75 years**, ashwagandha 200 mg twice daily significantly increased **free testosterone** (p = 0.048) and **LH** (p = 0.002) in men compared to placebo, suggesting HPG axis stimulation rather than peripheral effects alone.[12]
- A 12-month observational study (N = 191) demonstrated sustained cortisol reduction (p [13]
- A 60-day RCT showed significant morning cortisol reduction (p [14]

The proposed mechanism involves **modulation of central HPA-axis regulatory pathways** rather than direct receptor binding — reducing cortisol-mediated suppression of GnRH/LH and thereby disinhibiting the HPG axis.[15]

**Rhodiola rosea** — Moderate evidence for cortisol; no direct testosterone data

Rhodiola has demonstrated **cortisol-reducing** and anti-fatigue effects in clinical trials. In a 28-day RCT of 60 adults with stress-related fatigue, Rhodiola extract (576 mg/day) significantly decreased the cortisol response to awakening stress compared to placebo, with improvements in burnout scores and attention.[16] Pharmacological reviews describe interactions with the HPA system as a key mechanism, alongside effects on protein kinases, nitric oxide, and heat shock proteins.[17] However, **no human clinical trials have directly measured testosterone changes** with Rhodiola supplementation, limiting conclusions about its role in HPG axis modulation.[9]

**Panax ginseng (Korean Red Ginseng)** — Emerging evidence

A small RCT in 62 men with metabolic syndrome found that red ginseng (3.0 g/day for 4 weeks) significantly **increased total testosterone** (95% CI −70.1 to −1.0) and **decreased serum cortisol** (95% CI 1.1–5.5) compared to placebo, with concurrent improvements in mitochondrial function.[18] Preclinical data show that ginsenoside Rg1 modulates both HPA and HPG axes — reducing corticosterone while increasing testosterone and upregulating androgen receptor expression.[19] A 2026 study identified ginsenoside Rf as promoting testosterone synthesis via the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway in obesity-related hypogonadism models.[20] However, the systematic review of herbs and testosterone classified Asian red ginseng as having only "some evidence" — below the threshold of fenugreek and ashwagandha.[21]

**Other adaptogens** — Limited evidence

A systematic review of "testosterone boosters" found that **Eurycoma longifolia (Tongkat ali)** was "possibly effective" for men with late-onset hypogonadism, based on a study showing 90.8% of LOH patients achieved normal testosterone after 200 mg/day for one month.[22][23] Fenugreek seed extracts were identified alongside ashwagandha as having the most consistent positive effects on testosterone in the systematic review of herbal interventions.[21]

---

**Safety Considerations**

While ashwagandha is generally well-tolerated in clinical trials, emerging pharmacovigilance data warrant caution:

- **Hepatotoxicity:** Multiple case reports and a case series from India document ashwagandha-induced liver injury (HILI), predominantly presenting as **cholestatic hepatitis**. In patients with pre-existing liver disease, ashwagandha-HILI can precipitate acute-on-chronic liver failure with high mortality. One case required liver transplantation.[24][25][26]
- **Endocrine effects:** Ashwagandha modestly increases T4 levels (MD = 0.61 µg/dL) and has been implicated in cases of thyrotoxicosis, warranting monitoring in patients with thyroid disorders.[10][27]
- **Drug interactions:** Cytochrome P450-mediated metabolism of withanolides can form reactive intermediates; however, in vitro studies have not shown significant CYP3A4 or CYP2D6 inhibition.[27][28]
- A comprehensive preclinical safety review concluded ashwagandha is "remarkably safe" overall, but liver toxicity cases — mainly reversible cholestasis — were not predicted by preclinical data, and the responsible constituents remain unidentified.[29]
