**TSH reference ranges in men after 40 are age-dependent**, with the upper limit of normal (97.5th percentile) remaining relatively stable through ages 40–59 and then rising progressively after age 60. Current evidence strongly supports using **age-specific TSH thresholds** to avoid overdiagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism in older men. Regarding supplementation, **selenium has the strongest evidence** for reducing thyroid autoantibodies in Hashimoto's thyroiditis, **zinc plays a supportive role** in thyroid hormone metabolism, and **vitamin D** reduces autoantibody titers but has not been shown to prevent hypothyroidism in men in large RCTs.

---

**TSH Reference Ranges and Thresholds in Men After 40**

The standard laboratory TSH reference range of approximately **0.45–4.12 mIU/L** (NHANES III, disease-free population) does not account for the well-documented age-related rightward shift in TSH distribution.[1] A 2025 cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data found that the 97.5th percentile of TSH increases with age, and applying age-, sex-, and race-specific reference intervals **reclassified 48.5% of persons with subclinical hypothyroidism as normal**.[2]

A meta-analysis of factors influencing TSH reference intervals found that in men, TSH concentrations remain relatively stable from ages 20–59, then rise sharply after age 60, peaking in the 60–69 age group — a pattern distinct from women, in whom TSH begins rising after age 40.[3] The following figure illustrates this sex-specific age pattern:

---

**Treatment Thresholds for Subclinical Hypothyroidism**

Guidelines from the ATA, ETA, and NICE converge on the following approach:[7][8][9]

- **TSH ≥10 mIU/L** (confirmed on repeat testing 1–3 months later): Treatment with levothyroxine is generally recommended for adults ≤70 years, as levels above this threshold are unlikely to normalize spontaneously and carry increased cardiovascular risk.
- **TSH 7.0–9.9 mIU/L:** Treatment can be considered based on observational data indicating increased cardiovascular risk, particularly in patients [9]
- **TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L:** A therapeutic trial of levothyroxine may be considered in symptomatic patients [7][8]
- **Older adults (≥65–70 years):** Treatment should be approached cautiously. No clear benefits on neurocognitive function or quality of life have been demonstrated, and overtreatment increases the risk of **atrial fibrillation and osteoporosis**. The ETA recommends a higher TSH target of approximately **1–5 mIU/L** for patients >70–75 years, compared to the lower half of the reference range (0.4–2.5 mIU/L) for younger patients.[7][9][8]

---

**Thyroid–Testosterone Crosstalk in Aging Men**

Thyroid dysfunction directly impacts the HPG axis. **Primary hypothyroidism** causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with reduced free testosterone, which is reversible with levothyroxine replacement.[10][11] Conversely, **hyperthyroidism** elevates SHBG and total testosterone but reduces bioavailable testosterone, and is associated with gynecomastia and sexual dysfunction.[10] In aging men, both testosterone and free T3 decline in parallel, potentially reflecting a shared age-related attenuation at the pituitary level.[12][13] This underscores the importance of evaluating thyroid function as part of the workup for hypogonadism in men over 40.

---

**Selenium Supplementation and Thyroid Health**

Selenium is the trace element with the **strongest evidence base** for thyroid disease prevention and autoimmune modulation. The thyroid gland has the highest selenium concentration of any tissue, and selenium-dependent enzymes (deiodinases, glutathione peroxidases) are essential for thyroid hormone synthesis and antioxidant defense.[14]

A 2024 meta-analysis of 35 RCTs (2,358 participants) found that selenium supplementation in Hashimoto's thyroiditis:[15]

- **Decreased TSH** in patients not on levothyroxine (SMD −0.21, 95% CI −0.43 to −0.02)
- **Decreased TPO antibodies** (SMD −0.96, 95% CI −1.36 to −0.56)
- **Decreased malondialdehyde** (oxidative stress marker)
- Had comparable adverse effects to placebo (OR 0.89)
- Did not significantly change FT4, FT3, or TgAb

**Selenomethionine** (200 µg/day) appears to be the most effective form, superior to sodium selenite and selenium yeast.[17][18] A network meta-analysis found selenium was the only supplement that significantly reduced both TPOAb and TgAb in Hashimoto's thyroiditis, outperforming vitamin D, myo-inositol, and their combinations.[19]

---

**Zinc Supplementation and Thyroid Function**

Zinc plays a role in thyroid hormone metabolism through its involvement in deiodinase activity and thyroid hormone receptor binding. Evidence for zinc supplementation is more limited than for selenium:[20]

- A metabolic study in young men showed that **low zinc intake (5.5 mg/day for 54 days) decreased free T4** and tended to reduce TSH, with concurrent decreases in basal metabolic rate.[21]
- An RCT in overweight hypothyroid women found that zinc (30 mg/day) combined with selenium increased FT3 and FT4 and decreased TSH, though between-group differences were not all significant.[22]
- NHANES data (N = 6,402) demonstrated a significant **interaction between dietary selenium and zinc intake** on hypothyroidism risk: low zinc intake was associated with a 74% higher risk of new-onset hypothyroidism (OR 1.74, 95% CI 1.05–2.90), and the combined effect of low selenium + low zinc was synergistic (OR 5.99).[23]
- A systematic review concluded that evidence for zinc supplementation on thyroid hormones remains **inconclusive** due to heterogeneous study designs and populations, though zinc deficiency is consistently associated with impaired thyroid function.[20]

---

**Vitamin D Supplementation and Thyroid Disease Prevention**

The relationship between vitamin D and thyroid autoimmunity is supported by observational and genetic data, but **large RCT evidence for primary prevention is lacking**, particularly in men:

- The **D-Health Trial** (N = 21,315 Australians aged ≥60, 60,000 IU/month vitamin D3) found **no significant reduction in hypothyroidism incidence** overall (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.71–1.12). A possible benefit was suggested in females (HR 0.78) but **not in males** (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.74–1.50).[24]
- A Mendelian randomization study found that higher genetically predicted 25(OH)D levels were associated with a **50% reduced risk of autoimmune thyroiditis** (OR 0.499, 95% CI 0.289–0.860), suggesting a potential causal relationship.[25]
- In patients with **established Hashimoto's thyroiditis**, meta-analyses of RCTs show vitamin D supplementation reduces TPOAb (SMD −1.08 to −1.11) and TgAb titers, particularly with treatment >12 weeks and in those with baseline vitamin D deficiency. However, effects on TSH, FT3, and FT4 are inconsistent across studies.[26][27][28][29]
