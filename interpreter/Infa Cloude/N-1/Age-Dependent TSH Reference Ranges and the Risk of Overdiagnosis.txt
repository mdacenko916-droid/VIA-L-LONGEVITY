## Subclinical Hypothyroidism in Perimenopause: Who to Treat, Who to Monitor

**Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) — defined as elevated TSH with normal free T4 — affects up to 10% of the adult population**, is most commonly caused by autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis, and is particularly challenging in perimenopausal women because symptoms overlap extensively with those of estrogen decline.[1][2] The critical clinical decision hinges on **age, TSH level, TPO antibody status, and symptom burden** — with most guidelines recommending treatment for TSH ≥10 mIU/L and a more individualized approach for TSH 4.5–9.9 mIU/L in women [3][4]

---

**Age-Dependent TSH Reference Ranges and the Risk of Overdiagnosis**

TSH levels **rise physiologically with age** independent of thyroid disease. The traditional upper limit of normal (4.0–5.0 mIU/L) was derived from population-wide data and does not account for this age-related shift, leading to significant overdiagnosis — particularly in older adults.[1][3]

A landmark 2025 NHANES cross-sectional analysis (8,308 US participants) demonstrated that applying **age-, sex-, and race-specific reference intervals reclassified 48.5% of persons diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism as normal** — particularly women and White participants.[12] Similarly, a Dutch multicenter study (7.6 million TSH requests) found that TSH upper reference limits increased from age 50 in women and age 60 in men, and using age-specific ranges **decreased diagnoses of subclinical and overt hypothyroidism** in older adults.[15] A longitudinal analysis of the Whickham cohort confirmed that the calculated upper limit of TSH increased from 4.74 to 6.28 mIU/L over ~8 years of aging, and adopting visit-specific reference ranges reduced SCH prevalence from 9.0% to 2.0%.[16]

**For perimenopausal women (ages ~40–55):** The 97.5th percentile TSH is approximately **4.5–6.0 mIU/L**, meaning a TSH of 5.0–6.0 in this age group may represent the upper end of normal rather than true thyroid failure.[11][12][14]

---

**Age-Specific Thresholds for Initiating Therapy**

Guidelines from the ETA, ATA, and NICE converge on a **graded, age-dependent approach**:[3][11][4]

**TSH ≥10 mIU/L (Grade 2 SCH) — Treat regardless of age (in persons ≤70 years)**

- Levels above 10 mIU/L are unlikely to normalize spontaneously[3]
- Associated with increased cardiovascular risk and high progression rate to overt hypothyroidism[1][17]
- Levothyroxine 25–75 μg/day is typically sufficient[1]

**TSH 7.0–9.9 mIU/L — Consider treatment in women [1]

- The JAMA review notes treatment rationale is strongest when combined with symptoms or TPO antibody positivity[1]
- A 2024 UK population-based study (153,892 patients) found levothyroxine treatment of SCH (TSH 5–10 mIU/L) was associated with a **12% reduction in MACE** (HR 0.88; 95% CI 0.83–0.93), though residual confounding cannot be excluded[18]

**TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L — Generally monitor; consider therapeutic trial only if symptomatic**

- The ETA and NICE recommend considering a trial of levothyroxine in individuals **[3][4]
- Ross (2022) argues that treatment is generally not necessary unless TSH exceeds **7.0–10 mIU/L**, noting that **62% of elevated TSH levels may revert to normal spontaneously** and that in double-blinded RCTs, treatment does not improve symptoms or cognitive function if TSH is [11]

**TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L in women >65–70 years — Do not treat**

- A high TSH within the normal range appears **protective** and is associated with reduced mortality risk in older euthyroid individuals[3][4]
- Overtreatment risks include **atrial fibrillation, osteoporosis, and fractures** — complications particularly problematic in postmenopausal women[1][3]

**Spontaneous Resolution and Progression Risk**

The natural history of SCH is highly variable and depends on TSH level and TPO antibody status:[1][4][17]

**Spontaneous normalization rates:**

- **TSH [4]
- **TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L (Grade 1 SCH):** **60% normalize within 5 years**[1]
- **TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L without TPO antibodies:** **48% normalize within 2 years**[1]
- **62% of initially elevated TSH values** may revert to normal on repeat testing[11]

**Progression to overt hypothyroidism:**

- **Annual rate:** 2–6% per year overall[4][17]
- **TPO antibody-positive women:** ~4%/year with raised TSH + positive antibodies; cumulative incidence at 9 years is **59%** (vs. 23% in TPO-negative)[4][17]
- **TSH 10–14.9 mIU/L:** 40% progress to overt hypothyroidism over ~32 months[1]
- **TSH 15–19.9 mIU/L:** 85% progress to overt hypothyroidism[1]
- **Female sex, higher TSH, positive TPO antibodies, and high iodine intake** are independent risk factors for progression[17]

This underscores the critical importance of **confirming the diagnosis with repeat testing at 2–3 months** before initiating treatment, along with TPO antibody measurement at the confirmatory visit.[4][1]

**Screening Symptoms for Patient Questionnaires**

The challenge in perimenopause is that **many SCH symptoms are indistinguishable from estrogen deficiency**. A hypothyroidism symptom score (0–13 symptoms) has good discriminative ability in women [21] The following symptoms should be systematically assessed, with notation of which overlap with perimenopause:[4][21]

**Symptoms more suggestive of thyroid dysfunction (less typical of perimenopause alone):**

- **Cold intolerance** (35–65% of hypothyroid patients) — perimenopause more commonly causes heat intolerance/hot flashes
- **Constipation** (33–41%) — if new-onset and persistent
- **Bradycardia** (12–19%) — perimenopause more commonly causes palpitations/tachycardia
- **Dry, coarse skin / brittle nails / hair loss** (particularly lateral eyebrow thinning)
- **Hoarseness / deepening of voice**
- **Delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes**
- **Periorbital/facial puffiness or non-pitting edema**
- **Goiter or thyroid enlargement on palpation**

**Symptoms that overlap significantly with perimenopause:**

- **Fatigue** (68–86% in hypothyroidism; also the most common perimenopausal complaint)
- **Weight gain** (24–59%)
- **Depressed mood / anxiety** (common in both)
- **Cognitive impairment / "brain fog"** (45–48% in hypothyroidism)
- **Menstrual irregularities** (~23% — oligomenorrhea or menorrhagia)
- **Muscle weakness / joint pain**
- **Decreased libido**
- **Sleep disturbance**

**Recommended questionnaire approach for perimenopausal women:**

Given the extensive symptom overlap, **biochemical testing (TSH) should be the primary diagnostic tool** rather than symptom-based screening alone.[21] The USPSTF found insufficient evidence to recommend universal screening in asymptomatic adults, but the ATA recommends targeted screening in high-risk groups: personal or family history of thyroid/autoimmune disease, type 1 diabetes, prior neck radiation, vitiligo, pernicious anemia, or use of medications affecting thyroid function (lithium, amiodarone, immune checkpoint inhibitors).[21]

A practical screening questionnaire for perimenopausal women should include:

- **Thyroid-specific symptoms:** Cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair changes, hoarseness, facial puffiness, neck swelling
- **Overlapping symptoms requiring biochemical differentiation:** Fatigue severity and pattern, weight trajectory, mood changes, cognitive complaints, menstrual pattern changes
- **Risk factors for thyroid disease:** Family history of thyroid disease, personal history of autoimmune conditions, prior thyroid surgery/radiation, medication history (lithium, amiodarone, PPIs, immune checkpoint inhibitors)
- **Symptoms suggesting overtreatment risk (if already on levothyroxine):** Palpitations, tremor, heat intolerance, unintentional weight loss, anxiety, insomnia

---

**Perimenopause-Specific Considerations**

SCH in menopause can **worsen the cardiometabolic effects of estrogen loss** by exacerbating dyslipidemia and hypertension.[2] The combination of declining estrogen and subclinical thyroid failure creates a compounded risk for:

- **Dyslipidemia:** Both estrogen loss and SCH independently raise LDL cholesterol; the combination may accelerate atherogenesis[2][4]
- **Bone health:** Overtreatment with levothyroxine in postmenopausal women increases fracture risk — an already elevated baseline risk due to estrogen deficiency. A 2026 emulated target trial (22,621 patients) found no adverse bone effects with appropriately dosed levothyroxine (HR 1.04; 95% CI 0.93–1.17), but **15–38% of treated patients have suppressed TSH** indicating overreplacement[1][22]
- **Cardiovascular risk:** Levothyroxine treatment was associated with reduced cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, but **cardiovascular benefits were limited to patients with TSH levels above the age-specific range** and after at least 5 years of treatment[22]

“Subclinical hypothyroidism is common and most individuals can be observed without treatment. Treatment might be indicated for patients with subclinical hypothyroidism and serum thyrotropin levels of 10 mU/L or higher or for young and middle-aged individuals with subclinical hypothyroidism and symptoms consistent with mild hypothyroidism.”
— Bernadette Biondi, MD1,  et al.Department of Clinical Medicine and Surgery, University of Naples Federico II, Naples, Italy and other institutions
