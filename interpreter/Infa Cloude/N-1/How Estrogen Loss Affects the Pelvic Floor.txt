## Pelvic Floor Disorders in Perimenopause: Prolapse, Urinary Incontinence, and Evidence-Based Management

**Estrogen deficiency directly impairs all structural components of the pelvic floor** — promoting collagen degradation, pelvic floor muscle atrophy, and weakening of supportive ligaments — which contributes to both **pelvic organ prolapse (POP)** and **urinary incontinence (UI)**.[1][2][3] Approximately **50% of parous women** have some degree of prolapse on examination, and the prevalence of genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — which encompasses pelvic floor dysfunction — rises from **4% in perimenopause to 47% at 3 years postmenopause**.[4][5][6]

---

**How Estrogen Loss Affects the Pelvic Floor**

The pelvic floor continence mechanism depends on the coordinated function of muscles, connective tissue, ligaments, and neural innervation — all of which express estrogen receptors (ERα, ERβ, and GPER). Estrogen deficiency disrupts each component:[2][3][7][8]

**Connective tissue and collagen:** Estrogen promotes collagen and elastin synthesis while inhibiting matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity. After menopause, the balance shifts toward extracellular matrix degradation — a hallmark of POP. Estrogen also modulates fibroblast function, mitigating cellular senescence and enhancing migratory and anti-apoptotic capacities. An elevated ERα/ERβ ratio in prolapsed tissue contributes to this degradation. Estrogen integrates with mechanical signaling through the integrin-YAP/TAZ axis and suppresses chronic inflammation by influencing macrophage polarization.[2]

**Pelvic floor muscle atrophy:** In ovariectomized rat models, estrogen deficiency caused atrophy of both fast and slow muscle fibers in pelvic floor muscles, accompanied by decreased ERα expression. Mechanistically, ERα downregulation suppressed GLUT4 expression and its trafficking regulator RAC1, abolishing their critical co-localization, resulting in **defective glucose uptake, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired myoblast proliferation and differentiation**. Both ERα activation and GLUT4 overexpression rescued these defects.[3]

**Vaginal and urethral atrophy:** Estrogen loss leads to thinning of vaginal epithelium, loss of rugae, vaginal dryness, weakening of pelvic-supporting fascia and ligaments, and changes in vaginal pH — all contributing to reduced structural support.[7][8]

**The estrogen paradox:** Despite the clear biological rationale, the Women's Health Initiative demonstrated that **oral systemic MHT did not improve POP stage and worsened urinary incontinence**.[1] Conversely, basic science studies consistently show that estrogen loss promotes "weaker" pelvic tissues and that estrogen replacement decreases extracellular matrix degradation. **Local vaginal estrogen** may improve perioperative tissue quality, but systemic MHT has been shown to negatively impact vaginal mechanical properties.[1]

---

**Stress vs. Urgency Urinary Incontinence**

Understanding the distinction is critical because management strategies differ:[9][10]

**Stress urinary incontinence (SUI)** — involuntary leakage with increases in abdominal pressure (coughing, sneezing, exercise). Two mechanisms: (1) **urethral hypermobility** from loss of pelvic floor muscular/connective tissue support, and (2) **intrinsic sphincter deficiency** from loss of urethral mucosal and muscular tone. Overall prevalence among adult women is approximately **46%**, peaking at **50% in women ≥40 years**. More common in White women.[10]

**Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI)** — sudden compelling desire to urinate that is difficult to defer, with involuntary leakage. In most women, UUI is idiopathic, though it is common in neurologic conditions (Parkinson's, MS, pelvic nerve injury). More prevalent in Black women. The urinary microbiome in women with UUI may differ from the lactobacillus-predominant flora of continent women.[9]

**Mixed urinary incontinence** — coexisting stress and urgency symptoms — is common and often the most challenging to manage.[9]

**Vaginal delivery is the dominant obstetric risk factor.** A Swedish national register study (2.3 million women) found that **97.8% of women undergoing prolapse surgery** had ≥1 vaginal delivery. The first vaginal birth contributed the highest increase in absolute risk — **6-fold for POP surgery** and **3-fold for SUI surgery**. Cesarean delivery risk was on par with nulliparity.[13]

**Obesity** is an independent risk factor, with obese women having a **4.2-fold greater risk of SUI** than those with average BMI.[14]

**Symptoms for the Questionnaire**

Based on ACOG, JAMA, and NEJM guidelines, the following should be systematically assessed:[9][10][5]

**Urinary incontinence symptoms:**

- Leakage with coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting, or exercise (→ stress incontinence)
- Sudden, strong urge to urinate that is difficult to defer (→ urgency incontinence)
- Frequency of leakage episodes (daily/weekly/monthly)
- Amount of leakage (drops vs. soaking through clothing)
- Use and type of pads/protective garments
- Nocturia (number of nighttime voids)
- Degree of bother and impact on daily activities

**Prolapse symptoms:**

- Sensation of vaginal bulge or "something coming down"
- Pelvic pressure or heaviness, especially with prolonged standing
- Need to splint (digitally support) the vagina or perineum for voiding or defecation
- Incomplete bladder emptying
- Difficulty initiating urination

**Associated symptoms:**

- Defecatory dysfunction (straining, incomplete evacuation)
- Sexual dysfunction (dyspareunia, avoidance of intercourse)
- Vulvovaginal dryness, irritation (GSM overlap)

**Evidence-Based Pelvic Floor Muscle Training (PFMT / Kegel Exercises)**

PFMT is the **first-line treatment** for both stress and mixed urinary incontinence, with high-certainty evidence supporting its efficacy.[10][9][15][14]

**Effectiveness rates:**

- **Cure rate for SUI:** 76% of women with stress incontinence reported cure after PFMT vs. 9% with no treatment (high-certainty evidence from Cochrane review).[15]
- **Cure or improvement:** 74% of women reported cure or improvement after 3–6 months of PFMT vs. 11% with no treatment/inactive control (RR 6.33; moderate-quality evidence).[10]
- **12-month SUI cure rate:** 58.8% in supervised PFMT programs.[9]
- **Overall:** 62% of women significantly reduced their incontinence or were cured, with improved pelvic floor muscle contraction.[16]

“In a Cochrane review, 74% of women who underwent pelvic-floor muscle training for 3 to 6 months reported cure or improvement, as compared with 11% of women who received either no treatment or an inactive control treatment.”
— Jennifer M. Wu, M.D., M.P.H.University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

**Recommended protocols:**

There is no consensus on the single ideal protocol, but evidence supports the following parameters:[10][9][15][14]

- **Frequency:** At least **8–10 contractions per set, 3 sets per day** (i.e., ~30 contractions/day)
- **Hold duration:** Each contraction held for **6–10 seconds**, with equal rest between contractions
- **Duration:** Minimum **3–6 months** of consistent training before assessing efficacy
- **Supervision:** Programs with **weekly supervision by a healthcare professional** are associated with greater improvement than unsupervised programs[10]
- **"The Knack":** Voluntary pelvic floor contraction immediately before or during a cough/sneeze/lift — a functional training strategy that reduces leakage during provocative activities[17]
- **Important:** Patients should NOT be instructed to practice by interrupting their urine stream[9]

**When is biofeedback/specialist referral for PFMT indicated?**

A 2025 Cochrane review (PFMT with vs. without biofeedback) found that adding biofeedback **may increase patient satisfaction** (779 vs. 595 per 1,000) and **likely reduces leakage frequency** by ~1 fewer episode every 3–4 days, but has **little to no effect on quality of life** (high-certainty evidence) and **probably makes little to no difference to perception of cure** (moderate-certainty evidence).[17] A large multicenter RCT (600 women, 24-month follow-up) found **no evidence of any important difference** in UI severity between PFMT plus electromyographic biofeedback and PFMT alone, concluding that routine biofeedback should not be recommended.[18]

**Referral to a pelvic floor physical therapist** is beneficial for:[10][14]

- Women who have **difficulty isolating their pelvic floor muscles** (up to 30% of women cannot correctly contract on initial instruction)
- Women who have **not improved after 3 months** of self-directed PFMT
- Women with **complex pelvic floor dysfunction** (combined prolapse + incontinence + defecatory dysfunction)

---

**Lifestyle Interventions**

**Weight loss** has the strongest evidence among lifestyle modifications:[9][10][14][19]

The landmark **PRIDE trial** (338 overweight/obese women, NEJM 2009) demonstrated that a 6-month behavioral weight-loss program producing **~8% body weight loss** reduced stress incontinence episodes by **58%** (from 9 to 4 episodes/week) vs. 33% with education alone (P = 0.02). A **5–10% reduction in body weight** yielded 2–4× greater odds of achieving ≥70% reduction in incontinence episodes. Each 1 kg of weight loss was associated with a **3% decrease** in the odds of developing UI.[10][14][19]

**Bladder training:** Timed voiding (every 2–3 hours during the day) with gradual interval extension can reduce urgency incontinence episodes. Combined behavioral therapy (PFMT + bladder training + fluid management) was more effective than drug therapy alone for urgency incontinence (69% vs. 58% achieving ≥70% reduction in episodes).[9]

**Caffeine reduction:** Caffeine increases bladder muscle contractility. Intake equivalent to as little as **1 cup of coffee/day** is associated with UI. Reducing caffeine, carbonated beverages, diet drinks, and alcohol is recommended.[14][21]

**Fluid management:** Frequent small intakes (~4–5 oz/hour) up to **2 L/day** of predominantly water, rather than large episodic intakes. Reducing fluid intake several hours before bedtime for nocturia.[14][9]

---

**When to See a Urogynecologist**

**Referral to a specialist** (urogynecology, female pelvic medicine and reconstructive surgery) should be considered in the following situations:[5][22][10][23]

**For urinary incontinence:**

- Inadequate response to **3–6 months of conservative management** (PFMT, lifestyle modifications, bladder training)
- **Complicated SUI:** coexisting POP beyond the hymen, incomplete bladder emptying, prior anti-incontinence surgery, suspected intrinsic sphincter deficiency, neurologic conditions
- Desire for **surgical evaluation** (midurethral sling) — minimum pre-surgical evaluation includes history, urinalysis, physical exam with POP assessment, cough stress test, urethral mobility assessment, and postvoid residual measurement[23]
- **Urgency incontinence refractory** to behavioral therapy and pharmacotherapy

**For pelvic organ prolapse:**

- **Symptomatic prolapse** (bulge symptoms, voiding/defecatory dysfunction) — all symptomatic patients should be offered **nonsurgical treatment first** (PFMT or pessary trial) before considering surgery[5][22]
- **Pessary fitting:** Up to **92% of women can be successfully fitted**. Ring pessaries work best for stage II (100%) and stage III (71%); stage IV more frequently requires Gellhorn pessaries (64%). Follow-up every 3–4 months if the patient cannot self-manage the pessary.[5]
- **Surgical indications:** Failed conservative management in symptomatic women; recurrent prolapse; significant impact on quality of life. Approximately **13% of women** undergo surgery for prolapse in their lifetime. Pre-surgical evaluation should include POP-Q staging and assessment for occult SUI.[5][24]

**Key counseling point:** For asymptomatic prolapse (even stage II), **observation with education and reassurance** is appropriate — most women will have no change or only small increases over the next year. Only symptomatic prolapse or medical indications warrant treatment.[5][22]
