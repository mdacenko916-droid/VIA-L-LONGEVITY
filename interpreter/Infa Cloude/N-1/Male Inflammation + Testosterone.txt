## Male Inflammation + Testosterone: The Bidirectional T ↔ Inflammation Axis, Visceral Fat–Aromatase–Estrogen Pathway, and hs-CRP/Cardiovascular Risk in Men Over 40

The relationship between testosterone and inflammation in aging men is **bidirectional and self-reinforcing**: low testosterone promotes systemic inflammation (via reduced cytokine suppression and increased visceral adiposity), while inflammation directly suppresses the HPG axis and impairs Leydig cell function — creating a vicious cycle that accelerates cardiometabolic decline after age 40.[1][2][3] Visceral fat serves as the central amplifier through **aromatase-mediated conversion of testosterone to estradiol**, further depleting circulating androgens while fueling portal IL-6 and hepatic CRP production.[4][5] Men with testosterone below **7.4 nmol/L (213 ng/dL)** face increased all-cause mortality, and those below **5.3 nmol/L (153 ng/dL)** face increased cardiovascular mortality — thresholds that define the clinical urgency of breaking this cycle.[6]

**Direction 1: Inflammation → Testosterone Suppression**

Proinflammatory cytokines suppress testosterone production at **multiple levels** of the HPG axis:[1][2][7][8]

**Central (hypothalamic-pituitary) suppression:**

- IL-1β, TNF-α, and leptin bind to **kisspeptin neurons** in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, decreasing GnRH pulsatility and LH secretion[1][8]
- IL-6 negatively affects both central and peripheral parts of the HPG axis — the MESA study (n = 1,653 men) confirmed a significant inverse association between IL-6 and total/bioavailable testosterone, independent of hsCRP and estrogen[1]
- hsCRP itself may directly suppress LH production, as hypothesized in men with T2DM[1]
- A UK Biobank analysis (n = 17,617 men aged 40–70) found that IL-6 and GDF-15 **partially mediated** the inverse association between age and testosterone (P [9]

**Peripheral (testicular) suppression:**

- Endotoxin challenge in lean men produced a transient inflammatory response followed by a **decline in serum testosterone without changes in LH or FSH**, providing direct evidence that inflammation impairs Leydig cell steroidogenesis independently of central suppression[2]
- In obese men, gut-derived endotoxemia (measured by lipopolysaccharide-binding protein) was negatively correlated with testosterone and positively correlated with IL-6, suggesting that **metabolic endotoxemia** is a distinct pathway linking obesity-driven inflammation to androgen deficiency[2]
- High-fat diet in animal models induces hypothalamic inflammation, increased proinflammatory cytokine expression, and reduced kisspeptin-1 receptor expression — confirming that obesity-associated hypogonadism involves genuine HPG axis suppression, not merely SHBG-mediated artifact[7]

**Direction 2: Low Testosterone → Enhanced Inflammation**

Testosterone exerts direct **anti-inflammatory effects** that are lost in the hypogonadal state:[3][10][11]

- In 149 men (ages 18–77), testosterone and free testosterone were **inversely correlated with all acute-phase proteins** (CRP, ferritin, alpha-1-acid glycoprotein; P [3]
- Testosterone suppresses expression of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and potentiates the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 through NF-κB pathway inhibition and androgen receptor-mediated transcriptional regulation[10][11]
- Middle-aged/older men with low testosterone (269 ± 48 ng/dL) had significantly **worse endothelial function** (FMD 3.7% vs. 5.7%; P = 0.021) compared to age-matched men with higher testosterone (512 ± 115 ng/dL), with FMD correlating with IL-6 (r = −0.41) and CRP (r = −0.28). Acute vitamin C infusion rescued endothelial function only in the low-T group, confirming that oxidative stress mediates the vascular damage[12]

**2. Visceral Fat → Aromatase → Estrogen: The Central Amplifier**

Visceral adipose tissue is the **primary site of extragonadal estrogen production** in men, and this aromatization pathway is the mechanistic bridge connecting obesity to hypogonadism:[4][20][21][22][5]

**The aromatase pathway in men:**

CYP19A1 (aromatase) in adipocyte stromal-vascular cells converts testosterone to estradiol (E2) and androstenedione to estrone (E1). This process is **upregulated by the very inflammatory mediators** that visceral fat produces:[4][22][5]

- **TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and cyclooxygenase-2** — all elevated in visceral fat — are known inducers of aromatase gene transcription and enzymatic activity[5]
- Macrophage-derived proinflammatory mediators elevate aromatase expression in visceral fat adipocytes, creating a feed-forward loop: more inflammation → more aromatase → more estradiol → less testosterone → more fat → more inflammation[5]
- Aromatase expression in subcutaneous adipose tissue was **significantly higher in obese men** compared to non-obese men, and correlated positively with adiposity, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance markers[4]

**Estradiol-specific effects in men:**

The Gautier et al. MRI study in dysmetabolic men demonstrated a **strong and specific relationship between VAT (but not SAT) and estradiol**, and that the BT/E2 ratio decreased with increasing visceral adiposity — confirming that visceral fat specifically drives the aromatization process.[5] Key findings:

- E2 was positively associated with VAT but **not** with subcutaneous fat
- The association between VAT and E2 was mediated by low-grade inflammation (CRP)
- E2 was independently associated with γ-glutamyltransferase (a marker of fatty liver), suggesting estradiol may also promote hepatic steatosis[5]

**Metabolic consequences of elevated intracellular estradiol in men:**

- Estradiol activates ER-α in adipocytes → promotes adipogenesis and triglyceride uptake → further fat accumulation[22]
- E2 in combination with leptin and insulin causes insulin resistance through aberrant serine phosphorylation[22]
- Aberrant GPER (G protein-coupled estrogen receptor) signaling → vasoconstriction, dyslipidemia, and features of metabolic syndrome[22]
- ESR1 (estrogen receptor alpha) expression was **reduced** in SAT of obese men and men with T2DM, and correlated negatively with adiposity and insulin resistance — suggesting a paradox where high local E2 production coexists with impaired estrogen signaling[4]

The relationship between low testosterone and cardiovascular events is **nuanced**: while low T is consistently associated with **all-cause mortality** and markers of atherosclerosis (carotid intimal thickness, coronary artery disease severity, elevated hsCRP), the UK Biobank — the largest prospective study — found **no independent association** between low testosterone and incident MACE after full adjustment for confounders.[25][24] This suggests that low testosterone may be a **biomarker of poor cardiometabolic health** rather than a direct causal driver of cardiovascular events, with the shared mediator being visceral adiposity and its inflammatory consequences.[27][26]

**Aromatase inhibitors** effectively normalize testosterone biochemically but consistently **fail to improve clinical symptoms** (sexual function, strength, body composition, psychological measures) in RCTs, and carry a concerning signal of **bone density loss** at the lumbar spine due to estradiol suppression.[7][38][41][39] They are not recommended for routine clinical use in obesity-associated hypogonadism.

**6. Clinical Synthesis: The Integrated Vicious Cycle and Intervention Points**

The complete pathophysiological cascade in men over 40:

1. **Age + sedentary lifestyle + caloric excess** → visceral fat accumulation[1][20]
2. **VAT expansion** → macrophage infiltration → IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β secretion → portal IL-6 → hepatic CRP synthesis[1][5]
3. **Proinflammatory cytokines** → suppress kisspeptin/GnRH/LH centrally + impair Leydig cell steroidogenesis peripherally → **↓ testosterone**[2][7][8]
4. **VAT aromatase** (upregulated by inflammation) → converts testosterone to estradiol → further ↓ circulating T + E2-mediated negative feedback on HPG axis[4][5]
5. **Low testosterone** → reduced anti-inflammatory cytokine suppression → ↑ TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 → amplified inflammation[3][10]
6. **Low testosterone** → ↓ lean mass, ↑ adipogenesis → further VAT accumulation → cycle perpetuates[20][22][7]
7. **Chronic low-grade inflammation + endothelial dysfunction** → accelerated atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome → ↑ cardiovascular risk[12][26]

**Intervention hierarchy:**

- **First line:** Weight loss ≥5–10% (diet + exercise, especially resistance training) — breaks the cycle at the VAT node, reduces aromatase activity, lowers inflammation, and restores endogenous testosterone[35][36]
- **Second line:** Testosterone replacement therapy — for men with confirmed hypogonadism (T [32][35]
- **Not recommended:** Aromatase inhibitors for obesity-associated hypogonadism (biochemical improvement without clinical benefit; bone safety concerns)[7][41]
