## Biotin, Calcium, Iron, and PPIs: Critical Interferences with Thyroid Testing and Levothyroxine Therapy

**Biotin (vitamin B7) supplementation causes clinically dangerous interference with thyroid immunoassays**, producing **falsely low TSH and falsely elevated fT4/T3** — a pattern that exactly mimics Graves' disease and has led to misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.[1][2][3] Separately, **calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** impair levothyroxine absorption through distinct mechanisms, requiring strict timing separation of **≥4 hours** from levothyroxine administration.[1][4][5]

---

**1. Biotin Interference with Thyroid Immunoassays**

**Mechanism of interference:**

Modern thyroid immunoassays exploit the strong noncovalent binding between **biotin and streptavidin** as an anchor system. When a patient's serum contains excess biotin from supplementation, free biotin competes with biotinylated assay reagents for streptavidin binding sites, disrupting the assay in a predictable, platform-dependent manner:[1][6]

- **Sandwich (two-site) immunoassays** (used for TSH): Excess biotin prevents the biotinylated antibody-analyte complex from binding to the streptavidin-coated solid phase → signal is lost after washing → **falsely low or undetectable TSH**[1]
- **Competitive immunoassays** (used for fT4, fT3, total T4/T3): Excess biotin displaces the biotinylated analogue from streptavidin → less signal bound to the solid phase → **falsely elevated thyroid hormone levels**[1][7]

The prospective study by Ylli et al. (2021) demonstrated that even at **10 mg/day** (a dose found in many OTC supplements marketed for hair and nails), significant interference occurred on the **Roche Cobas 6000** platform: TSH was falsely lowered, fT4 falsely elevated, and TT3 falsely elevated. Maximal interference occurred **2 hours after biotin ingestion** on both day 1 and day 8 of supplementation. Critically, the **Abbott Architect** platform showed no significant interference at this dose, highlighting that the effect is **platform-dependent**.[2]

The JAMA study by Li et al. (2017) systematically evaluated 10 mg/day biotin in healthy adults and confirmed interference across multiple analytes — not just thyroid markers but also parathyroid hormone, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, troponin, NT-proBNP, and others.[13]

**Prevalence of biotin use:** Biotin is one of the most commonly used supplements in hair loss clinics — **81% of patients** in one study used supplements, with biotin being the most common.[14] OTC products marketed for hair, skin, and nails typically contain **2.5–10 mg** of biotin — up to **333 times the recommended daily intake** of 30 μg.[13][15] There is **no evidence** that biotin supplementation improves hair or nail growth in individuals without documented biotin deficiency.[16][14]

**Clinical case examples:**

- A patient taking biotin for hair loss presented with markedly abnormal thyroid function tests mimicking Graves' disease; after biotin cessation, TFTs normalized **far more rapidly than possible given the half-life of T4**, confirming assay interference rather than true thyroid disease[3]
- Four cases of OTC biotin causing erroneous thyroid diagnosis and clinical confusion in patients with preexisting thyroid disease, measured on the Vitros 5600 platform[17]

**The FDA has issued a safety communication** warning that biotin can significantly interfere with laboratory tests, including troponin (risking missed myocardial infarction) and thyroid function tests.[14]

**2. Calcium Carbonate — Chelation of Levothyroxine**

**Mechanism:** Calcium carbonate forms an **insoluble chelate** with levothyroxine in the gastrointestinal tract, physically binding the drug and preventing absorption. All calcium salts (carbonate, citrate, acetate) have similar effects.[5][19]

**Magnitude of effect:**

- Absorption studies in healthy volunteers show a **~20% decrement** in levothyroxine absorption with concurrent calcium ingestion[19]
- In hypothyroid patients, co-administration raises TSH significantly — the ATA Treatment Guidelines note this as a well-established interaction requiring a **4-hour separation**[19]
- The effect is clinically significant: calcium supplementation is one of the most common causes of **persistently elevated TSH despite apparently adequate levothyroxine replacement**[20]

**Perimenopausal relevance:** Calcium supplementation is frequently recommended for bone health in peri- and postmenopausal women — the very population with the highest prevalence of hypothyroidism and levothyroxine use. This creates a high-risk scenario for drug-supplement interaction.[20][21]

---

**3. Ferrous Sulfate — Formation of Ferric-Thyroxine Complex**

**Mechanism:** Ferrous sulfate likely forms a **ferric-thyroxine complex** that is poorly absorbed from the GI tract.[5][22]

**Magnitude of effect:**

- In a clinical trial of 14 hypothyroid patients, simultaneous ingestion of ferrous sulfate and levothyroxine increased mean TSH from **1.6 mIU/L to 5.4 mIU/L** — a clinically significant 3.4-fold rise[19]
- The FDA label mandates administration of levothyroxine **at least 4 hours apart** from ferrous sulfate[5][22]

**Perimenopausal relevance:** Iron deficiency is common in perimenopausal women (due to menorrhagia) and in Hashimoto's thyroiditis (due to comorbid autoimmune gastritis). Iron supplementation is frequently co-prescribed with levothyroxine, making this interaction particularly relevant.[20]

**Mechanism:** Levothyroxine tablets require an **acidic gastric environment (pH [1][23][24]

**Evidence:**

- A systematic review (Guzman-Prado et al. 2021; 7 studies) found that **all included studies showed an increase in TSH** following concomitant LT4 and PPI use, with the majority reaching statistical significance[23]
- The Lancet Hypothyroidism review (2024) lists PPI-induced hypochlorhydria as a key cause of levothyroxine malabsorption alongside H. pylori infection and autoimmune atrophic gastritis[20]
- The ATA Treatment Guidelines note conflicting findings: short-term PPI use (1 week) in healthy volunteers did not alter LT4 absorption, but **chronic PPI therapy** in hypothyroid patients was associated with increased TSH — suggesting the effect requires sustained acid suppression[19]

- **Increase levothyroxine dose** and monitor TSH[1]

- Both novel formulations contain **pre-dissolved levothyroxine** that bypasses the need for gastric acid dissolution[26][27][28]

**Optimal levothyroxine administration protocol:**

The 2025 JAMA Hypothyroidism Review and the 2014 ATA Treatment Guidelines recommend:[4][19]

1. Take levothyroxine **on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before breakfast** with water only
2. Alternatively, take at **bedtime ≥2 hours after the last meal** (equally effective)
3. Maintain **≥4 hours** between levothyroxine and calcium, iron, or bile acid sequestrants
4. For patients on chronic PPIs: consider switching to **liquid or soft gel capsule** formulations
5. **Recheck TSH 4–8 weeks** after starting or stopping any potentially interfering medication or supplement
6. **Always ask about biotin** before ordering thyroid function tests

“Patients should be instructed to avoid taking levothyroxine within 4 hours of ingesting calcium or iron supplements or other medications that can interfere with absorption (such as proton pump inhibitors and phosphate binders).”
— Layal Chaker, MD, MPH, PhD,  et al.Academic Centre for Thyroid Disease, Erasmus Medical Center Rotterdam, Rotterdam, the Netherlands and other institutions
