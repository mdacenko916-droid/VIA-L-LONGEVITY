## The HPA Axis, Diurnal Cortisol Rhythm, HPA Dysregulation, and "Adrenal Fatigue"

The **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis** is the central neuroendocrine stress response system, producing cortisol in a robust **circadian rhythm** — peaking at **06:30–09:00 h** (mean ~21.5 µg/dL by LC-MS/MS) and reaching a nadir at **22:00–02:00 h** — with superimposed **ultradian (pulsatile) secretion** every 60–90 minutes.[1][2][3][4] **"Adrenal fatigue" is not a recognized medical diagnosis** by any endocrine society; however, **HPA axis dysregulation** — characterized by altered cortisol rhythmicity, enhanced or impaired negative feedback, and blunted stress responsiveness — is a well-documented phenomenon in chronic stress, burnout, and chronic fatigue syndrome.[5][6]

---

**Normal HPA Axis Physiology and the Diurnal Cortisol Rhythm**

The HPA axis operates through a three-tier hormonal cascade: the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) secretes **CRH** (corticotropin-releasing hormone) and arginine vasopressin → the anterior pituitary releases **ACTH** → the adrenal zona fasciculata produces **cortisol**. Cortisol then exerts negative feedback at both the hypothalamus and pituitary to self-regulate the axis.[1][7]

The diurnal cortisol rhythm is governed by the **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)**, the master circadian pacemaker, which is entrained by the light-dark cycle. Key features of the normal rhythm include:[2][3][8]

- **Cortisol awakening response (CAR):** A rapid 50–75% increase in cortisol within the first **30–45 minutes after waking**, proposed to mobilize resources for anticipated daily demands and to counterregulate prior-day negative emotional experiences.[9]
- **Morning peak (acrophase):** 06:30–09:00 h, with geomean peak of **594 nmol/L (21.5 µg/dL)** (10th–90th centile: 409–973 nmol/L).[3]
- **Progressive decline** throughout the day, entering a **quiescent phase** from approximately 16:30–02:00 h.[3]
- **Nadir:** 22:00–02:00 h, with cortisol levels at their lowest.[3][8]
- **Nocturnal rise:** Cortisol begins rising again around 03:00–06:30 h, driven by the SCN, before the next awakening.[3]

Critically, cortisol is not secreted continuously but in **ultradian pulses** — approximately 15–18 secretory bursts per 24 hours — and it is the modulation of pulse amplitude (not frequency) that creates the circadian rhythm. This pulsatile pattern is essential for proper glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling; continuous cortisol exposure (as with synthetic glucocorticoids) produces different genomic effects than pulsatile exposure.[4]

Two receptor types mediate cortisol's effects in the brain: the high-affinity **mineralocorticoid receptor (MR)**, which is substantially occupied even at basal cortisol levels and regulates tonic HPA activity, and the low-affinity **glucocorticoid receptor (GR)**, which is occupied during peak and stress-induced cortisol levels and mediates the stress response and negative feedback.[4][1]

---

**What Is HPA Axis Dysregulation?**

HPA axis dysregulation refers to **alterations in cortisol secretion patterns, feedback sensitivity, and stress responsiveness** that deviate from the normal circadian and reactive profile. It is not a single disease but a spectrum of functional changes observed across multiple conditions.[10][11]

A 2025 integrative review in the American Journal of Medicine describes HPA axis dysfunction as influenced by a complex interplay of chronic psychological stress, dietary imbalances, disrupted circadian rhythms, environmental exposures, gut health, inflammation, and hormonal imbalances.[10]

**The Allostatic Load Model** provides the most coherent framework for understanding HPA dysregulation. Under chronic stress, the HPA axis initially hyperactivates (elevated cortisol), but with prolonged exposure, the system undergoes **allostatic adaptation** — a recalibration that can manifest as enhanced negative feedback, reduced CRH drive, and ultimately **hypocortisolism**.[30][31][28] This progression from hyperactivation to hypoactivation has been described as a biphasic trajectory:[26]

1. **Initial phase:** Chronic stress → sustained HPA activation → elevated cortisol → disrupted negative feedback → metabolic consequences (insulin resistance, visceral fat, immunosuppression).[23]
2. **Late phase:** Prolonged stress → enhanced negative feedback sensitivity → reduced CRH/ACTH drive → **mild hypocortisolism** with attenuated diurnal variation → symptoms of fatigue, inflammation, and poor stress tolerance.[6][26]

A study of healthy workers found that higher allostatic load indices were associated with increased burnout symptoms and **lower morning and stress-reactive cortisol levels** — the hypocortisolemic profile characteristic of late-stage HPA dysregulation.[28]

**"Adrenal Fatigue": Why It Is Not a Recognized Diagnosis**

The term "adrenal fatigue" was coined in 1998 by a naturopath and proposes that chronic stress exhausts the adrenal glands' ability to produce cortisol, leading to suboptimal cortisol output and symptoms of fatigue, brain fog, and poor stress tolerance. **No major endocrine society recognizes this as a valid diagnosis:**

- The **AACE/ACE (2020) Position Statement** explicitly states that "adrenal fatigue" is "a condition not recognized by endocrinology specialists." The many tests proposed to diagnose it "have not been well studied to develop reference standards." Supplements marketed for "adrenal support" may contain undisclosed thyroid and steroid hormones, and treatment for adrenal fatigue "risks masking the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency or other endocrine disorders."[5]
- Supplementation with **pregnenolone or DHEA does not improve well-being** and commonly causes dermatologic and androgenic symptoms. **Hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone do not improve energy** in chronic fatigue syndrome.[5]

**Why the concept is problematic:**

1. **The adrenal glands do not "fatigue."** The adrenal cortex has enormous reserve capacity. Even under extreme physiological stress (sepsis, major surgery), the adrenals can produce 200–300 mg of cortisol daily — 10–15 times the normal daily output. True adrenal insufficiency requires destruction of >90% of the adrenal cortex (primary) or significant pituitary/hypothalamic pathology (secondary/tertiary).[35][7][36]
2. **The symptoms attributed to "adrenal fatigue" are nonspecific** and overlap with dozens of conditions: hypothyroidism, depression, sleep disorders, anemia, diabetes, chronic infection, and true adrenal insufficiency itself. Labeling these as "adrenal fatigue" delays proper diagnosis.[7][37]
3. **What is actually happening physiologically** in chronically stressed, fatigued individuals is **HPA axis dysregulation at the central (hypothalamic/pituitary) level** — not adrenal gland failure. The evidence consistently shows altered CRH drive, enhanced negative feedback, and blunted ACTH pulsatility, with the adrenal glands themselves remaining capable of responding to exogenous ACTH stimulation (i.e., normal cosyntropin stimulation test).[6][38]

**Evidence-Based Approaches to HPA Axis Support**

For individuals with symptoms suggestive of HPA dysregulation (fatigue, poor stress tolerance, sleep disruption) who have been appropriately evaluated and do **not** have true adrenal insufficiency:

- **Sleep optimization** is paramount — even modest sleep restriction significantly alters cortisol rhythmicity and HPA stress responsiveness.[32][33][34]
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** can increase cortisol levels in CFS patients and is considered the first-line approach for correcting HPA axis dysfunction, as steroid replacement is not recommended.[6]
- **Regular physical activity** helps restore normal cortisol rhythmicity and improves stress resilience.
- **Circadian hygiene** — consistent sleep-wake timing, morning light exposure, evening light restriction — supports SCN-driven cortisol rhythm entrainment.[2][33]
- **Addressing modifiable contributors:** depression, low activity levels, and early-life stress appear to reduce cortisol levels, while psychotropic medication can increase cortisol.[6]
- **Adaptogens** (ashwagandha, rhodiola) have some preliminary evidence for modulating cortisol patterns, as discussed in the earlier section on adaptogens.
