## When hs-CRP Is Insufficient: Advanced Inflammatory Biomarkers, Ferritin, Fibrinogen, CRP Thresholds for CV Decision-Making, and CRP Reference Ranges in Men Over 40

While hs-CRP remains the **cornerstone inflammatory biomarker** for cardiovascular risk stratification — with the 2025 ACC Scientific Statement now recommending **universal screening** in both primary and secondary prevention — it has important limitations: high intraindividual variability (CV ~29%), susceptibility to acute-phase confounders, and inability to capture all inflammatory pathways driving atherosclerosis.[1][2] Advanced markers such as **GlycA, ferritin (as an inflammatory marker), and fibrinogen** each capture distinct aspects of the inflammatory milieu and can provide additive prognostic information, particularly when hs-CRP results are discordant with clinical suspicion.[3][4][5][6]

**The ≥2 mg/L treatment threshold — JUPITER and beyond:**

The **≥2 mg/L** threshold has emerged as the key clinical decision point for pharmacological intervention, based on converging evidence:[1][10][12]

- **JUPITER trial:** Rosuvastatin 20 mg reduced first MACE by **47%** (HR 0.53; 95% CI 0.40–0.69; P [10]
- **Collaborative analysis of PROMINENT, REDUCE-IT, and STRENGTH** (n = 31,245 statin-treated patients): Residual inflammatory risk (hsCRP ≥2 mg/L) was a **stronger predictor** of MACE, CV death, and all-cause death than residual cholesterol risk (LDL-C ≥70 mg/dL). Patients with hsCRP ≥2 mg/L had approximately **double the risk** of CV death and all-cause death compared to those with hsCRP [12]
- **2025 ACC consensus:** Persistently elevated hsCRP should lead to consideration of statin initiation or intensification **irrespective of LDL-C** in both primary and secondary prevention[1]
- **2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline:** Lists hsCRP as a risk enhancer that can support statin initiation decisions at borderline estimated 10-year ASCVD risk[13]

**The dual residual risk framework:**

Ridker's framework distinguishes between **"residual cholesterol risk"** (LDL-C >70 mg/dL on statin) and **"residual inflammatory risk"** (hsCRP >2 mg/L on statin) — recognizing that these represent distinct pathophysiological drivers requiring different therapeutic strategies:[14]

- In the PROVE-IT and IMPROVE-IT trials, **one-third** of all patients had post-statin hsCRP >2 mg/L despite achieving LDL-C [14]
- For residual cholesterol risk → ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors
- For residual inflammatory risk → colchicine (FDA-approved), with IL-6 inhibitors (ziltivekimab) in clinical trials[1]

**Colchicine — the first FDA-approved anti-inflammatory for ASCVD:**

Low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg daily) is now FDA-approved for reducing CV events in established atherosclerotic disease, based on:[15][16][1]

- **COLCOT** (post-MI): 23% reduction in primary composite endpoint (HR 0.77; P = 0.02)[15]
- **LoDoCo2** (chronic coronary disease): 31% reduction in CV events (HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.57–0.83; P [16]
- **LoDoCo2 biomarker substudy:** Colchicine reduced median hsCRP from 1.34 to **0.80 mg/L** (P [17]
- **Combined COLCOT + LoDoCo-MI analysis:** Colchicine increased the odds of achieving hsCRP ≤1.0 mg/L by **64%** (OR 1.64; 95% CI 1.07–2.51; P = 0.024)[18]
- **Updated meta-analysis (including CLEAR-SYNERGY):** Overall **25% relative risk reduction** in MACE (HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.56–0.93)[1]

**2. GlycA: The Composite NMR Inflammatory Biomarker**

**GlycA** (glycoprotein acetyls) is a composite NMR-measured biomarker reflecting the integrated concentration and glycosylation complexity of multiple acute-phase proteins — primarily **α1-acid glycoprotein, haptoglobin, α1-antitrypsin, α1-antichymotrypsin, and transferrin**.[2][19] Its key advantage over hsCRP is **dramatically greater stability** as a measure of chronic inflammation.

**Key clinical advantages of GlycA:**

- **Stability:** The intraindividual CV of GlycA (4.3%) is **nearly 7-fold lower** than hsCRP (29.2%), meaning a single GlycA measurement more reliably reflects chronic inflammatory status than a single hsCRP measurement[2]
- **Captures distinct inflammatory pathways:** GlycA and hsCRP are only moderately correlated (r ≈ 0.5), indicating they reflect overlapping but different inflammatory processes. GlycA remains associated with CVD after adjusting for hsCRP, and vice versa[3][23]
- **Additive risk prediction:** In the Intermountain Heart Collaborative Study (n = 2,996, 7-year follow-up), patients with both high GlycA and high hsCRP had MACE rates of **49.1%** vs. 33.5% for those with both low (P-trend [4]
- **MESA study (n = 6,523, 12.1 years):** GlycA predicted total death, CVD, chronic inflammatory disease, and total cancer after adjustment for hsCRP, IL-6, and d-dimer. Notably, **only GlycA** (not hsCRP) predicted total cancer[5]
- **Better in young adults:** In the Cardiovascular Risk in Young Finns Study and ALSPAC cohort, GlycA predicted future hypertension and metabolic syndrome over 9–10 years, while CRP did not — CRP appeared predominantly driven by BMI in younger populations[24]

**When to consider GlycA:**

- When hsCRP is discordant with clinical suspicion (e.g., normal hsCRP in a patient with multiple metabolic risk factors)
- When hsCRP is acutely elevated and chronic inflammatory status needs assessment
- When available through NMR LipoProfile® testing (which simultaneously provides lipoprotein particle data)
- As a more stable longitudinal monitoring tool for anti-inflammatory interventions

**3. Ferritin as an Inflammatory Biomarker (Beyond Iron Status)**

Ferritin has a **dual identity**: it is both the primary clinical marker of iron stores and a potent **acute-phase reactant** whose elevation signals systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation — particularly relevant in men over 40 where iron accumulation and chronic inflammation converge.[6][25]

**Ferritin–inflammation axis in men:**

- In 149 men (ages 18–77), testosterone and free testosterone were **inversely correlated with ferritin** (P [29]
- A Mendelian randomization study (n = 62,537) using the HFE C282Y/C282Y genotype as an instrument demonstrated that increased ferritin is **causally associated** with low-grade inflammation (elevated CRP and complement C3), with 74% of the genetic association with CRP mediated through ferritin concentration[30]
- In the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (n = 5,471, age ≥52), men in the **highest ferritin quartile (194–598 ng/mL)** without major chronic diseases had significantly increased all-cause mortality (HR 1.49; 95% CI 1.03–2.16) and cardiovascular mortality. This association was partially attenuated after adjustment for inflammatory markers, confirming that ferritin's prognostic value is partly mediated through inflammation[26]
- Ferritin promotes atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms: catalyzing formation of reactive oxygen species, oxidizing atherogenic lipoproteins, controlling both innate and adaptive immune responses within the arterial wall, and affecting macrophage, endothelial cell, smooth muscle cell, and platelet function[31]

**Interpreting ferritin in the inflammatory context:**

To distinguish iron overload from inflammation-driven ferritin elevation, the following adjunctive tests are essential:

- **Transferrin saturation:** >45–55% suggests true iron overload; normal (20–50%) with elevated ferritin suggests inflammation[6][28]
- **Concurrent hsCRP and IL-6:** If elevated alongside ferritin, inflammation is the likely driver
- **Soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR):** Not elevated in inflammation-driven ferritin elevation; elevated in true iron deficiency
- **Ferritin/sTfR ratio:** Helps distinguish iron deficiency from inflammation in the setting of chronic disease

**4. Fibrinogen: The Coagulation–Inflammation Bridge**

Fibrinogen is a hepatic acute-phase glycoprotein that uniquely bridges inflammation and thrombosis — it is both a coagulation factor (Factor I) and an inflammatory mediator that directly promotes atherosclerosis.[32][33][34]

**Cardiovascular risk data:**

- **Individual participant meta-analysis** (31 prospective studies, n = 154,211): A **1 g/L increase** in plasma fibrinogen was associated with an approximate **doubling of risk** for CHD (HR ~1.8 after adjustment for established risk factors), stroke, other vascular mortality, and nonvascular mortality. This association was **independent of CRP** in the subset with available data[33]
- **Meta-analyses of 18–22 prospective studies:** Risk ratio of **1.8–2.0** (95% CI 1.6–2.0) for individuals in the upper vs. lower tertile of fibrinogen[32]
- Fibrinogen promotes atherosclerosis through: regulation of cell adhesion and chemotaxis, vasoconstriction at injury sites, stimulation of platelet aggregation, determination of blood viscosity, upregulation of TNF-α/IL-1/IL-6, and macrophage activation[32][35]

**Clinical limitation:** Despite strong epidemiological associations, the 2025 ACC Scientific Statement concludes that fibrinogen and other alternative inflammatory biomarkers "generally have not proven superior to hsCRP and often are not widely available in standardized commercial formats," and that "routine evaluation of these adds little to hsCRP." Fibrinogen measurement is most useful when assessing coagulation status concurrently with inflammatory burden, or when hsCRP is unreliable.[1]

**Age-related CRP changes in men:**

- The CDC/AHA workshop found **no significant relation** between age and CRP for individuals aged 20–70 years in most studies[11]
- However, a 12-year prospective cohort study using GAMLSS modeling showed that in men, smoothed centile curves of hsCRP **gradually increased from age 35 onwards**, with CRP increasing over the course of adulthood for nearly all birth cohorts — driven primarily by intracohort change (aging effects) rather than cohort replacement[41][42]
- The NHANES III-derived formula for the age-adjusted upper reference limit in men is approximately **CRP = age/50** (in mg/dL). For a 40-year-old man, this yields an upper limit of ~0.8 mg/dL (8.0 mg/L); for a 50-year-old, ~1.0 mg/dL (10.0 mg/L)[39]
- The Copenhagen Sarcopenia Study confirmed significant increases in hsCRP and TNF-α with aging, with elderly participants displaying significantly higher levels than both young and middle-aged participants — explained at least partly by increasing visceral fat[43]

**Sex-specific considerations:**

- CRP distributions are **comparable in men and women** not taking HRT. Women on HRT have approximately double the CRP levels of non-users[11][40]
- The CDC/AHA workshop concluded that **no sex- or ethnicity-specific decision points are warranted** for cardiovascular risk assessment with CRP — the 3 mg/L thresholds apply universally[11]
- However, the association between elevated hsCRP and all-cause mortality was stronger in subjects **aged [41]

**The testosterone–CRP connection in men over 40:**

The BACH Survey (n = 1,559 men, ages 30–79) demonstrated a **robust, inverse dose-response correlation** between testosterone (total and free) and CRP levels, persisting after adjustment for age, BMI, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors.[44] This confirms that the age-related testosterone decline in men directly contributes to rising inflammatory burden, creating the bidirectional vicious cycle discussed in the previous response — and underscores why hsCRP screening is particularly informative in men over 40 experiencing andropause.

The 2025 ACC Scientific Statement represents a paradigm shift: hsCRP is no longer merely a "risk enhancer" but is recommended for **universal screening** alongside LDL-C and Lp(a), with the explicit recognition that "clinicians will not treat what they do not measure." For men over 40 — where declining testosterone, accumulating visceral fat, and rising aromatase activity converge to amplify systemic inflammation — hsCRP screening provides a measurable, actionable target that can guide lifestyle interventions, statin therapy, and potentially colchicine in those with established ASCVD.[1]
