## The Male Thyroid–Testosterone Axis: Hypothyroidism, Hypogonadism, and Cardiovascular Risk in Men

The thyroid and gonadal axes are **bidirectionally linked** in men: primary hypothyroidism causes **hypogonadotropic hypogonadism with reduced free testosterone**, which is reversible with levothyroxine replacement, while testosterone therapy reciprocally **decreases thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)** and can unmask or alter thyroid disease.[1][2][3] A recent Mendelian randomization study confirms a **causal association** between hypothyroidism and decreased SHBG and testosterone levels.[4] In men over 40, where both testosterone decline and rising TSH converge, this bidirectional relationship has significant implications for metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular risk, sexual function, and clinical management.

---

**1. Hypothyroidism → Low Testosterone: Mechanisms and Evidence**

**Primary hypothyroidism causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism** — the defect is at the hypothalamic-pituitary level, with subnormal LH responses to GnRH stimulation and reduced free testosterone concentrations that **normalize with thyroid hormone replacement**.[2][5]

**Mechanisms by which hypothyroidism suppresses testosterone:**

- **Blunted GnRH/LH pulsatility:** Thyroid hormone deficiency impairs hypothalamic GnRH pulse generation, leading to inappropriately normal or low LH/FSH despite low testosterone — a central (hypogonadotropic) pattern. LH and FSH fail to rise appropriately, placing the defect at the hypothalamus-pituitary level.[2][5]
- **Reduced Leydig cell steroidogenesis:** Thyroid hormone receptors (TRα1, TRβ) are expressed on Leydig cells, Sertoli cells, and germ cells. T3 acutely stimulates StAR protein expression and progesterone/testosterone production via the cAMP-PKA pathway, but chronic T3 deficiency reduces cholesterol delivery to the inner mitochondrial membrane and impairs steroidogenic enzyme expression.[6][7][8][9]
- **Reduced LDL-cholesterol uptake by Leydig cells:** Hypothyroidism raises circulating LDL-C but paradoxically reduces LDL-C uptake by Leydig cells, limiting the cholesterol substrate available for testosterone synthesis.[10]
- **Decreased SHBG:** Unlike hyperthyroidism (which raises SHBG), hypothyroidism lowers SHBG, reducing total testosterone. Critically, **free testosterone is also reduced** — direct measurement confirmed this, overturning the earlier assumption that only total testosterone was affected.[5]
- **Mild hyperprolactinemia:** Present in some cases of severe or longstanding hypothyroidism, further suppressing GnRH pulsatility.[2][10]
- **Increased aromatase activity:** Some evidence suggests a higher rate of testosterone-to-estradiol conversion in hypothyroid men, further depleting bioavailable testosterone.[10]

**Reversibility:** Free testosterone concentrations **return to normal** with levothyroxine replacement therapy, confirming a direct causal relationship rather than mere association.[2][5] This is a critical clinical point: **in men presenting with low testosterone, thyroid function should be assessed before initiating testosterone replacement**, as levothyroxine alone may restore eugonadism.[1][5]

**2. Testosterone → Thyroid: The Reverse Direction**

Androgens exert clinically significant effects on thyroid hormone economy, primarily through **TBG modulation**:[3][11]

**Androgen effects on TBG and thyroid parameters:**

- Testosterone therapy **decreases TBG by ~14%** (in transsexual studies) by accelerating hepatic TBG clearance.[12]
- Total T4 and total T3 decrease proportionally, but **free T4 and TSH remain unchanged** in men with normal thyroid glands — clinical euthyroidism is maintained.[13][14][11]
- Testosterone **increases the T3/T4 ratio by ~30%**, suggesting enhanced peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 (increased deiodinase activity).[12]
- High-dose anabolic steroids in bodybuilders caused significant TBG, total T4, and total T3 reductions, with an exaggerated TSH response to TRH and diminished T3 response — suggesting **mild thyroidal impairment** at supraphysiological androgen doses.[15]
- In a rat model, high-dose testosterone enanthate combined with resistance training significantly **increased TSH and decreased T4**, suggesting primary hypothyroidism as a complication of supraphysiological androgen exposure.[16]

**3. Subclinical Hypothyroidism, Metabolic Syndrome, and Cardiovascular Risk in Men**

SCH in men carries **sex-specific metabolic and cardiovascular risks** that are distinct from those in women:[18][19][20]

**Metabolic syndrome:** A population-based cohort study (3,615 participants; median follow-up 3 years) found that SCH was significantly associated with incident metabolic syndrome **only in men** (adjusted HR **1.87**; 95% CI 1.21–2.90), with no increased risk in women or in persons aged ≥50 years.[18] This sex-specific vulnerability may reflect the compounding effects of declining testosterone and rising TSH in aging men.

**Cardiovascular risk:** An individual participant data meta-analysis (>55,000 participants) demonstrated that cardiovascular risk increases with TSH level in a graded fashion:[21]

- TSH 4.5–6.9 mIU/L: HR 1.00 (no increased risk)
- TSH 7.0–9.9 mIU/L: HR 1.17 (95% CI 0.96–1.43)
- **TSH 10.0–19.9 mIU/L: HR 1.89** (95% CI 1.28–2.80; P 7 mIU/L, and with **increased carotid intima-media thickness** (a surrogate for subclinical atherosclerosis) in grade 2 SCH (TSH ≥10).[19][20]

**Dyslipidemia:** Hypothyroidism is one of the most frequent secondary causes of dyslipidemia. A meta-analysis of 16 observational studies confirmed elevated total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides in grade 2 SCH, with weaker evidence for HDL-C changes. Screening for hypothyroidism is recommended in individuals with hypercholesterolemia.[19]

**The convergence of low T + high TSH in aging men:**

Both testosterone and thyroid function decline with age in men, creating a **compounded cardiometabolic risk profile**:[22][21]

- Lower testosterone is independently associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and mortality in middle-aged and older men[22]
- Higher TSH is independently associated with increased BMI, waist circumference, and insulin resistance[21]
- The combination may accelerate the progression from subclinical to clinically significant metabolic disease
- In older men (>65 years), one-fifth develop subclinical or overt hypothyroidism over 8 years, with age and baseline TSH >2.85 mIU/L predicting progression[23]

**4. Sexual Dysfunction: The Thyroid Connection**

Thyroid dysfunction is a **significantly underrecognized cause of male sexual dysfunction**:[24][25][26]

**Prevalence of sexual dysfunction in thyroid disease:**

- **Hypothyroid men:** 64.3% report hypoactive sexual desire, 64.3% delayed ejaculation, 7.1% premature ejaculation, and 64.3% erectile dysfunction[24]
- **ED prevalence:** 78.9% of men with thyroid dysfunction (hypo- or hyperthyroid) had SHIM scores ≤21 (indicating ED), compared with 33.8% of controls (P [25]
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism:** Even men with SCH show impaired erectile function scores on IIEF-15, though other sexual domains may be preserved.[26]

**Reversibility with treatment:** Levothyroxine therapy **normalized erectile function** in men with SCH and **improved all five IIEF-15 domains** in men with overt hypothyroidism within 6 months.[26] SHIM scores improved significantly in both hypo- and hyperthyroid men after achieving euthyroidism.[25]

**Clinical recommendation:** Screening for thyroid dysfunction is recommended in men presenting with ED, and **specific ED treatment (PDE-5 inhibitors) should be postponed for at least 6 months** after achieving euthyroidism, as thyroid normalization alone may resolve the dysfunction.[25][27]

**The bidirectional relationship summarized:** Hypothyroidism suppresses testosterone through central (GnRH/LH) and peripheral (Leydig cell) mechanisms, while testosterone therapy modulates thyroid hormone binding and peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. In men over 40, the age-related convergence of declining testosterone and rising TSH creates a compounded risk for metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and sexual dysfunction — making integrated assessment of both axes essential in this population.
