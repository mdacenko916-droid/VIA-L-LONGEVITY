# The Influence of Age and Perimenopause on Estrogen Metabolism and the Role of Genetic Polymorphisms

The perimenopause is characterized by **paradoxical hyperestrogenism with progesterone deficiency** in the early stages, followed by a precipitous estradiol decline around the final menstrual period (FMP) — a trajectory significantly modulated by **genetic polymorphisms in estrogen-synthesizing (CYP19A1), hydroxylating (CYP1A1, CYP1B1), and conjugating (COMT, SULT1A1, UGT1A1) enzymes** that determine the balance between protective and genotoxic estrogen metabolites.[1][2][3][4]

---

**Hormonal Dynamics Across the Menopausal Transition (STRAW+10 Staging)**

The STRAW+10 staging system divides reproductive aging into 7 stages centered on the FMP.[5][6] The hormonal changes are not a simple linear decline but a complex, often erratic process:

- **Late reproductive stage (Stage −3):** Regular cycles but rising FSH, declining inhibin B and AMH. Estradiol remains normal or slightly elevated due to FSH-driven follicular recruitment.[7][8]
- **Early menopausal transition (Stage −2):** Cycle variability ≥7 days. FSH rises further; estradiol is often **elevated above midreproductive levels** (mean estrone conjugate excretion 76.9 vs. 40.7 ng/mg Cr in younger women, P = 0.023), while luteal progesterone is significantly diminished. This creates a state of **unopposed estrogen** that may account for increased gynecological morbidity (menorrhagia, endometrial hyperplasia, fibroids) during this period.[1][8][9]
- **Late menopausal transition (Stage −1):** Amenorrhea ≥60 days. Estradiol begins its precipitous decline ~2 years before the FMP, with the steepest drop occurring within 6 months of the FMP. The E1:E2 ratio increases as ovarian estradiol production fails and peripheral conversion of androstenedione to estrone becomes the dominant estrogen source.[10][11]
- **Postmenopause (Stages +1/+2):** Estradiol falls to 10–20 pg/mL; estrone (30–70 pg/mL) becomes the predominant circulating estrogen, derived almost entirely from **peripheral aromatization of adrenal androstenedione in adipose tissue**. Body weight strongly correlates with postmenopausal estrogen levels — obese women maintain significantly higher estrone and estradiol through increased adipose aromatase activity.[12][13][11]

A key finding from the SWAN study is that **not all women experience a uniform estradiol decline**: 44.5% of midlife women actually show a significant estradiol rise 5.5 years before the FMP before declining, while 55.5% show a slow decline or flat pattern.[10]

**Estrogen Metabolism Pathways: The Three Hydroxylation Routes**

Estrogens undergo Phase I oxidative metabolism via three competing cytochrome P450-mediated hydroxylation pathways, each producing metabolites with distinct biological activities:[3][14][15]

- **2-Hydroxylation pathway (CYP1A1, CYP1A2):** Produces **2-hydroxyestrone (2-OHE1)** and 2-hydroxyestradiol — considered **protective** metabolites with minimal estrogenic activity. These are further methylated by COMT to 2-methoxyestrogens, which have antiproliferative and antiangiogenic properties.[16][14][17]
- **4-Hydroxylation pathway (CYP1B1):** Produces **4-hydroxyestrone (4-OHE1)** and 4-hydroxyestradiol — considered the most **genotoxic** metabolites. 4-OH catechols form unstable depurinating DNA adducts via quinone intermediates, generating reactive oxygen species and causing mutations. This pathway is considered the primary genotoxic mechanism of estrogen carcinogenesis.[16][3][18]
- **16α-Hydroxylation pathway (CYP3A4, CYP3A7):** Produces **16α-hydroxyestrone (16α-OHE1)** — a potent estrogen that binds covalently to the estrogen receptor and nuclear proteins. Elevated levels are associated with increased breast cancer risk, particularly in premenopausal women.[19][20][17]

**Phase II detoxification** then determines the fate of these metabolites through three conjugation pathways:[3][15]

- **Methylation (COMT):** Converts catechol estrogens (2-OH and 4-OH) to methoxylated metabolites — a critical protective step that prevents quinone formation
- **Sulfation (SULT1A1, SULT1E1):** Inactivates estrogens and catechol estrogens by sulfate conjugation, facilitating excretion
- **Glucuronidation (UGT1A1, UGT2B7, UGT2B15):** Conjugates estrogens with glucuronic acid for biliary and urinary excretion

**Genetic Polymorphisms in Estrogen Metabolism**

Polymorphisms across the estrogen metabolism pathway create substantial interindividual variation in the balance between protective and genotoxic metabolites. The clinical significance is greatest when **multiple at-risk genotypes co-occur**, particularly in the context of prolonged estrogen exposure during perimenopause.

**Phase I Enzymes (Hydroxylation)**

**CYP1A1 (2-hydroxylation):** The rs2606345 polymorphism significantly influences estradiol concentrations and estrogen metabolite levels in premenopausal/perimenopausal women, with effects varying by race/ethnicity. In the SWAN study (N = 1,340), Japanese women with the CC genotype had lower E2, while African American women with CC had higher 16α-OHE1.[21] The C4887A polymorphism (A allele carriers) is associated with **accelerated estrogen catabolism** — higher total urinary estrogen metabolites, lower free estradiol index, and consequently lower femoral BMD in postmenopausal women.[22]

**CYP1B1 (4-hydroxylation):** The Leu432Val polymorphism (rs1056836, CYP1B1*3) alters the enzyme's catalytic properties for estradiol metabolism. The Val allele is associated with higher 4-hydroxylation activity, producing more genotoxic 4-OHE2. Leukocytes with the CYP1B1*3 variant show significantly increased 4-OHE2 production and DNA adduct formation upon estradiol exposure.[23] CYP1B1 polymorphisms have also been associated with menopausal symptom severity.[24]

**CYP19A1 (Aromatase):** Polymorphisms in the aromatase gene directly influence the **rate and trajectory of estradiol decline** during the menopausal transition. In the SWAN study (N = 681), the CYP19 rs749292 GG variant showed an E2 decline rate that was only **67% of the AG variant**, while the CYP19 rs936306 CT variant declined at **54% the rate of TT** — meaning certain genotypes maintain higher estrogen levels longer into the transition.[4] The CYP19 11r polymorphism was associated with more severe hot flashes and higher estrone levels during the transition.[25] Obesity further modifies these genetic effects: obese women with selected CYP19 variants had "remarkably different E2 trajectories" and different postmenopausal E2 levels.[4]

**Phase II Enzymes (Conjugation/Detoxification)**

**COMT (Val158Met):** This is arguably the most clinically significant single polymorphism in estrogen metabolism. The Met/Met (low-activity) genotype results in a **3–4-fold decrease** in COMT activity, reducing the protective methylation of catechol estrogens.[3][26] Effects are **menopausal status-dependent**: the low-activity COMT Met/Met genotype is associated with increased breast cancer risk in **premenopausal women** (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.4–4.3) but paradoxically with decreased risk in postmenopausal women (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.7).[27] This differential effect is modified by BMI — the association is strongest among the heaviest premenopausal women (OR 5.7) and the leanest postmenopausal women.[27] Folate status further modulates COMT function: low folate reduces S-adenosylmethionine (the methyl donor for COMT), amplifying the risk associated with the low-activity genotype.[28]

**SULT1A1 (Arg213His):** The His/His genotype has substantially lower sulfation activity, reducing estrogen inactivation. This polymorphism is associated with increased breast cancer risk (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.0–3.2), particularly among postmenopausal women with high BMI, alcohol consumption, or prolonged estrogen exposure.[29][30] The risk is amplified by gene-gene interactions: combined SULT1A1 His + GSTM1 null genotypes confer significantly elevated risk.[29][31]

**UGT1A1:** Reduced-activity polymorphisms (e.g., UGT1A1*28, the Gilbert's syndrome allele) may increase systemic estrogen concentrations through competitive inhibition between bilirubin and estrogen glucuronidation. A 2026 review highlighted that chronically elevated bilirubin could reduce estrogen conjugation, potentially increasing breast cancer risk in women with Gilbert's syndrome. The UGT1A1*[32]28 variant was associated with later age at breast cancer diagnosis (≥60 years).[33]

**Multigenic Risk:** The most compelling evidence supports a **cumulative, multigenic model** of risk. A trend of increasing breast cancer risk is observed with a greater number of high-risk genotypes across the pathway (high-activity CYP17 + high-inducibility CYP1A1 + low-activity COMT), with the COMT genotype being the most significant single determinant (4-fold increased risk).[34] This association is stronger in women with prolonged estrogen exposure, early menarche, and higher BMI.[35][34][36]

**Modifiable Factors That Influence Estrogen Metabolism**

Several lifestyle factors can shift the balance between protective and genotoxic estrogen metabolites, which is particularly relevant during perimenopause when estrogen levels are erratic and often elevated:

**Physical activity** is one of the most potent modulators. In the Nurses' Health Study II (N = 603 premenopausal women), high physical activity (≥42 MET-h/week) was associated with **15% lower urinary estradiol**, 15% lower 16-hydroxylation pathway metabolites, and a significantly higher 2-OHE1/16α-OHE1 ratio (P trend = 0.01).[37] Importantly, exercise can **counteract the adverse effect of adiposity** on estrogen metabolism — physically active women with higher BMI maintained favorable 2/16 ratios comparable to lean women.[38][39]

**Body weight/adiposity** strongly influences postmenopausal estrogen levels through adipose aromatase activity. Weight is the strongest correlate of estrogen metabolic clearance rates, and the aromatization of androgens to estrogens increases with age independently of weight.[40][12] Obese women have slower E2 decline during the menopausal transition and higher postmenopausal estrogen levels.[4]

**Cruciferous vegetables (I3C/DIM)** modulate estrogen metabolism by inducing CYP1A1 (increasing protective 2-hydroxylation) and suppressing CYP1B1 (decreasing genotoxic 4-hydroxylation).[41][42] A 2024 retrospective study (N = 19,294) found that DIM supplementation significantly increased the **2-OHE1/16α-OHE1 ratio** and decreased total urinary estrogens, including estrone, estradiol, estriol, and 16-hydroxyestrone (all P [43] An RCT of oral I3C (6–7 mg/kg/day) confirmed significantly increased urinary C-2 estrogens with decreased concentrations of estradiol, estrone, estriol, and 16α-OHE1.[42]

**Alcohol consumption** increases estrogen levels and may shift metabolism toward the 16α-hydroxylation pathway. The SULT1A1 His allele amplifies the breast cancer risk associated with alcohol consumption.[29]

**Smoking** paradoxically increases 2-hydroxylation of estradiol (potentially explaining lower uterine cancer risk in smokers) but introduces carcinogenic aromatic hydrocarbons that may offset any protective effect.[14][31]

**Dietary polyphenols, fiber, and caffeine** are associated with altered estrogen metabolite concentrations. In the SWAN study, anthocyanidins, hydroxybenzoic acid, wine, and caffeine were significantly associated with 2-OHE1 concentrations, while dietary fiber from fruits and vegetables was associated with 16α-OHE1 levels.[44]

---

**The 2/16α-OHE1 Ratio: Clinical Utility and Controversy**

The hypothesis that a higher 2-OHE1/16α-OHE1 ratio is protective against breast cancer has been influential but remains **debated**. The ORDET prospective study (N = 10,786) found that premenopausal women in the highest quintile of the 2/16 ratio had a reduced breast cancer risk (OR 0.58), but no association was found in postmenopausal women.[20] A 2020 critical review by Stanczyk concluded that evidence for the 2/16 ratio as a breast cancer risk biomarker is **insufficient**, citing assay limitations, lack of validation of the "pathway" approach, and the need for improved LC-MS/MS methodology.[45] The PLCO nested case-control study found that while more extensive 2-hydroxylation relative to parent estrogens was associated with lower risk, the most clinically significant finding was that **less extensive methylation of 4-hydroxylation pathway catechols** (reflecting COMT activity) was associated with higher risk — suggesting the 4-OH pathway and its detoxification may be more important than the 2/16 ratio alone.[46]

---

**Clinical Implications for Perimenopausal Women**

The perimenopause represents a uniquely vulnerable window: erratic and often elevated estrogen levels combined with progesterone deficiency create prolonged unopposed estrogen exposure, while the balance of estrogen metabolism is determined by an individual's genetic polymorphism profile across the entire pathway.[1][9][3] Women with multiple at-risk genotypes (high-activity CYP1B1 + low-activity COMT + low-activity SULT1A1 + GSTM1 null) who are also obese, sedentary, and consuming alcohol may be at substantially elevated risk for estrogen-driven pathology during this transition. Conversely, modifiable factors — particularly **regular physical activity, weight management, cruciferous vegetable intake, adequate folate status, and alcohol moderation** — can favorably shift estrogen metabolism toward protective pathways regardless of genotype.[47][37][38]
