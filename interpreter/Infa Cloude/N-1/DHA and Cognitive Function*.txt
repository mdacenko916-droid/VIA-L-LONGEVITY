# 8. DHA, Creatine, and Sleep Quality: Effects on Cognition in Men Over 40

**DHA supplementation shows the strongest observational evidence for cognitive protection**, with each 0.1 g/day increment of DHA intake associated with an **8–9.9% lower risk of cognitive decline** in a meta-analysis of 48 longitudinal studies (103,651 participants).[1] **Creatine supplementation improves memory primarily in older adults** (ages 66–76; SMD 0.88) but not younger individuals.[2] **Sleep quality — particularly slow-wave sleep preservation — is the most potent modifiable factor**, with each 1% annual decrease in SWS percentage associated with a **27% increased risk of incident dementia** over 17 years of follow-up.[3]

---

**DHA and Cognitive Function**

DHA constitutes approximately 40% of brain polyunsaturated fatty acids and is concentrated in neuronal membrane phospholipids, particularly in gray matter and synapses. It is metabolized into neuroprotectin D1 and other specialized pro-resolving mediators involved in neurogenesis, neurotransmission, and inflammation resolution.[4]

The Framingham Heart Study found that higher red blood cell DHA and omega-3 index levels were associated with **larger hippocampal volumes and better abstract reasoning** as early as midlife, with threshold effects suggesting that even moderate omega-3 intake may be sufficient to preserve brain structure.[4] In the ADNI cohort, long-term omega-3 supplement users had a **64% reduced risk of Alzheimer's disease** (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.18–0.72).[1]

However, RCT evidence is more nuanced. A meta-analysis found that DHA/EPA supplementation significantly improved **episodic memory in adults with mild memory complaints** (P 1 g/day** improved episodic memory regardless of baseline cognitive status (P [5] In cognitively healthy individuals with coronary artery disease, 3.36 g/day EPA+DHA slowed cognitive aging by **2.5 years**.[6] Conversely, the AREDS2 trial (N = 3,501, mean age 72.7) found no benefit of 1 g/day DHA+EPA on cognitive function over 5 years in cognitively healthy older adults.[7] An 18-week RCT in mid-life adults (ages 30–54) with 1.4 g/day EPA+DHA also showed no overall cognitive benefit, though exploratory analysis suggested improvement in executive function among those with **low baseline DHA levels**.[8]

Key factors that may explain discrepant RCT results include dose (≥1 g/day appears necessary), baseline omega-3 status (benefit greatest in those with low levels), APOE ε4 genotype (carriers may benefit more from early supplementation), and stage of cognitive decline (benefit in MCI but not established AD).[6][9]

**Practical DHA dosing:** Based on available evidence, **≥900 mg DHA/day** (or ≥1 g/day combined DHA+EPA) appears to be the minimum effective dose for cognitive benefit, with the strongest evidence in individuals with mild memory complaints, low baseline omega-3 status, or APOE ε4 carriers.[5][6][9][10]

---

**Creatine and Cognitive Function**

The brain accounts for ~20% of total body energy expenditure despite representing only 2% of body mass, making it highly dependent on ATP recycling — the primary function of the phosphocreatine/creatine kinase system.[11]

A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found that creatine supplementation improved memory overall (SMD 0.29, P = 0.02), with a **dramatically larger effect in older adults aged 66–76** (SMD 0.88, P = 0.009) compared to younger adults aged 11–31 (SMD 0.03, P = 0.72).[2] A 2025 systematic review of studies specifically in adults ≥55 years found that 5 of 6 studies (83.3%) reported a positive relationship between creatine and cognition, particularly in **memory and attention** domains.[12]

An earlier systematic review found evidence that creatine may improve **short-term memory and intelligence/reasoning**, with vegetarians responding better than omnivores on memory tasks — consistent with lower baseline brain creatine stores in those with limited dietary creatine intake.[13] Creatine also shows particular benefit during **metabolic stress states** such as sleep deprivation, where it may buffer the cognitive effects of acute sleep loss.[11]

However, the **EFSA Panel (2024)** concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship has **not been established** between creatine supplementation and cognitive improvement, noting that the acute working memory effect observed at 20 g/day for 5–7 days was not replicated at lower doses or with continuous consumption.[14] The largest RCT to date (N = 123, crossover design, 5 g/day for 6 weeks) found only a borderline effect on backward digit span (P = 0.064) and no effect on Raven's Progressive Matrices.[15]

**Practical creatine dosing:** The most commonly studied regimen is **3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate**. The cognitive benefit appears most relevant for older adults, vegetarians/low-meat consumers, and during periods of sleep deprivation or cognitive stress.[2][11][13]

---

**Sleep Quality and Cognitive Function**

Sleep is arguably the **most impactful modifiable factor** for cognitive health in aging men. The relationship operates through multiple mechanisms: glymphatic clearance of amyloid-β and tau proteins during slow-wave sleep, memory consolidation, synaptic homeostasis, and hormonal regulation.[3][16]

**Slow-wave sleep and dementia risk:** The Framingham Heart Study prospective cohort (N = 346, mean age 69, 17-year follow-up) demonstrated that SWS percentage declines with aging (mean −0.6% per year), and each 1% annual decrease was associated with a **27% increase in dementia risk** (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06–1.54), independent of APOE ε4 status. APOE ε4 carriers experienced accelerated SWS loss.[3]

**Optimal sleep duration:** The A4 Study (N = 4,417 cognitively normal adults, ages 65–85) found a **U-shaped relationship** between sleep duration and cognitive outcomes. Short sleep (≤6 hours) was associated with higher brain amyloid-β burden and reduced memory performance, while long sleep (≥9 hours) was associated with worse executive function and processing speed. The optimal range was **7–8 hours**.[17] The following figure illustrates these domain-specific cognitive associations:

# 9. Sleep Apnea: Impact on Weight and Testosterone in Men Over 40

**OSA and obesity form a bidirectional vicious cycle**, with obesity being the strongest risk factor for OSA (OR >4 for BMI ≥30) and OSA independently promoting weight gain through hormonal dysregulation, reduced physical activity, and metabolic disruption.[24][25] OSA is significantly associated with **lower serum testosterone** in men (SMD −0.97, 95% CI −1.47 to −0.47), an effect that is **independent of age and BMI**.[26][21]

---

**Prevalence and the OSA-Obesity Nexus**

OSA prevalence in men increases sharply with age: an estimated **10% of men aged 30–49** and **17% of men aged 50–70** have OSA, with approximately 24 million undiagnosed cases in the US.[27] Among individuals with obesity, prevalence exceeds **40%** (BMI >30) and reaches **50–98%** in morbid obesity.[25][28] A 2025 individual participant data meta-analysis of 12,860 adults found that while obesity dramatically increases OSA risk (OR >4), notably **68.5% of individuals with OSA do not have obesity**, highlighting that non-obese men over 40 remain at significant risk.[24]

The bidirectional relationship operates through several mechanisms:[25][29]

- **Obesity → OSA:** Increased pharyngeal fat deposition narrows the airway; abdominal adiposity reduces lung volumes and tracheal traction; adipokine dysregulation impairs ventilatory control
- **OSA → Obesity:** Sleep fragmentation increases ghrelin and leptin resistance, promoting excess caloric intake; daytime somnolence reduces physical activity and energy expenditure; intermittent hypoxia drives insulin resistance and dyslipidemia

A 10% weight gain is associated with a **32% increase in AHI**, while a 10% weight loss produces a **26% decrease in AHI**.[30][31] Metabolic syndrome prevalence among adults with polysomnography-confirmed OSA is **55.4%** (95% CI 51.0–59.8%), and Mendelian randomization confirms a **bidirectional causal relationship** between OSA and metabolic syndrome.[32][33]

---

**OSA and Testosterone**

A meta-analysis of 24 case-control studies (1,389 patients) found that male OSA patients had significantly lower testosterone than controls (SMD −0.97), with no such effect in women.[26] A cross-sectional study in reproductive-aged men (20–40 years) showed a **stepwise decline in testosterone** with increasing OSA severity: mean testosterone was 13.1 nmol/L in OSA patients versus 21.8 nmol/L in controls (P [34]

The mechanisms linking OSA to hypogonadism include:[35][21]

- **Intermittent hypoxia** directly suppresses Leydig cell testosterone production, independent of obesity
- **Sleep fragmentation** disrupts the nocturnal testosterone surge (testosterone is primarily secreted during sleep, with peak levels during the first REM period)
- **HPA axis activation** — elevated cortisol from repeated arousals suppresses GnRH pulsatility
- **Systemic inflammation** — CRP and pro-inflammatory cytokines independently correlate with lower testosterone

A 2026 study of 204 men with severe obesity found that "decompensated OSAS" (AHI >30) was independently associated with lower total testosterone (P = 0.014), even after adjusting for BMI, diabetes, and CRP.[36]

---

**Effect of CPAP on Testosterone**

Evidence on CPAP's effect on testosterone is **conflicting**. Two meta-analyses (2014 and 2019) found **no significant change** in total testosterone, free testosterone, or SHBG after CPAP treatment.[37][38] However, these analyses were limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, and variable CPAP adherence.

More recent and better-quality studies suggest otherwise. The 2026 longitudinal study found that 3 months of CPAP therapy significantly **increased total testosterone** (P = 0.009), with improvement independently associated with reduction in oxygen desaturation index, not BMI changes.[36] An RCT in 32 men with severe OSA and erectile dysfunction showed significant increases in testosterone, FSH, and LH after 1 month of CPAP.[39] A comprehensive review concluded that "the conclusion that CPAP has no effect on testosterone is premature because available studies are underpowered and better-quality studies suggest otherwise."[21]

---

**Emerging Pharmacotherapy: GLP-1 Receptor Agonists**

The evidence supports a **synergistic approach** for men over 40: adequate DHA intake (≥900 mg/day, preferably from fatty fish 2–3 times/week plus supplementation if needed), creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day, particularly for those with low dietary meat intake), and prioritization of sleep quality (7–8 hours, with screening and treatment of OSA). Untreated OSA represents a particularly high-yield intervention target, as it simultaneously impairs cognition, suppresses testosterone, promotes weight gain, and accelerates metabolic syndrome — creating a self-reinforcing cycle that can be interrupted through CPAP, weight management, and emerging pharmacotherapy.
