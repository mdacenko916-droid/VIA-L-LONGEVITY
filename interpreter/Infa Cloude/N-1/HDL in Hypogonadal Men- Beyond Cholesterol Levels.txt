In men with andropause/hypogonadism, the cardiovascular risk associated with low HDL is driven less by **HDL-cholesterol concentration** and more by **impaired HDL function** — particularly reduced cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC), which correlates directly with testosterone levels.[1][2][3] The **Mediterranean diet** is the only dietary pattern demonstrated in a large RCT (PREDIMED) to both improve HDL function and reduce major cardiovascular events by **30%**.[4][5] **Combined aerobic and resistance training** provides the most comprehensive CVD risk reduction, with aerobic exercise being the primary driver of HDL-C increases (+2–5 mg/dL) while resistance training contributes independently through improvements in body composition, insulin sensitivity, and mortality reduction.[6][7]

---

**HDL in Hypogonadal Men: Beyond Cholesterol Levels**

The traditional view of HDL-C as a protective biomarker has undergone a paradigm shift. Genetic studies and failed HDL-raising drug trials (niacin, CETP inhibitors) have demonstrated that **HDL-C concentration is not causally related to atherosclerosis** — rather, HDL function, particularly cholesterol efflux capacity, is the critical determinant.[2][3][8] A landmark study in the NEJM showed that CEC in the highest quartile was associated with a **67% reduction in cardiovascular risk** even after adjusting for HDL-C levels, while HDL-C itself lost significance after multivariable adjustment.[9]

This distinction is especially relevant in hypogonadal men. A study comparing hypogonadal patients with controls found that despite similar HDL-C concentrations, hypogonadal men had **16.2% lower total cholesterol efflux capacity** and **43% higher serum cholesterol loading capacity** (a proatherogenic measure). Testosterone levels positively correlated with total HDL CEC (r² = 0.359, P = 0.0001) and ABCG1-mediated CEC (r² = 0.367, P = 0.0001), but **HDL-C concentrations did not correlate with either testosterone or HDL function**.[1] This means standard lipid panels may underestimate cardiovascular risk in hypogonadal men.

The Endocrine Society guideline on lipid management in hypogonadism states that men with low testosterone typically show **increased LDL-C and triglycerides with low HDL-C**, alongside insulin resistance and features of metabolic syndrome. However, the guideline recommends testosterone therapy only for symptomatic indications, **not as an approach to improve dyslipidemia or CVD risk** (Recommendation 11.1, 2⊕⊕OO).[10]

---

**Effect of Testosterone Replacement on HDL and CVD Risk**

The effect of TRT on HDL is paradoxical and clinically important:

- **The Testosterone Trials** (788 men ≥65 years): 12 months of testosterone gel significantly **decreased HDL-C by 2.0 mg/dL** (P [11]
- A large retrospective cohort (N = 4,307 men, median age 41) found HDL decreased by **8.73%** over 12 months of testosterone treatment, while triglycerides decreased by 20% and HbA1c by 4.5%.[12]
- The **TestES meta-analysis** and **Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology 2024 review** confirmed slightly lower HDL-C with testosterone treatment, concluding that testosterone is "unlikely to produce a clinically significant change in lipid parameters."[13]
- A systematic review found that studies where testosterone therapy **lowered HDL-C** experienced significantly increased cardiovascular event rates, while studies where HDL remained stable or increased showed no excess events — with intramuscular testosterone having the least detrimental effect on HDL compared to oral and transdermal preparations.[14]

The **TRAVERSE trial** (N = 5,246) provided reassurance that short-to-medium-term testosterone does not increase major adverse cardiovascular events, though higher rates of pulmonary embolism (0.9% vs 0.5%), atrial fibrillation (3.5% vs 2.4%), and acute kidney injury (2.3% vs 1.5%) were observed in the testosterone arm.[13]

**Mediterranean Diet: CVD Prevention and HDL Function**

The Mediterranean diet has the strongest evidence base of any dietary pattern for cardiovascular prevention, with particular relevance to HDL function rather than HDL quantity.

**PREDIMED Trial — Hard Cardiovascular Outcomes**

The PREDIMED trial (N = 7,447 high-risk adults, 4.8-year follow-up) demonstrated that a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil (EVOO) or nuts reduced the composite endpoint of MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death by **30%** (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.55–0.89). Stroke risk was reduced by **42%** (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.42–0.82).[5][15][16]

**HDL Function Improvements — PREDIMED Substudy**

A randomized substudy of PREDIMED (N = 296) demonstrated that the Mediterranean diet, especially enriched with virgin olive oil, significantly improved multiple HDL functional traits after 1 year:[4]

- **Increased cholesterol efflux capacity** (P = 0.018 for MedDiet-EVOO; P = 0.013 for MedDiet-Nuts)
- **Increased paraoxonase-1 arylesterase activity** (antioxidant function; P = 0.012 vs control)
- **Increased HDL vasodilatory capacity** (nitric oxide release; P = 0.026 vs control)
- **Decreased CETP activity** (P = 0.028), reducing atherogenic cholesterol transfer
- Improved HDL oxidative status and composition

These functional improvements occurred **without significant changes in HDL-C levels**, reinforcing that the Mediterranean diet's cardiovascular benefit operates through HDL quality rather than quantity.[4][17]

The CARDIVEG crossover trial confirmed that the Mediterranean diet maintained significantly higher ABCA1-mediated CEC compared to a vegetarian diet (18.6% higher, P [18]

Specific Mediterranean diet components driving HDL function improvements include virgin olive oil (+0.7% CEC per 10 g/day increase), whole grains (+0.6% CEC per 25 g/day), nuts (increased paraoxonase-1 activity), and legumes (decreased CETP activity).[17]

The **PREDIMED-Plus** substudy further showed that combining an energy-restricted Mediterranean diet with physical activity improved HDL triglyceride metabolism beyond a non-restrictive Mediterranean diet alone, with weight loss mediating 72–77% of the improvement.[19]

**Resistance Training, Exercise, and CVD Risk**

**HDL-C Effects by Exercise Type**

A 2025 meta-analysis of 42 RCTs in middle-aged and older individuals found that exercise overall significantly increased HDL-C (SMD 0.22, P = 0.001), but subgroup analysis revealed that **aerobic exercise drove the HDL-C increase**, while resistance and stretching exercises had **no significant effect on HDL-C levels**.[7]

The AHA 2024 Scientific Statement on resistance training reports modest HDL-C improvements of **+2 to +12 mg/dL** with RT, with the effect being less pronounced in adults [6] A meta-analysis among men specifically found aerobic training increased HDL-C by approximately 2–5 mg/dL and reduced triglycerides by 12 mg/dL.[20]

**Combined Training: Superior CVD Risk Reduction**

Despite resistance training's limited effect on HDL-C, **combined aerobic + resistance training** provides the most comprehensive cardiovascular benefit:[6][21]

- Large observational studies show combined training is associated with **40–46% lower all-cause and CVD mortality**, compared to 18–29% for either modality alone.[6]
- The **CardioRACE Trial** (N = 406, ages 35–70, overweight/obese) found that aerobic and combined exercise improved composite CVD risk profiles (blood pressure, LDL-C, fasting glucose, body fat) compared to controls, while **resistance training alone did not** significantly improve the composite score.[21]
- An 8-week RCT in adults aged 58 ± 7 years showed combined training was the only modality to significantly reduce both peripheral and central diastolic blood pressure (−4 mmHg each), while also increasing cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular strength.[22]

The JACC Focus Seminar emphasizes that while exercise-induced lipid changes are modest compared to statins, exercise capacity and cardiorespiratory fitness **independently reduce mortality** in patients with hypercholesterolemia — the survival benefit is primarily attributed to improved fitness rather than lipid changes alone.[23]

**Resistance Training — Independent Benefits Beyond Lipids**

The AHA Scientific Statement highlights that RT in older adults with CVD improves glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, body composition, and functional capacity. A dose-response relationship exists: higher training volumes (2–3 sets) and intensity (55–80% 1-RM) produce greater reductions in body fat and waist circumference.[6]

The optimal strategy for cardiovascular prevention in men with andropause combines a **Mediterranean dietary pattern** (particularly rich in EVOO, nuts, legumes, whole grains, and fish) with **combined aerobic and resistance training** (≥150 min/week aerobic + 2–3 sessions/week RT). This approach addresses both HDL quantity and quality while providing the broadest CVD risk reduction. Testosterone replacement, while potentially improving HDL function through its correlation with CEC, paradoxically lowers HDL-C and should be prescribed for symptomatic hypogonadism rather than as a cardiovascular intervention.[10][13][4][6]
