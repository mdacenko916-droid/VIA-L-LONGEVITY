## When to Test for Progesterone Deficiency

Serum progesterone should be measured in the **mid-luteal phase, approximately 7 days before the expected onset of the next menses** (not on a fixed "day 21"), because progesterone peaks **6–8 days after ovulation** and the timing of ovulation varies between women and cycles.[1][2][3]

---

**Optimal Timing**

The common instruction to "test on day 21" assumes a textbook 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. In reality, cycle length varies from 24 to 35 days in regularly menstruating women, so the correct approach is to count **backward from the anticipated next period**:[1][3][4]

- For a **28-day cycle:** test around day 21 (= day 28 minus 7)
- For a **35-day cycle:** test around day 28
- For a **25-day cycle:** test around day 18

If ovulation timing is known (via LH surge detection with home urine kits or ultrasound), the ideal window is **5–7 days post-ovulation**, when the corpus luteum is at peak function.[2][5][4]

**Interpreting Progesterone Values**

A critical caveat: progesterone is secreted in **pulses** in response to LH, with values oscillating between **5 and 40 ng/mL over short periods** in normally ovulatory women, making a single random measurement difficult to interpret.[2]

The ASRM (2021) emphasizes that **no single progesterone value can reliably assess luteal phase "quality"** — it can only confirm or exclude ovulation. The best diagnostic approach combines ovulation detection (LH surge or ultrasound) with a well-timed mid-luteal progesterone measurement.[1][2]

---

**Clinical Context: Progesterone in Perimenopause**

In the perimenopausal transition, progesterone deficiency is nearly universal and progressive. The SWAN Daily Hormone Study found that ovulatory cycles declined from **80.9% at baseline to 64.7%** by the third annual assessment, with pregnanediol glucuronide (a urinary progesterone metabolite) decreasing **6.6% per year** as women progressed through the transition.[9] This creates a state of relative **unopposed estrogen** — elevated or erratic estradiol with diminished progesterone — which is associated with menorrhagia, vasomotor symptoms, and mood disturbance.[10][11][12]

Low progesterone concentrations during perimenopause have been associated with **worse mood** and increased vasomotor symptoms, with fluctuating LH patterns and low progesterone production being the hormone patterns most strongly associated with hot flashes.[13][11]

## What Is "Pregnenolone Steal"?

**"Pregnenolone steal" is a popular but scientifically unsubstantiated concept** from alternative/functional medicine. It is not recognized in mainstream endocrinology literature and has no supporting evidence from peer-reviewed clinical studies.

**The theory proposes** that under chronic stress, the body preferentially shunts pregnenolone (the common precursor of all steroid hormones) toward cortisol production at the expense of sex hormones (progesterone, DHEA, testosterone, estrogen), thereby "stealing" substrate from the sex hormone pathways and causing deficiencies.

**Why this model is physiologically incorrect:**

1. **Compartmentalized steroidogenesis:** Steroid hormone synthesis is not a single shared pool. The adrenal cortex and the gonads are separate organs with independently regulated enzyme systems. The adrenal zona fasciculata produces cortisol under ACTH control, while the ovaries produce progesterone under LH control. Pregnenolone produced in the adrenal gland does not compete with pregnenolone produced in the ovary — each tissue synthesizes its own pregnenolone from cholesterol via CYP11A1 (cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme).[14][15][16][17]
2. **Rate-limiting step is cholesterol transport, not pregnenolone availability:** The rate-limiting step in steroidogenesis is the transport of cholesterol to the inner mitochondrial membrane by StAR (steroidogenic acute regulatory protein), not the availability of pregnenolone itself. ACTH stimulates StAR expression in the adrenal; LH stimulates StAR in the ovary — these are independent regulatory pathways.[15][16][17]
3. **ACTH stimulates pregnenolone sulfate production acutely** (32-fold increase in adrenal vein), confirming that the adrenal can massively upregulate pregnenolone synthesis without depleting gonadal pools.[18]
4. **"Adrenal fatigue" is not a recognized diagnosis.** The Endocrine Society has explicitly stated that "adrenal fatigue" does not exist as a medical condition. While chronic stress can alter HPA axis dynamics — potentially leading to hypocortisolism through enhanced negative feedback or HPA axis downregulation — this occurs through changes in CRH/ACTH signaling, not through substrate depletion.[19][20]

**What pregnenolone actually is:** Pregnenolone is the **first steroid synthesized from cholesterol** and serves as the universal precursor for all steroid hormones — progesterone, cortisol, aldosterone, DHEA, testosterone, and estradiol. It is produced in the adrenal glands, gonads, and brain (where it functions as a neurosteroid).[14][15][21] Beyond its precursor role, pregnenolone has independent biological activity: it modulates the CB1 cannabinoid receptor as a negative allosteric modulator, enhances microtubule stability, promotes neuroplasticity, and has anti-inflammatory properties through degradation of TLR signaling proteins.[22][23][24]

**What is actually happening when stress lowers sex hormones:** Chronic stress suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis through elevated cortisol and CRH, which directly inhibit GnRH pulsatility — this is a well-documented neuroendocrine mechanism that does not involve "stealing" pregnenolone.[20][26] In women, this manifests as functional hypothalamic amenorrhea or luteal phase deficiency through suppressed LH pulsatility, not substrate diversion.[2]

**Practical Summary for Testing**

For a woman concerned about progesterone deficiency:

- **Track cycle length** for 2–3 months to establish the expected period date
- **Test serum progesterone 7 days before expected menses** (or 5–7 days post-ovulation if using LH kits)
- A value **>3 ng/mL confirms ovulation**; a value **[1][3][8]
- In perimenopause, a single value is less informative due to erratic cycles — serial measurements or urinary metabolite testing (pregnanediol glucuronide) across the cycle may provide a more complete picture[2][9]
- If progesterone is consistently low, evaluate for underlying causes: thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, PCOS, excessive exercise, stress, or diminished ovarian reserve[2]
