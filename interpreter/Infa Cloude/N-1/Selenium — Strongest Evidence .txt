## Hashimoto's Thyroiditis — What Really Works: Evidence Hierarchy for Supplements and Dietary Interventions

Among the interventions commonly discussed for Hashimoto's thyroiditis (HT), **selenium has the strongest evidence** — multiple meta-analyses consistently demonstrate significant reductions in TPOAb titers, with moderate-certainty evidence (GRADE).[1][2][3] Myo-inositol + selenium shows promising but preliminary data for TSH reduction. Vitamin D reduces antibodies primarily in deficient patients. A gluten-free diet without celiac disease has **insufficient evidence** for routine recommendation.[4][5][6]

---

**1. Selenium — Strongest Evidence (Moderate Certainty)**

**The effect on anti-TPO antibodies is real**, though its clinical significance remains debated. The largest and most rigorous meta-analysis (Huwiler et al. 2024; 35 RCTs, 2,358 participants, GRADE-assessed) found:[2]

- **TPOAb reduction:** SMD **−0.96** (95% CI −1.36 to −0.56; 29 cohorts; I² = 90%) — significant regardless of whether patients were on levothyroxine or not
- **TSH reduction** (in patients NOT on levothyroxine): SMD **−0.21** (95% CI −0.43 to −0.02; 7 cohorts, 869 participants; I² = 0%)
- **Malondialdehyde (oxidative stress marker):** SMD −1.16 (significant reduction)
- **No significant effect** on fT4, fT3, TgAb, thyroid volume, or interleukins
- **Safety:** Adverse events comparable to placebo (OR 0.89; I² = 0%)
- **Overall GRADE certainty: Moderate**

A 2025 meta-analysis (21 RCTs, 1,610 patients) confirmed time-dependent effects: TPOAb reduction was significant at both **3 months** (SMD −0.46) and **6 months** (SMD −0.80), with the effect strengthening over time. TgAb decreased at 3 months but not at 6 months. TSH decreased significantly only after 6 months.[1]

**Key caveats:**

- High heterogeneity (I² = 90%) across TPOAb studies reflects differences in baseline selenium status, selenium form, dose, and population characteristics[2][7]
- The clinical relevance of antibody reduction alone is uncertain — no meta-analysis has demonstrated that selenium supplementation **prevents progression to overt hypothyroidism, reduces levothyroxine dose requirements, or improves clinical outcomes** beyond antibody titers[7][8]
- The AACE/ATA 2012 guideline concluded there are "simply not enough outcome data to suggest a role at the present time for routine selenium use"[8]
- A 2020 Nature Reviews Endocrinology review noted that selenium trials "have generally resulted in reduced thyroid autoantibody titre **without apparent improvements in the clinical course** of the disease"[7]

**2. Myo-Inositol + Selenium — Promising but Preliminary**

Myo-inositol (MI) is a precursor of phosphoinositides involved in the **TSH signaling pathway** — it is required for intracellular TSH signal transduction via the phospholipase C/IP3/DAG pathway and for H₂O₂ generation needed for thyroid hormone synthesis.[13][14]

**MI + Se vs. Se alone (2026 meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis; 3 RCTs, 288 patients):**[15]

- **TSH reduction:** SMD **−1.26** (95% CI −1.51 to −1.00; I² = 0%) — significantly greater with combination vs. selenium alone; TSA suggested this finding may be robust
- **TgAb reduction:** SMD −0.51 (95% CI −0.78 to −0.24; I² = 0%) — significant, but TSA indicated potential type I error risk
- **No significant difference** for TPOAb, fT3, or fT4

An observational study (Pace et al. 2020) found that TSH decreased by **31% with Se alone** and **38% with Se + MI**, with the combination achieving TSH reduction **earlier** (by 6 months vs. 12 months).[16]

**However**, the network meta-analysis by Peng et al. (2024) found that the MI + Se combination did **not** significantly reduce TPOAb or TgAb compared to placebo (wide confidence intervals crossing zero), and only selenium alone achieved significant antibody reductions.[4]

**Dosing:** MI 600 mg/day + selenomethionine 83 μg/day (the most studied combination)[16]

**Bottom line:** The combination appears to offer an **additional TSH-lowering benefit** beyond selenium alone in subclinical hypothyroidism, but evidence is limited to 3 small studies. It should not replace levothyroxine when indicated, and larger RCTs are needed.[15]

---

**3. Vitamin D — Conditional Benefit (Only in Deficient Patients)**

Vitamin D deficiency is **more prevalent in HT patients** than controls, and an inverse relationship between 25(OH)D levels and TPOAb titers has been consistently observed.[17][12] However, the direction of causality remains debated — low vitamin D may be a consequence rather than a cause of autoimmune disease.[12]

**Meta-analytic evidence:**

- Tang et al. (2023; 12 RCTs, 862 patients): Vitamin D significantly reduced **TPOAb** (SMD −1.08), **TgAb** (SMD −1.00), and **TSH** (SMD −0.17), while increasing fT3 and fT4. Active vitamin D (calcitriol) was more effective than native D3; treatment >12 weeks was more effective than shorter courses.[18]
- Jiang et al. (2022; 6 RCTs, 258 patients): Vitamin D significantly reduced **TPOAb** (WMD −158 IU/mL) but had **no significant effect** on TSH, fT3, or fT4.[19]
- Luo et al. (2025; 10 cohorts, 577 patients): TPOAb reduction was significant **only in patients with baseline vitamin D insufficiency/deficiency** ([20]
- The network meta-analysis (Peng 2024) found vitamin D alone did **not** significantly reduce TPOAb or TgAb compared to placebo.[4]

**4. Gluten-Free Diet Without Celiac Disease — Insufficient Evidence**

This is the most controversial intervention. The first meta-analysis on this topic (Piticchio et al. 2023; 4 studies, only 87 patients) found:[5]

- **TPOAb:** Trend toward reduction (ES −0.40; P = 0.07) — **not statistically significant**
- **TgAb:** Trend toward reduction (ES −0.39; P = 0.06) — **not statistically significant**
- **TSH:** Significant reduction (ES −0.35; P = 0.02)
- **fT4:** Significant increase (ES +0.35; P = 0.02)
- Subgroup analysis showed that antibody reductions were significant **only in patients with gluten-related conditions** (positive anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies or non-celiac gluten sensitivity), not in those without

A 2026 comprehensive review concluded that "evidence remains **insufficient to recommend routine gluten withdrawal** for all patients with non-celiac HT" and that "long-term GFD also carries practical and nutritional burdens that require professional supervision."[6]

A Mendelian randomization study (2025) found **no genetic causal relationship** between a gluten-free diet and protection against autoimmune thyroid disease, suggesting any observed benefits may operate through non-genetic mechanisms.[22]

The JCEM review (Larsen et al. 2022) stated that "avoidance of gluten and dairy has positive impacts **only in patients with concomitant sensitivities** to those substances, likely by improving absorption of levothyroxine."[23]

**Practical recommendation:** Screen HT patients for celiac disease (tissue transglutaminase IgA + total IgA) given the 2–5× increased prevalence of celiac disease in HT. A GFD is indicated only if celiac disease or documented gluten sensitivity is confirmed.[6][23]

---

**5. Other Supplements and Eliminations**

**Zinc:** Limited evidence. One RCT of zinc + vitamin A + magnesium co-supplementation in hypothyroid patients showed increased fT4 and decreased hs-CRP, but no specific RCTs exist for zinc monotherapy in HT.[24] Zinc is a cofactor for thyroid hormone synthesis and immune function, and deficiency should be corrected, but routine supplementation in zinc-replete HT patients is not evidence-based.[25][23]

**Iron:** Iron deficiency impairs thyroid peroxidase function (a heme-containing enzyme) and is common in HT due to frequent comorbid autoimmune gastritis. Correction of iron deficiency improves thyroid hormone concentrations, and combined iron + levothyroxine is more effective than either alone.[12]

**Iodine:** Excess iodine intake can **worsen** autoimmune thyroiditis — highly iodinated thyroglobulin is more immunogenic. Supplemental iodine should be **avoided** in HT patients in iodine-sufficient regions.[12][23]
