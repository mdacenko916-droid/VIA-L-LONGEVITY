Several **pharmacological and nonpharmacological alternatives to testosterone replacement therapy (TRT)** are available, particularly for men with **secondary (functional) hypogonadism** and an intact hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis. These alternatives work by stimulating endogenous testosterone production rather than replacing it exogenously, and are especially relevant for men desiring fertility preservation or those intolerant to TRT side effects. Importantly, the AUA guideline notes that most of these agents are used **off-label**, with only hCG having prior FDA approval for use in males.[1]

---

**Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators (SERMs)**

SERMs are the most studied alternative to TRT for secondary hypogonadism. They block estrogen-mediated negative feedback at the hypothalamus, thereby increasing GnRH, LH, and FSH secretion and stimulating endogenous testosterone production while **preserving spermatogenesis**.[2][3]

- **Clomiphene citrate (CC)** — the most widely used SERM for male hypogonadism — is typically dosed at **25–50 mg daily or every other day**. In a randomized study of 282 hypogonadal men, CC raised testosterone by approximately **223%** from baseline over 3 months, comparable to hCG alone or combination therapy. Long-term data (up to 7 years) in 400 patients showed that **88% achieved eugonadism**, 77% reported symptom improvement, and only 8% reported side effects (mood changes, blurred vision, breast tenderness). CC carries a **lower risk of polycythemia** compared with TRT. However, evidence for improvement in erectile dysfunction specifically has been inconsistent — one placebo-controlled RCT in older men showed robust testosterone increases but no improvement in sexual function.[4][5][3][6]
- **Enclomiphene citrate** — the trans-isomer of clomiphene and a purer estrogen antagonist — has been evaluated in phase II/III trials. In two parallel phase III RCTs of 256 obese men with secondary hypogonadism, enclomiphene increased testosterone into the normal range while **maintaining sperm counts**, whereas testosterone gel suppressed spermatogenesis. Enclomiphene also raised LH and FSH, confirming restoration of HPT axis function rather than replacement. Long-term effects on bone health, sexual function, and adiposity still require further study.[7][8][9][6]

---

**Aromatase Inhibitors (AIs)**

AIs such as **anastrozole (1 mg/day)** and **letrozole (2.5 mg/day)** reduce the conversion of testosterone to estradiol, thereby decreasing estrogen-mediated negative feedback and increasing LH/FSH secretion.[3] They are particularly considered in obese men with elevated estradiol levels driving functional hypogonadism.[10] However, AIs carry a risk of **reduced bone mineral density** due to estrogen suppression, and data on improvement in erectile dysfunction have been inconclusive.[3] The AUA guideline includes AIs among agents that may be used to maintain fertility in hypogonadal men, though evidence is graded as Level C.[1]

---

**Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG)**

hCG acts as an LH analog, directly stimulating Leydig cells to produce testosterone. It is the **only FDA-approved alternative** (for males) among these agents.[1] Typical dosing is **1,000–3,000 IU two to three times weekly**, titrated to achieve eugonadal testosterone levels.[11] In the randomized trial by Habous et al., hCG (5,000 IU twice weekly) was equally effective as CC in raising testosterone, with the combination of CC + hCG producing the greatest symptom improvement. hCG maintains intratesticular testosterone and spermatogenesis, making it the treatment of choice for men with[4] **hypogonadotropic hypogonadism desiring fertility**.[11][12]

---

**Selective Androgen Receptor Modulators (SARMs)**

SARMs are nonsteroidal compounds designed to provide **tissue-selective anabolic effects** (muscle, bone) while minimizing androgenic effects on the prostate, skin, and hematopoiesis.[3][13] Enobosarm (GTx-024) demonstrated dose-dependent increases in lean body mass in a phase II trial of elderly men and postmenopausal women.[3] However, SARMs remain **investigational** — no SARM has received regulatory approval for hypogonadism. Key concerns include HDL suppression, transient hepatotoxicity, and a lack of consistent improvements in functional outcomes such as strength or mobility.[13][3] A recent perspective concludes that SARMs may eventually enter clinical practice for **niche indications** (frailty, sarcopenia, cachexia) but widespread approval for hypogonadism requires further trials demonstrating both efficacy and long-term safety.[13]

---

**Emerging Therapies: Kisspeptin and Neurokinin B Pathway**

**Kisspeptin** and **neurokinin B (NKB)** are hypothalamic neuropeptides that regulate GnRH pulsatility. Kisspeptin administration stimulates LH secretion in healthy and hypogonadal men and can restore pulsatile GnRH/LH secretion in patients with functional hypogonadism.[14][15] However, clinical development for male hypogonadism is still in early translational phases. NKB pathway modulation has advanced further in female reproductive disorders — the NK3R antagonist fezolinetant is FDA-approved for menopausal vasomotor symptoms — but its application to male hypogonadism remains exploratory.[14][16]

---

**Nonpharmacological Approaches**

For men with functional hypogonadism, **lifestyle modification remains the first-line intervention** before any pharmacotherapy.[17] Weight loss (dietary or surgical), exercise, sleep optimization, and treatment of comorbidities can modestly increase testosterone and may render pharmacotherapy unnecessary.[18][17] Among nutraceuticals, only **zinc, vitamin D (in deficiency states), ashwagandha, and mucuna** have shown promising results in controlled trials, though effects are modest and do not reliably translate into clinical benefits.[18]
