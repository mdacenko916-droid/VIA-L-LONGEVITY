## Autoimmune Disease Onset in Perimenopause, Antibody Testing, and the Role of Selenium, Vitamin D, and Gluten in Autoimmune Thyroiditis

The **perimenopausal transition represents a critical window of increased autoimmune disease susceptibility** in women, driven by the abrupt decline in estrogen and progesterone, which synergizes with age-related immunosenescence to shift the immune balance toward a proinflammatory state.[1][2][3][4] **Hashimoto's thyroiditis (HT)** is the most common autoimmune disease encountered in this population, and among adjunctive therapies, **selenium supplementation (200 µg/day selenomethionine)** has the strongest evidence for reducing thyroid autoantibodies, while **vitamin D repletion** shows benefit primarily in those with baseline deficiency.[5][6][7][8]

---

**Why Perimenopause Triggers Autoimmune Disease**

Approximately **80% of autoimmune disease patients are female**, and the peak incidence of several autoimmune conditions coincides with the menopausal transition.[9][2] The immunological basis involves multiple converging mechanisms:

**Estrogen's concentration-dependent immune effects:** At high physiological levels (pregnancy, mid-cycle), estrogen is predominantly anti-inflammatory — it downregulates Th1 and Th17 cells, promotes Th2 responses, and supports regulatory T cell (Treg) function.[9][10][2] At low levels (postmenopause), this immunomodulatory effect is lost. A study comparing postmenopausal and reproductive-age women found that postmenopausal women had significantly **higher Th1/Th2 ratios and Th17/Treg ratios**, with increased NK cell proportions — a proinflammatory immune profile that favors autoimmunity.[4]

**Estrogen loss and IgG glycosylation:** Estrogen induces the sialylation enzyme St6Gal1 in antibody-producing cells, increasing anti-inflammatory Fc sialylation of IgG. After menopause, this sialylation decreases, rendering circulating IgG more proinflammatory — a mechanism directly linked to increased RA risk in postmenopausal women.[11]

**Immunosenescence:** Menopause accelerates age-related immune changes including decreased naive CD4+ T cells, increased memory T cells, reduced T-helper cell diversity, and chronic low-grade inflammation with elevated IL-1, IL-6, IL-17A, and TNF-α.[12][2][4]

**When to Test Autoimmune Antibodies in Perimenopausal Women**

There are no universal screening guidelines for autoimmune antibodies in asymptomatic perimenopausal women. Testing should be **symptom-driven and targeted** based on clinical suspicion:

**Indications for antibody testing:**

- **New-onset fatigue, weight changes, cold intolerance, hair loss, menstrual irregularity beyond expected perimenopausal patterns** → test TSH, TPOAb, TgAb (Hashimoto's thyroiditis)[27][28]
- **Joint pain, morning stiffness >30 minutes, joint swelling** → RF, anti-CCP (ACPA), ANA, ESR/CRP (rheumatoid arthritis)[29]
- **Dry eyes, dry mouth, dental caries** → ANA, anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La (Sjögren's)
- **Malar rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, serositis** → ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement levels (SLE)
- **Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)** → TSH, TPOAb, adrenal antibodies (21-hydroxylase), vitamin B12, celiac screen (in descending order of clinical relevance)[30]
- **Known autoimmune disease** → screen for coexisting autoimmunity (e.g., celiac disease in HT patients and vice versa; thyroid function in type 1 diabetes)[31]

**Important caveat:** ANA prevalence increases with age and is higher in females — a positive ANA alone does not diagnose autoimmune disease and must be interpreted in clinical context. In a low pre-test probability population, solid-phase assays (ALBIA, FEIA) showed higher specificity than indirect immunofluorescence (IIF).[32]

**Autoimmune Thyroiditis (Hashimoto's): Diagnosis and Management**

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis** is the most common autoimmune disorder and the leading cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions, with a prevalence approximately **4 times higher in women than men**.[22][28] Diagnosis is established by the combination of:[27][34][28]

1. **Clinical features:** painless goiter (firm, bumpy, symmetric) or thyroid atrophy; symptoms of hypothyroidism
2. **Serological markers:** elevated TPOAb (present in ~95% of HT) and/or TgAb (60–80%)
3. **Thyroid function:** subclinical hypothyroidism (↑ TSH, normal fT4) or overt hypothyroidism (↑ TSH, ↓ fT4)
4. **Ultrasound:** diffuse hypoechogenicity, heterogeneous echotexture

**Natural history:** In a 20-year follow-up, clinical or biochemical hypothyroidism developed in **55% of women** who initially had positive thyroid antibodies and elevated TSH (>6 mIU/L) but normal fT4.[27]

**Treatment:** Levothyroxine replacement is the mainstay for overt hypothyroidism, with the goal of normalizing TSH. Treatment of subclinical hypothyroidism with positive antibodies is generally recommended given the high progression rate.[27][35][28]

**Selenium Supplementation in Hashimoto's Thyroiditis**

The thyroid gland has the **highest selenium concentration of any tissue**, and selenium-dependent enzymes (glutathione peroxidases, thioredoxin reductases, iodothyronine deiodinases) are critical for thyroid hormone synthesis and protection against oxidative damage.[36]

**Meta-analytic evidence (multiple systematic reviews, 2013–2025):**

The largest and most recent meta-analysis (35 RCTs, 2,358 participants, GRADE: moderate certainty) found that selenium supplementation significantly reduced **TPOAb** (SMD −0.96; 95% CI −1.36 to −0.56) and **TSH in patients not on thyroid hormone replacement** (SMD −0.21; 95% CI −0.43 to −0.02), with no significant adverse effects compared to placebo.[6]

A 2025 meta-analysis (21 RCTs, 1,610 subjects) confirmed significant TPOAb reduction at both 3 months (SMD −0.46) and 6 months (SMD −0.80), with TSH reduction reaching significance at 6 months. **Selenomethionine was more effective than sodium selenite or selenium yeast.**[5]

A network meta-analysis comparing selenium, vitamin D, myo-inositol, and combinations found that **selenium alone was the only supplement that significantly reduced both TPOAb and TgAb** — vitamin D alone, myo-inositol, and selenium + myo-inositol combinations did not reach significance for antibody reduction over 6 months.[38]

**Practical recommendations for selenium:**

- **Form:** Selenomethionine (most evidence; best bioavailability)[5][6][39]
- **Dose:** 200 µg/day (most commonly studied dose)[6][40][36]
- **Duration:** Minimum **6 months** for meaningful antibody reduction; effects are progressive and more pronounced at 12 months[41][37]
- **Safety:** Adverse effects comparable to placebo in meta-analyses; however, chronic intake >400 µg/day risks selenosis (garlic breath, hair/nail loss, GI symptoms, neurological effects)[39]
- **Caveat:** No major thyroid society (ATA, ETA) has issued a formal recommendation for routine selenium supplementation in HT, though the European Thyroid Association has acknowledged potential benefit in mild Graves' orbitopathy. The evidence supports use as an **adjunctive measure**, not a replacement for levothyroxine when indicated.[6][39]

**Vitamin D3 and Autoimmune Thyroiditis**

**Epidemiological association:** Vitamin D deficiency (25(OH)D [42] A Mendelian randomization study confirmed a **causal association** between higher serum 25(OH)D levels and reduced risk of autoimmune thyroiditis (OR 0.499; 95% CI 0.289–0.860).[43]

**Mechanism:** Vitamin D acts through the vitamin D receptor (VDR) expressed on immune cells to:[8][44][45]

- Promote **Treg cell differentiation and function** while suppressing Th17 differentiation
- Inhibit **B cell proliferation and immunoglobulin production**
- Reduce **proinflammatory cytokine production** (IL-17, IFN-γ, TNF-α)
- Downregulate **MHC class II and costimulatory molecules** on antigen-presenting cells
- In animal models, active vitamin D3 reversed thyroiditis by inhibiting Tfh, Th17, and Th1 cells while promoting Treg and B10 regulatory cells[45]

**Clinical trial evidence (meta-analysis, 12 RCTs, 862 patients):** Vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced TPOAb (SMD −1.084) and TgAb (SMD −0.996), decreased TSH, and increased fT3 and fT4. **Active vitamin D (calcitriol) was more effective than native vitamin D3**, and treatment durations **>12 weeks** produced more significant effects.[7]

**Critical nuance — the "therapeutic window":** A 2025 review found that vitamin D supplementation (2,000–4,000 IU/day) reduces antibodies by **15–30% only in euthyroid TPOAb+ patients with baseline deficiency (30 ng/mL**.[8]

However, the network meta-analysis by Peng et al. found that **vitamin D alone did not significantly reduce TPOAb or TgAb** over 6 months when compared head-to-head with selenium, suggesting vitamin D may be less effective as a standalone intervention.[38]

**Gluten and Autoimmune Thyroiditis**

The relationship between gluten and HT is complex and involves several interconnected pathways:

**Celiac disease–HT comorbidity:** Between **5–7% of patients with autoimmune thyroid disease** are IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG) antibody positive, while **10–30% of celiac disease patients** have thyroid antibodies.[31] This bidirectional association is largely explained by shared HLA-DQ2/DQ8 genetic susceptibility.[31]

**Molecular cross-reactivity:** Anti-tissue transglutaminase 2 (tTG2) antibodies from celiac disease patients **bind to thyroid follicular cells and extracellular matrix** in an identical pattern to monoclonal anti-tTG2 antibodies. This binding was eliminated when sera were depleted of anti-tTG2, and a positive correlation was found between anti-tTG2 and TPOAb titers (r = 0.63, P = 0.0001) — suggesting that anti-tTG2 antibodies may directly contribute to thyroid autoimmunity in celiac disease.[46][47]

**Gluten-free diet (GFD) evidence:**

- In women with HT and positive anti-tTG antibodies (suggesting celiac disease or non-celiac gluten sensitivity), a **6-month GFD reduced TPOAb and TgAb titers** and slightly increased 25(OH)D levels and thyroid secretory capacity (SPINA-GT index).[48]
- A 2026 comprehensive review concluded that evidence supports lifelong GFD in confirmed celiac disease and suggests indirect thyroid benefit in CD-HT comorbidity, but **evidence remains insufficient to recommend routine gluten withdrawal for all HT patients without confirmed celiac disease**.[49]
- A Mendelian randomization study found **no causal protective effect of GFD on autoimmune thyroid disease** at the genetic level, suggesting that any benefits may operate through non-genetic mechanisms (e.g., gut barrier repair, reduced inflammation).[50]
- Interestingly, one pilot study found that GFD **attenuated** the beneficial effects of vitamin D supplementation on thyroid autoimmunity — women on GFD showed less TPOAb reduction with vitamin D than those on a regular diet.[51]

**Practical recommendation:** Screen all HT patients for celiac disease with anti-tTG IgA (and total IgA to exclude IgA deficiency). A GFD is indicated for confirmed celiac disease. For HT patients without celiac disease, routine gluten elimination is not supported by current evidence, though an individualized trial may be considered in patients with GI symptoms or non-celiac gluten sensitivity.[49][31]

---

**Integrated Approach for Perimenopausal Women**

For perimenopausal women at risk for or diagnosed with autoimmune thyroiditis:

1. **Screen thyroid function** (TSH, fT4) and antibodies (TPOAb, TgAb) when symptoms suggest thyroid dysfunction or when other autoimmune conditions are present[27]
2. **Check 25(OH)D levels** — replete to >30 ng/mL with 2,000–4,000 IU/day vitamin D3 if deficient; greatest benefit in euthyroid TPOAb+ patients with baseline deficiency[8][52]
3. **Selenium supplementation** (selenomethionine 200 µg/day for ≥6 months) as adjunctive therapy — the strongest evidence among supplements for reducing thyroid autoantibodies[5][6][38]
4. **Screen for celiac disease** (anti-tTG IgA) in all HT patients; implement GFD only if confirmed[31]
5. **Monitor for polyautoimmunity** — HT patients have increased risk of other autoimmune conditions; periodic screening for adrenal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anemia, and celiac disease is warranted[30][31]
6. **Consider HRT** when indicated — estrogen replacement may modulate autoimmune risk (reduced RA risk with prolonged HRT use; HR 0.2 with ≥8 years of HRT), though effects are disease-specific and complex[24][3]
