This is a broad topic spanning several interrelated domains. Below is a synthesis of the current evidence on testosterone in the context of aging, hypogonadism, obesity, and strength training.

---

**Testosterone Decline With Aging ("Andropause")**

The term "andropause" has largely been replaced by **late-onset hypogonadism (LOH)** or **functional hypogonadism (FH)**, as aging per se does not inevitably cause clinically significant testosterone deficiency.[1][2] Serum testosterone declines approximately **0.4–1.3% per year** after age 35–40, but this decline is modest in healthy, lean men.[3] The European Male Ageing Study (EMAS) defined LOH strictly as the presence of at least three sexual symptoms (decreased libido, morning erections, and erectile dysfunction) with total testosterone [4][2]

Two distinct pathophysiologic patterns emerge with aging:[1]

- **Before age ~65–70:** Predominantly secondary (central) hypogonadism driven by decreased GnRH outflow, often exacerbated by obesity and comorbidities. This form is frequently **reversible** with weight loss and treatment of underlying conditions.
- **After age ~70:** Progressive Leydig cell dysfunction leads to primary hypogonadism (low testosterone with elevated LH), which is generally **irreversible**.

**Obesity and Testosterone: A Bidirectional Relationship**

**Obesity is the single most important modifiable driver of low testosterone in men**, often exceeding the effect of chronological age.[4][6] A Mendelian randomization study confirmed a causal effect of BMI on testosterone: reducing BMI from 30 to 25 kg/m² would be expected to increase serum testosterone by approximately **13%**.[7]

Key mechanisms of the obesity–hypogonadism cycle include:[4][3][8]

- Increased adipocyte aromatase activity converting testosterone to estradiol, which suppresses GnRH/LH via negative feedback
- Hyperinsulinemia-driven suppression of hepatic SHBG, leading to lower total testosterone (though free testosterone may initially be preserved — so-called "pseudo-hypogonadism")
- Proinflammatory adipocytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and leptin resistance further suppressing the HPG axis
- In class 3 obesity (BMI ≥40), genuine suppression of free testosterone occurs with reduced LH pulse amplitude[3][9]

**Weight loss is the most effective intervention** for restoring testosterone in obese men. A meta-analysis found bariatric surgery increases total testosterone by **8.73 nmol/L**, while dietary interventions increase it by **2.87 nmol/L**, with the degree of weight loss being the strongest predictor of testosterone recovery.[10] A recent study of sleeve gastrectomy showed MOSH remission rates of **81–90%** at 6–24 months post-surgery.[11] Guidelines from the Endocrine Society and others recommend addressing obesity as the first-line approach before considering testosterone replacement in men with functional hypogonadism.[1][9][12]

---

**Testosterone Replacement Therapy: Benefits and Safety**

For men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) produces the following effects on body composition:[12][13][14]

- Increases lean body mass by approximately **1.6 kg**
- Decreases fat mass by approximately **1.6 kg**
- Improves maximal voluntary strength and some measures of physical function, though improvements in gait speed and disability measures have been inconsistent[5]

A landmark NEJM study demonstrated that testosterone and estradiol have distinct roles: **testosterone primarily regulates lean mass and muscle strength**, while **estradiol (via aromatization) primarily regulates fat mass**.[15]

Regarding cardiovascular safety, the **TRAVERSE trial** (N=5,246 men with hypogonadism and high cardiovascular risk) demonstrated that TRT was **noninferior to placebo** for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: 7.0% vs. 7.3%; HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.78–1.17).[16] However, a higher incidence of **atrial fibrillation** (3.5% vs. 2.4%), **acute kidney injury** (2.3% vs. 1.5%), and **pulmonary embolism** (0.9% vs. 0.5%) was observed in the testosterone group.[17]

**Impact of Strength Training on Testosterone**

The relationship between resistance exercise and testosterone is nuanced and depends on whether one considers acute versus chronic effects:

**Acute effects:** A single bout of heavy resistance exercise transiently increases serum testosterone in men, particularly with high-volume, metabolically demanding protocols.[18] This acute rise is more pronounced in younger men and with resistance training compared to aerobic exercise, though testosterone typically returns to baseline within hours.[19] The acute response leads to upregulation of the androgen receptor for several hours, potentially amplifying anabolic signaling.[20]

**Chronic (resting) effects:** Meta-analyses have found that exercise training programs (median 12 weeks) have a **negligible effect on resting total testosterone** in insufficiently active, eugonadal men (SMD 0.00, 95% CI −0.20 to 0.20).[21] In older men (≥60 years), resistance training does not significantly raise basal testosterone, though aerobic and interval training may produce small increases.[22] Importantly, resistance training can restore **intramuscular steroidogenic enzyme expression** and local sex steroid hormone levels in older men, even without changing circulating levels.[23]

**Clinical implications:** While resistance training alone does not reliably raise resting serum testosterone, it remains critically important for hypogonadal and obese men because it:

- Promotes muscle hypertrophy and strength independent of hormonal changes[20]
- Contributes to weight loss, which is the most potent stimulus for testosterone recovery in obese men[4][10]
- May enhance local androgen action within muscle tissue[23]
